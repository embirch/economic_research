# Emerging questions log

Programme lead, 1 October 2026. Unranked and deliberately unordered. Each entry records the pattern, contradiction or gap that produced it and what would have to be true for it to become a study. These are observations and leads only: no paper topic is selected here, no brief is commissioned, and the earlier workplace and exposure examples in `RESEARCH-AGENDA.md` carry no priority. Deep dives follow v1.

Claim IDs refer to [`claims-evidence.csv`](claims-evidence.csv).

---

**Q1 — Does a closing extensive-margin gap coincide with a stable or widening intensive-margin gap, within the same instrument?**
From the contradiction between US ever-use parity and the persisting daily-use difference [P04, I01], and the foundational review's finding that gaps extend to duration, prompt volume and persistence. Needs a single source that measures both margins on the same respondents in at least two periods. Pew's repeated items and the DSIT tracker are the obvious candidates; neither has been checked for comparable frequency items across waves.

**Q2 — Is the remaining difference mostly about which tools people use rather than whether they use any?**
From the US tool-mix pattern: ChatGPT identical at 44%, Gemini, Copilot, Grok and Claude all male-skewed, Meta AI slightly female-higher [I02], alongside the coding-agent result of 22% against 9% in a sample at near-parity on broad use [W04]. Would need tool-level items with self-reported gender, which official surveys do not currently carry.

**Q3 — Why do crude and adjusted comparisons point in different directions, and which question does an index indicator answer?**
From Canadian crude parity with an adjusted odds ratio of 1.15 [P06], an EU-wide survey where gender loses significance after controls [W03], Eurostat's own judgement that gender is a weaker predictor than age or education [P02], and the foundational review's contrary finding of within-occupation and within-firm gaps [P08]. This is an estimand question before it is an empirical one. A v1 indicator has to declare whether it reports the unconditional difference or a composition-adjusted one.

**Q4 — How much of the measured difference is composition by age and education rather than gender?**
From the same Eurostat table carrying a 5-point sex difference and a 57-point age difference [P01, P02]. Eurostat crosses sex with age and with education but never with occupation or employment status, so separate margins cannot be assembled into joint cells. The question may be answerable only where permitted microdata exist.

**Q5 — Are women's reported reasons for non-use different from the predictors of their non-use?**
From the tension between Eurostat's single-main-reason data, where privacy and security account for 7% and "no need" for 64% [B01], and UK tracker modelling in which societal-risk concern is among the strongest predictors of women's use [B05]. Requires a source with both a stated-reason item and attitude items on the same respondents, and honesty that neither design identifies a cause.

**Q6 — Does "no need" mean the tools are genuinely less useful for the work and life many women do?**
From the dominance of "no need" among non-users [B01] set against the finding that women are less likely to say chatbots help their productivity [B03] and the ILO observation that women more often perform routine codifiable tasks while abstract analytical tasks are the ones AI complements [E01, E02]. This reframes a familiarity story as a product-fit story and would need task-level data alongside use.

**Q7 — Is augmentation distributed differently by sex than automation?**
From reading the ILO exposure gradients together with the Allas complexity argument [E01, E02, E04]: female-dominated high-exposure occupations cluster in clerical and administrative work, which both sources treat as the automation-prone case, while male-dominated high-exposure occupations cluster in ICT. Needs exposure scores, sex-disaggregated employment and independently measured task characteristics on compatible occupation codes; exposure alone cannot answer it.

**Q8 — Does observed adoption track exposure differently for women and men within the same occupation?**
From Henseke's finding that the gender gap is concentrated in the most exposed occupations [W01]. Blocked until the underlying survey is confirmed and its joint cells are audited; the register's family assignment is currently unverified.

**Q9 — What happens to measured gaps when a third gender category is published?**
From the DSIT table publishing 49.2% on a base of 99 for "identify in another way" and 45.0% on 671 for "prefer not to say" [P03], against Eurostat's binary-only item and Canada's confidentiality aggregation of nonbinary respondents into Male+ and Female+ [P06]. The substantive question — whether non-binary respondents' use resembles either group — is currently unanswerable at adequate precision anywhere in this register.

**Q10 — Do female-higher patterns share a domain signature?**
From emotional support in the US [I05], longer visits on companion-oriented tools [I03], and the female-majority tool categories the foundational review reports for quizzes, language learning and education products. If the signature is care, learning and personal life, then a participation index that weights productivity tools is measuring something narrower than engagement with generative AI.

**Q11 — Do identical published numbers on different denominators get read as a cross-country finding?**
From the EU figures of 35% and 30% among all individuals aged 16–74 [P02] and Brazil's 35% and 30% among internet users aged 10 and over [P05]. This is a presentation question for v1 with a research tail: how much of apparent cross-country variation in gender gaps is denominator and age-range artefact? Testable only where the same instrument runs in several countries.

**Q12 — Can anything be said about who captures the gains?**
From the emptiness of the outcomes strand: no source in this register observes pay, hours, promotion or job security in relation to generative-AI use by sex, while the foundational review's closing extrapolation assumes the mapping from use to output [E06]. The honest finding is the absence. The question is whether any accessible panel links AI use to a later labour-market outcome at all.

**Q13 — Does training change behaviour, and does it change it differently by sex?**
From the widely repeated Danish encouragement and training figures that could not be verified in this session, and from a UK training pilot reported second-hand in the foundational review. Before any study, the primary sources must be read: this register currently holds a mechanism claim with no verified magnitude.

**Q14 — Are platform proxies measuring a different construct, and should they share an explorer page with surveys?**
From near parity among active ChatGPT consumer accounts coexisting with a 44.1% female share of US visits [X01], and from the 20–30% unknown-gender share in the provider study. A methodological question with a direct v1 design consequence: these belong in a separately labelled construct group.

**Q15 — What does the register systematically not see outside Europe and North America?**
From the fact that the only non-European participation cells verified here are Brazilian [P05] and Canadian [P06], while the ILO exposure data cover 84 countries with reference years as early as 2014 [E03]. Coverage asymmetry between exposure evidence and use evidence is itself a finding and should not be presented as a finding about the countries concerned.

**Q16 — Is the "stabilised gap" a property of the world or of the source mix?**
From the foundational review's finding that the relative gap settled near 16% from early 2025 [P07] and that traffic shares have been flat near 40% since October 2024 [I04]. Because the source mix changes over time and the pooled estimand is heterogeneous, stabilisation could reflect composition. Would need the same instruments repeated across periods rather than a changing corpus.

**Q17 — Could a small original study use commercial clickstream data to separate a changing tool mix from change within tools? (candidate for assessment after v1; explicitly to be decided)**
Added on Emily's clarification of 1 October 2026: she finds the foundational report's SimilarWeb analysis interesting and suggests assessing a possible small study on that kind of data. Nothing is commissioned, no purchase or access is authorised, and no novelty is claimed. The pattern that motivates it is that aggregate female traffic share has been flat near 40% since October 2024 [I04] while tool-level shares differ sharply and newer products skew more male [I03, W04] — so a stable aggregate could reflect either stable within-tool composition or offsetting shifts between tools.

Before this could become a brief, the following must be settled in order, and any one of them can stop it:
- **Overlap with the foundational paper.** Cranney, Delecourt and Koning already build worldwide and US top-ten monthly panels from January 2023 to January 2026, country averages for 59 countries, tool-level duration tests and a 11,365-domain breadth check. A decomposition would have to state precisely what it adds beyond their Figures 3–6, Table 1 and Appendix B, and whether they have effectively already answered it.
- **Historical export and schema.** Whether a historical monthly export at the domain–country–month–gender–age level is actually obtainable, with a stable schema and documented revisions, rather than a dashboard view. Entry and exit of products over the window must be handled explicitly; a decomposition is meaningless if the panel composition changes silently.
- **Inferred demographics.** The gender variable is vendor-imputed by an undisclosed method. Its error structure is unknown and may differ by country, age and product, which is exactly the dimension a decomposition would exploit.
- **Visits versus people.** The unit is visits, with no population at risk, so no adoption rate or gap in people can be produced. Shared accounts, repeat visits and the same user across several tools are not separable; a within-tool change cannot be attributed to individual behaviour.
- **Device and app coverage.** Web traffic excludes native apps, API and assistant integrations, and logged-out use, and that exclusion is likely to be correlated with both gender and product.
- **Reuse terms.** Redistribution and publication rights for vendor estimates must be established in writing before any figure could be shown; access permission and redistribution permission are separate.

Recorded as an unranked candidate alongside the others. Assessment belongs after v1, as a scoped feasibility question for the data steward and a novelty check against the foundational report — not as a selected paper.
