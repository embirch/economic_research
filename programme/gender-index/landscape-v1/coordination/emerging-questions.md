## What attracts or discourages women from using AI tools?

Emily, 1 October 2026, explicitly parked this for future exploration: could we plot women's usage against specific news events, scandals, advertisements, offers or other developments, and compare the response with men's? Retain this as a second unranked candidate, potentially connected to the SimilarWeb idea, rather than treating it as an instruction to run a study now.

A descriptive first step could align consistently measured usage with a dated event log. An event-study design would require additional identification work. Specify in advance the events, affected products, countries, plausible response windows and outcomes; select events independently of visible usage spikes. Separate usage levels or intensity from female audience share: the share can fall when men's visits rise even if women's visits do not change. Cross-tool substitution could differ from changes in total AI engagement.

Feasibility depends on sufficient temporal resolution, stable demographic coverage and revisions, reliable event dates and exposure geography, and usable pre-event and post-event observations. Monthly data may obscure short-lived reactions. News coinciding with a product launch, outage, price change, seasonal pattern or advertising campaign complicates attribution. Tool/country comparisons, pre-trends, placebo dates and sensitivity to event windows would be considered only once the actual data are known. Avoid searching many events/windows and reporting only those with large apparent gender differences.

Timing alone cannot establish why people changed behaviour or that the event caused it. Usage traces could be triangulated with surveys, interviews or experiments about trust, usefulness, reputation and affordability, where available and ethically appropriate. The provider's inferred demographics would not identify individuals' motivations. No data purchase, ad experiment, outreach, new paid session or selected paper is authorised by this suggestion.
