# Research contribution, evolving papers and triangulation

Emily's direction, 1 October 2026. This develops the [execution proposal](EXECUTION-PROPOSAL.md). Original empirical research is a required part of the programme alongside synthesis. This note scopes the work; it does not launch another paid session or claim that candidate data have been acquired.

## Three connected contributions

| Contribution | What we produce | How it connects |
|---|---|---|
| Evidence synthesis | A coherent account of existing findings, differences in definitions, coverage and unresolved disagreements | Establishes what is known and identifies questions worth analysing |
| Original empirical research | Deep-dive papers answering defined questions through our own reproducible analysis of suitable datasets | Generates findings that feed the index and may revise earlier interpretations |
| Interpretation and triangulation | A source-linked account of what our results mean alongside academic research, official evidence, qualitative work and informed writers | Tests competing explanations and adds context, including when evidence disagrees |

The explorer is a way to communicate this research. A complete programme cannot consist solely of collected statistics. Conversely, originality does not require a new algorithm or a causal claim: a well-motivated descriptive or associational analysis can make a contribution if its question and added knowledge are established. Benchmark reproduction is quality control; a new visualisation of the same finding is not by itself a new paper.

If workplace or outcome data fail, alternatives already in the register include within-source patterns of platform activity or motivations. Name-associated message categories cannot become measures of women's participation, and recalled motives cannot become observed first-use behaviour. Later selection must preserve the requirement for substantive original research rather than quietly falling back to compilation alone.

Each selected paper needs a short brief recording:

1. The unanswered question and the closest existing work; what a null or contrary finding would add.
2. Exact permitted files, joint variables, population, source sex/gender measurement and feasible comparisons.
3. Our proposed estimand and analytical work, separated from benchmark replication and literature synthesis.
4. Prior data inspection, uncertainty, alternative explanations, robustness checks and the interpretation allowed by the design.
5. Code, derived results, figures, an analysis log and a claims/evidence record, with independent reproduction of key results.
6. Which reviewed findings could enter the index or trigger a revision to an earlier paper.

## The first paper remains a living research output

“Authoritative” identifies the manuscript to continue; it does not make its interpretation permanent. Emily explicitly welcomes new nuance, references, insights and justified analytical revisions. Preserve the live author edits and editorial voice while incorporating changes deliberately in that manuscript. Do not restart the comparison with the alternative Claude version.

Keep each raw source snapshot and previously released result reproducible. Record a new vintage as a new version rather than replacing old inputs. An index update does not automatically regenerate paper text. A revision should link the new evidence, identify affected claims/figures, show the proposed changes against the current author copy, and rerun only affected analysis/checks. Cite a new source where it informs discussion; change a numerical result only with a documented calculation. Material changes in question or geography should be stated explicitly rather than hidden in a refresh. ## Interpretation as research work

Maintain a claims-to-evidence table in each paper brief or review. Record the interpretation, supporting and contrary evidence, underlying study/sample, design and population, what our data can test, and what remains unobserved. A writer and the study they discuss are one evidence lineage, not two independent confirmations. Agreement across incompatible estimands is not replication; disagreement can reveal different populations, timing or mechanisms.

Use informed writing to sharpen questions and challenge readings of our results. Trace numerical claims to original research where possible, retain thoughtful qualitative insight with its attribution, and actively seek evidence against a preferred explanation. A synthesis should explain which findings align, where they differ and why, rather than collecting quotations that favour one narrative.

### Initial reading: exposure and opportunity

[Tera Allas, “AI and jobs: when exposure is a strength”](https://teraallas.substack.com/p/ai-and-jobs-when-exposure-is-a-strength), 16 April 2026, read 1 October. Allas argues that task complexity and continuing human contributions can make exposed work amenable to augmentation; productivity and demand may support employment and pay. Her UK occupational examples motivate investigation, not a causal estimate of AI's effects or a gender-specific benefit. The linked task-bundling paper was not readable through the accessed Dropbox preview, so that supporting analysis remains unverified.

Two related primary-source records were checked on 1 October:

- [ILO Working Paper 140](https://www.ilo.org/publications/generative-ai-and-jobs-refined-global-index-occupational-exposure): the publisher abstract describes task-based exposure and distinguishes potential transformation from actual outcomes. Same source family as the existing ILO exposure/gender records; this check is of the abstract, not a reproduction of its model or country data.
- [OpenAI's AI Jobs Transition Framework overview](https://openai.com/index/modeling-ai-jobs-transition/): technical capability, human involvement and demand are considered separately. Treat this as a provider-authored framework to examine, not validated forecasts. The linked full PDF exceeded the browser fetch size limit; methods/data were not audited.

These readings do not establish a direction of gender effects. Our proposed interpretation checks are: distinguish technical exposure, actual deployment, changes to tasks, productivity, and worker outcomes; examine who captures any gains and who bears adjustment costs; consider pay, security, hours, autonomy and access to training where evidence supports them. Higher exposure, higher use and higher productivity each need separate interpretation. Do not assign a universally favourable or unfavourable sign to them in an index.
