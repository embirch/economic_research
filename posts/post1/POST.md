# Is AI delegated more on low-wage work or on high-wage work?

*September 2026 · Evidence from Claude*

## Half of Claude.ai use is delegation, and no report has asked what that work pays

Ask Claude to translate a contract clause and the exchange is over in a turn: the request goes in, the translation comes back. Spend an afternoon with it on a grant proposal, paragraph by paragraph, and something else has happened. In the first conversation the person **hands the task over**. In the second they **work with the model**: asking to understand something, revising a draft together, bringing finished work to be checked. Anthropic's classifier separates those two shapes on every conversation it can label, and calls them automation and augmentation. This post calls them **delegation** and **collaboration**, which is what they are; where Anthropic's own terms are needed, they mean the same numbers.

About half of the conversations people bring to Claude.ai are the first kind. The [Anthropic Economic Index](https://www.anthropic.com/news/the-anthropic-economic-index) has published that split in every report since its first, in February 2025, and has watched it move: delegation rose sharply through the summer of 2025, then eased back as more of what people brought to Claude became learning and iteration. The [January 2026 report](https://www.anthropic.com/research/anthropic-economic-index-january-2026-report) reads the reversal as a correction rather than a turn, with the underlying trend still toward delegation and "the August spike" having "overstated how quickly it was materializing" ([p. 9](https://www-cdn.anthropic.com/096d94c1a91c6480806d8f24b2344c7e2a4bc666.pdf)).

Economists have a name for why the split matters. Anthropic's own [first paper](https://assets.anthropic.com/m/2e23255f1e84ca97/original/Economic_Tasks_AI_Paper.pdf) gives it in one sentence: "A key distinction in the economics literature is between automation—where technology substitutes for human labor—and augmentation—where technology complements and enhances human capabilities" (p. 9). A technology that substitutes for a person's work and a technology that complements it have opposite consequences for whether that person is employed and what they are paid. Nobody can observe substitution directly. The delegation share is the closest thing the Index has to a signal of which one is happening, task by task, and the [September 2025 report](https://www.anthropic.com/research/anthropic-economic-index-september-2025-report) calls the question behind it "perhaps the most important question that we hope our data will help answer" ([p. 47](https://assets.anthropic.com/m/218c82b858610fac/original/Economic-Index.pdf)).

Anthropic leaves the reading of its own number open, on purpose, and states both branches. If more capable models simply expand the set of tasks that can be handed over, then "the risk increases that workers performing such tasks will be displaced". If the rise instead reflects learning-by-doing — people discovering they can trust the model with work they still supervise — then "workers most able to adapt to new AI-powered workflows are likely to see greater demand and higher wages" (September 2025, [p. 10](https://assets.anthropic.com/m/218c82b858610fac/original/Economic-Index.pdf)). The same measurement supports a story about displacement and a story about gains to adaptable workers, and which one it supports depends on whose work is being delegated.

The number is also already a parameter in two of Anthropic's own models of the economic future. Its [labour-market exposure measure](https://www.anthropic.com/research/labor-market-impacts) counts a task's observed use at full weight when the use is delegated and at half weight when it is collaborative — "fully automated implementations receive full weight, while augmentative use receives half weight" ([p. 6](https://cdn.sanity.io/files/4zrzovbb/website/2b5bbaf2c1eb81dbf6e6fb813c1a24e35a64d376.pdf)) — so a job's exposure score rises with the delegation share of the work it is made of. Its [economic-scenario model](https://www.anthropic.com/institute/econ-scenarios) carries a delegation share as one of its scenario primitives, held constant in each scenario, anchored on the Index at about a half for chat use today and set as high as nine tenths for a world in which agentic use is the norm ([pp. 27–28](https://www-cdn.anthropic.com/files/4zrzovbb/website/cf58f84d46a4a76bf5a5b039ac695fba6b80041c.pdf)). In that model the split decides who collects the gain from a task: "The gain on an automated instance is available only if capital performs it. The gain on an augmented instance is available only if a worker performs it" (p. 11).

The Index has learned a good deal about **who** delegates more. Four findings are on the record, and each is a comparison between groups of users rather than between kinds of work:

1. Businesses running Claude through the API delegate far more than people on Claude.ai — "three-quarters of interactions classified as automation compared to less than half on Claude.ai" (January 2026, [p. 26](https://www-cdn.anthropic.com/096d94c1a91c6480806d8f24b2344c7e2a4bc666.pdf)).
2. Countries with less Claude use per person delegate more than countries with more, after allowing for the different work they bring: "As Claude usage per capita increases, countries shift from automation-focused to augmentation-focused usage" (September 2025, p. 26).
3. Newer users delegate more than long-standing ones; users who signed up at least six months before the data were pulled are "much less likely to delegate greater responsibility through directive use patterns" ([March 2026](https://www.anthropic.com/research/economic-index-march-2026-report), Table 2.1, [p. 15](https://cdn.sanity.io/files/4zrzovbb/website/4053bf3440c0c85b8852052770c5b4cf882689c3.pdf)).
4. The education a task's prompt demands makes no difference at all: "the automation share is essentially unrelated to the human levels of education required to write the prompt" (January 2026, p. 40).

What no report has asked is what the delegated work pays. The price of the work itself is published: since March 2026 each task has been priced by "the average hourly wage of US workers who perform that task", with the series plotted back to the first report (p. 8). The same price then sorts other things: the share of conversations served by the most capable model rises with it (March 2026, p. 14), and so do the tokens a conversation consumes ([June 2026](https://www.anthropic.com/research/economic-index-june-2026-report), [p. 13](https://cdn.sanity.io/files/4zrzovbb/website/9e0eadc8097864886c5d5060ebb1f89b02ea29d6.pdf)). The price of the work and the way the work was done ship in the same file, for the same week, at the same task grain. They have never been put in the same table.

One case makes plain what the missing cross decides. A week in which people hand over clerical work and collaborate on clinical judgement, and a week in which they hand over the clinical judgement and collaborate on the clerical work, produce the same published delegation share, because that share counts conversations and gives every task the same weight. The exposure measure and the scenario models do not treat those two weeks alike: both weight delegated work more heavily, and both price work by what it pays. So if delegation sits at one end of the pay scale, reading an unweighted share as though it were weighted by wages carries an error, and the direction of that error is not in print.

So a plain question has no published answer. When people hand work over to Claude rather than work through it with them, is it more often the low-paid work or the high-paid work? The answer matters to two readers, and this post keeps returning to both.

1. **For anyone thinking about jobs**, it is the difference between two stories. In one, the routine, low-paid work goes to AI first and the well-paid work is protected by its complexity. In the other, the well-paid work is handed over first, because it is the work AI is best at. Anthropic's reports raise the second: "If AI disproportionately substitutes for tasks requiring less expertise while complementing higher-skilled work, it could be another form of skill-biased technical change—increasing demand for highly skilled workers while displacing lower skilled workers" (January 2026, p. 21). Which story the data favour decides who is thought to be at risk first.
2. **For anyone using the Index's numbers**, it is a question of measurement. If delegation is concentrated at one end of the pay scale, then the unweighted share Anthropic publishes is the wrong input for a measure that weights delegated work by what it pays, and the correction has a sign.

This post crosses the two for the first time, in three separate weeks of Claude.ai conversations between August 2025 and February 2026. Three things follow, stated here without numbers so that a reader who stops here still has the answer.

1. The best-paid quarter of the work brought to Claude is delegated a little more often than the worst-paid quarter, in every one of the three weeks — though the size of the difference is not the same in any two of them.
2. That difference is entirely a matter of what kind of work sits at the top of the pay scale. It is software. Set the coding family aside and the best-paid work is delegated *less* than the worst-paid.
3. Weighting the published delegation share by the wage of the work barely moves it.

The answer is two-sided, which is why the title is a question.

Two limits belong before the first table, because a reader will think of them first.

1. **This is Claude.ai, not the economy.** The work people bring to a chat assistant leans heavily toward writing, coding and analysis, and Anthropic bounds its own data the same way: "Claude is used for high-value, complex work that is not broadly representative of the US economy" (March 2026, p. 19).
2. **The unit is a task, not a person.** No one's occupation or salary is observed. The wage attached to a conversation is the wage of the American occupations whose work the request most resembles, so "low-wage work" and "high-wage work" here mean the bottom and top quarter of the work brought to Claude, ranked by what that work pays in the United States — not the bottom and top of anyone's payroll.

## What each possible answer would have looked like in the data

The measured quantity is one number per week: the delegation share of the best-paid quarter of the work minus the delegation share of the worst-paid quarter, in percentage points, with each task counted by the conversations it received. Call it **the gap**. A gap of one percentage point was fixed in advance as the smallest difference worth calling a relation with pay, and each possible answer was written down before the data were run, with the pattern that only it would produce.

| If the truth were this | The pattern only it would leave | What the three weeks showed |
|---|---|---|
| The more the work pays, the more it is handed over | the gap positive and more than 1 percentage point from zero in all three weeks, and still of that sign and at least half that size whenever the kind of work is held fixed | the gap is more than a point from zero in two weeks of three, and the second half of the signature fails in every week |
| The opposite: the best-paid work is the work people stay involved in | the gap negative and more than a point from zero in all three weeks | not found — the gap is positive in all three |
| A difference whose direction holds but whose size the design cannot pin down | all three gaps of one sign and none of them zero, once the first two answers have failed | **this is the answer the rule recorded** |
| Pay makes no difference worth the name | all three gaps within a point of zero, and not all of one sign | not found |
| No steady difference at all | anything else | not found |
| It is the kind of work, not the pay | a difference that loses its sign, or more than half its size, when the coding family is set aside or the occupational group is held fixed, in every week in which that test can be run | **this too, on both tests, in every week** |

The rule takes the first five rows in the order they are printed and stops at the first whose pattern the data match; it stopped at the third. The last row is not a rival value of the gap but a rival explanation of one, so it is asked only once a difference has been recorded — and it is the row that decides how everything else reads. It was given three tests, each re-estimating the same gap. The first sets aside every task belonging to the Computer & Mathematical family of occupations and leaves the quarter boundaries where they were. The second compares like with like: management tasks against management tasks, clerical against clerical, across the twenty-two broad groups of the American occupational classification, then averages. The third keeps only the tasks that people mostly bring to Claude as work rather than as personal or coursework requests, to see whether the difference is really between working and non-working use. Two further cuts — dropping one occupational group at a time, and the mix of work, personal and coursework requests by quarter — were registered as description rather than as tests, and are reported beside them.

## In all three weeks the best-paid quarter of the work on Claude.ai was delegated a little more than the worst-paid, and only the direction is steady

Two definitions first, because every number below depends on them.

**Delegated** means what Anthropic's classifier calls automation: the person either hands the whole task over — "Human delegates complete task execution to AI with minimal interaction" ([February 2025 paper](https://assets.anthropic.com/m/2e23255f1e84ca97/original/Economic_Tasks_AI_Paper.pdf), Appendix F.3, p. 25) — or hands it over and steers it with feedback. Its counterpart, **collaborated**, covers asking to learn, iterating on a draft together, and asking for a check on one's own work. A task's **delegation share** is its delegated conversations divided by all five of those patterns together; conversations that fit none of the five sit outside that denominator and are reported beside every quarter below. Two of Anthropic's own cautions travel with the measure. Delegation is not autonomy: a one-line translation request is fully delegated and asks almost nothing of the model, which is "high automation (directive, minimal back-and-forth) but low AI autonomy" (January 2026, p. 19). And the shape of one conversation does not show what the person did afterwards, since "users might edit and adjust the response they receive from Claude outside the chat window" (February 2025 paper, p. 9).

**The wage of the work** is the median hourly wage of the American occupations that do a task, averaged over those occupations by how many people they employ. Tasks are ranked by that wage and cut into four quarters of equal conversation volume, the convention Anthropic's own wage-quartile exhibits use — "Wage quartiles are calculated using BLS data, weighted by number of transcripts" (June 2026, Figure 1.3, p. 7). So "the best-paid quarter" holds a quarter of the week's conversations, not a quarter of its tasks. The exhibits and the sentences that carry numbers call those quarters quartiles; they are the same thing.

The delegation share is not a smooth line in pay. In every week it falls from the worst-paid quarter to the second and rises again to the best-paid: 53.1, 47.0, 49.8 and 54.4% in August 2025; 46.6, 41.3, 46.0 and 54.0% in November 2025; 48.8, 41.6, 43.5 and 49.5% in February 2026. Printed beside each quarter, so that the base is never implicit, the share of conversations fitting none of the five patterns runs 3.6, 1.9, 1.9 and 1.2% in August; 3.1, 1.1, 1.9 and 1.2% in November; 3.4, 1.1, 2.2 and 1.1% in February. The middle of the pay scale, where most of the writing and analysis sits, is where people collaborate most; the two ends are where they delegate.

The contrast between the two ends is the finding. **In each of the three Claude.ai weeks the delegation share of conversations on top-wage-quartile tasks exceeded that of conversations on bottom-quartile tasks: by 1.4 points (4–11 August 2025), 7.4 points (13–20 November 2025) and 0.7 points (5–12 February 2026).** The intervals around those numbers treat every conversation as an independent draw with the task mix held fixed, at two-sided 95%; no release carries a user, session or account identifier, so nothing can be clustered on and the intervals are a lower bound on the sampling variance. On the corrected rule for drawing the quarters, which shares the boundary wage between the two adjacent quarters instead of letting sort order decide, the differences are 0.5, 7.2 and 0.3 points, and August no longer exceeds one point. The size of the difference is not stable across weeks: it varies from two thirds of a point to more than seven points, a spread twenty-six times the standard error inside a week, so those intervals describe conversation sampling within a week and say nothing about the variation between weeks. Each week is estimated on its own and never pooled, so a change in who was using Claude between one week and the next sits inside that test of persistence rather than being controlled for. And a task's rate is a blend over every country that sent conversations on it, which cannot be cleaned at this grain; the one geography that can be bounded, Seychelles in November, moves the difference by up to three points and does not account for that week's excess.

The rule set in advance reads those three intervals against the table's rows in turn. The difference exceeds one percentage point in two of the three weeks and not in the third; the pre-registered rule therefore records a persistent sign and does not record a gradient above the one-point margin in every week. Dropping the 23 November tasks in which Seychelles sent more than a tenth of the world's conversations turns that week's difference to −4.6 points, while netting Seychelles out of the task weights alone moves it to +6.5; the rates themselves cannot be cleaned. Those 23 tasks include the two "modify existing software to correct errors…" tasks, which by themselves carry 8.5 of the top quartile's 22.5 points of conversations in November (6.5 in August, 6.0 in February), so the reversal is not Seychelles' doing. Counting every Seychelles conversation on a top-quartile task as delegation and every one on a bottom-quartile task as collaboration, with the weights netted, still leaves November's difference at +4.5 points. In the other direction, dropping tasks with fewer than 100 classified conversations raises the differences to 2.6, 9.1 and 1.6 points.

Three cautions belong beside these numbers, because each changes how they should be read.

**(i) Where the quarters are cut decides August.** The boundary between the third and the fourth quarter falls at $43.40 an hour in every week — the wage the file assigns to Computer Occupations, All Other — and the 99 / 106 / 111 tasks priced at exactly that wage carry 8 to 11 percentage points of a week's conversations, so the top quarter's membership is settled partly inside the coding family. Which of those tasks land in the top quarter is decided by sort order, and the order-free rule takes August below a point.

**(ii) The top quarter rests on very few tasks.** A count that discounts a list of tasks for being dominated by a few large ones — the Kish effective number of tasks — gives 94 / 83 / 126 for the whole analysis set and 10.6 / 8.9 / 16.4 for the top quarter, where a handful of software tasks carry most of the conversations.

**(iii) What the best-paid quarter is made of decides how all of this reads.** Its tasks are overwhelmingly software, and setting that family aside reverses the sign in every week. That is the next section, and it comes before any reading of the numbers above.

The slope taken over the whole wage range has no steady sign either: −0.05 points per $10 in August, against a smallest detectable slope of 0.11 and not distinguishable from zero; +1.77 in November; −0.28 in February. A relation that is positive at the extremes, negative in the middle and sign-switching in the slope is not a gradient in pay. It is a contrast between two ends of a distribution, and Figure 2 is the distribution it is drawn from.

## The whole of the excess is software: set that family aside and the best-paid quarter on Claude.ai is delegated less than the worst-paid

The composition rule was registered because the coding family is most of the best-paid quarter of the work in this data, and because it is the work Anthropic's own gloss of automative use names — "asking the model to directly complete a task or debug errors" ([March 2025](https://www.anthropic.com/research/anthropic-economic-index-insights-from-claude-sonnet-3-7)). The excess is carried by Computer & Mathematical tasks, which hold 73, 71 and 65% of the top quartile's Claude.ai conversations in the three weeks against 43, 40 and 36% of the analysis set's.

Remove them and the sign goes with them. **With Computer & Mathematical tasks excluded and the quartile boundaries kept where they were, the top quartile's delegation share was 13.0, 10.5 and 11.9 points *below* the bottom quartile's.** That is a comparison of task types and not of pay: what is left of the best-paid quarter is a small set of managerial, scientific, engineering and clinical tasks carrying 6.1, 6.5 and 7.8 percentage points of a week's conversations, set against clerical, educational and sales tasks at the bottom. Dropping one occupational group at a time is the same evidence seen from the other side rather than a second test — removing any single major group other than Computer & Mathematical moves the difference by at most 3.2, 2.3 and 2.9 points; removing Computer & Mathematical moves it by 14.4, 17.9 and 12.6.

The second test holds the kind of work fixed instead of deleting it. Comparing within occupational groups — averaging the top-minus-bottom difference over the ten (August, November) or nine (February) groups that hold tasks in both extreme quarters, weighted by their conversations there — the difference was −4.2, −1.9 and −6.2 points; those groups carry 80, 80 and 76% of the two quarters' conversations, and the other twelve or thirteen groups hold tasks at only one end and cannot be compared. A blunter cut agrees: removing the ten largest tasks by conversation volume, a fifth to a quarter of all named conversations and mostly software, turns the difference to −11.6, −13.1 and −11.4 points. And inside the coding family alone there is no pay relation to speak of: the difference within it is −0.1 points in August on an interval containing zero, +6.5 in November and +0.8 in February, on a bottom quarter of 24, 23 and 14 tasks.

The pre-registered composition rule therefore fires: on both the coding-excluded and the within-group test the difference loses its sign in every week, and the relation between delegation on Claude.ai and the wage of the work reads as a mix that travels with wage — coding — rather than as a property of the wage. The pay mechanism is not refuted by this. It is unidentified: the wage attached to a task is the wage of the occupation the task resembles, so the design cannot separate what the work pays from what the work is, at any grain.

The third test is the one that did not fire, and it settles nothing. A test that does not fire is nothing shown rather than a rival excluded, and this one is not independent of the first: the work-dominant top quarter is the coding top quarter under another name, since 81 to 83% of the top quartile's conversations are kept against 20 to 30% of the bottom quartile's.

The mix it is drawn from is stark. The work share of the bottom quartile's conversations is 33% in November and 29% in February, against 61% in the top quartile, and the bottom quartile is 45 to 56% personal requests. Low-wage work on Claude.ai is, for the most part, not low-wage labour. It is people asking for help with their own lives. For the jobs story that matters as much as the coding result: the bottom of this pay scale is not a workforce.

One further reading is recorded because it bears on the word "delegated" itself. It was labelled exploratory in advance and sits in no decision rule: the fully directive conversations alone are *less* common on top-quartile tasks in every week, by 7.0, 9.5 and 9.9 points, while feedback-loop conversations are more common, by 8.4, 16.9 and 10.6 points — so the positive difference in the delegation share is a feedback-loop difference. The best-paid work is handed over and steered, not handed over and forgotten. Both that reversal outside coding and the lower directive share at the top are consistent with the reading Anthropic offered for its own turn counts, that in conversations mapped to higher-wage occupations users take more turns, "more labor-augmenting than labor-displacing" (June 2026, pp. 13–14) — consistent with, not confirmation of it — and the U-shape of Figure 2 echoes the one published cross of the collaboration facet with occupational category, which was not monotone in wage either.

## Weighting the published delegation share by the wage of the work moves it by less than a point, and not in one direction

The measurement question can now be answered directly. **Weighting the published delegation share by the hourly wage of the work would move it by −0.02 points in August, on an interval containing zero, by +0.84 points in November and by −0.15 points in February — below one point in every week, and in the same direction as the quartile difference in one week of three.** It is a correction to an hourly rate and not to a bill: the re-weighting uses the price of an hour of the work, no hours enter it, and the wage is the wage of the occupation a task resembles. The one-point line it is measured against was set for this quantity in the post's own design brief, separately from the one-point margin the quartile difference is judged on. August's null is a tight one: the smallest movement the design would have detected there is 0.05 points, so a weighting error as large as a twentieth of a point would have shown and did not. In levels: the delegation shares of the tasks in the analysis are 51.1, 47.0 and 45.9% unweighted and 51.1, 47.8 and 45.7% weighted by the wage of the work.

The null value is zero, which is what this quantity is when the delegation share is uncorrelated with the wage across the task mix. The materiality line of one percentage point is the design brief's own and is separate from the one-point margin applied to the quartile difference.

The correction is small in every week and indistinguishable from zero in one, and it cannot be applied in a known direction, because it depends on all four quarters and the trough of the U sits in the middle.

The reason it is small is the reason the headline is two-sided. Pay does not order delegation across the task mix as a whole; one family of tasks does, and that family happens to be well paid. Add each task's work share to the wage regression and the wage slope moves from +1.78 to −0.06 points per $10 in November, and from −0.28 to −1.63 in February, with the two predictors far from interchangeable when it happens (a variance inflation factor of 1.13 to 1.14). The wage and the work share compete for the same variation, and the wage loses.

Two further probes were labelled exploratory in advance, so they are context and not findings, and neither is a replication.

1. On the first-party API, where businesses run Claude and delegation dominates, the difference is negative in every week: −2.4, −2.5 and −5.2 points. The February API sample includes Claude Code.
2. Against Job Zone, the O\*NET ordering of work by how much preparation it requires, the delegation share falls by 6.7, 6.0 and 6.8 points per zone.

## What this means: it is the kind of work, not the pay

On Claude.ai, in these three weeks, the wage of the work is not what orders whether people delegate or collaborate. The kind of work is. Software is both the best-paid work people bring to Claude and the work they most readily hand over, and that coincidence is what a wage-sorted table picks up. Compare like with like and the best-paid work is delegated slightly less than the worst-paid. Neither statement is a statement about pay: the design prices a task by the occupation it resembles, so it cannot separate what the work pays from what the work is, and the sentence "delegation depends on the wage of the work" is withdrawn in both directions.

**For the jobs story**, this changes which story to tell. The evidence does not support the picture in which well-paid work is handed to AI first because AI is good at it, and it does not support the picture in which routine low-paid work goes first. It supports a narrower and more useful claim: the work being delegated is a particular kind of work, and its pay is incidental. That has a consequence for how Anthropic's own findings on delegation should be read. Wherever a report has found delegation concentrated — in businesses using the API, in countries with less usage per person, among newer users — the same question is now worth asking of each: is it the pay of the work, or is it that those users bring more software? None of the three is settled here. What this post settles is which variable to test them against: the share of software in what those users bring, rather than the pay of it. The bottom of this pay scale also turns out not to be a workforce at all: most of what arrives there is people asking for help with their own lives, which is a caution for anyone reading a low-wage delegation rate as a statement about low-wage workers.

**For the reader who takes the Index's numbers into a model**, the answer is more direct. A published delegation share can be read as a wage-blind average without much error, because its wage-weighted version is close to it in every week and not consistently above or below, and the correction is to an hourly rate with no hours in it. What such a reader should condition on instead is the share of software in the task mix, which is the one composition that moves the number and the one an unweighted share hides. A measure that weights delegated work by what it pays is not wrong to do so. But in this data the quantity it is really tracking is coding: the one composition that moves the wage difference is the share of software, so a delegation share taken from these weeks carries the coding mix with it whether or not the model says so. The direction is also not corroborated on the second wage source in any week, because the Employment Projections table does not price the computer occupations; its rebuild is the coding exclusion again.

The obvious objection is that three weeks of one product cannot carry a claim about work, and the instability of the size is the warning. The difference was many times larger in one week than in another, across three weeks whose sampling error is a small fraction of either, so something other than sampling moved it and this design cannot say what. Anthropic's own reports name candidates: the influx of newer users, who bring lower-paid work, and the drift of coding toward agentic tools, which would thin the top of the pay scale. Read as a claim about tasks in general rather than about these weeks' conversations, a design that resamples tasks resolves nothing below about fourteen to twenty percentage points. The honest generalisation is the direction and the mechanism, never the number.

What would change the reading is specific and nameable. A later week in which the difference keeps its sign under every rule for drawing the quarters and for pricing tasks that several occupations share, and after the largest tasks are dropped, would make the direction more than a sign. A wage source that prices the computer occupations would make the check against a second source a real one. And a week in which the work-versus-personal mix is published alongside the collaboration patterns would let the third test be run where it has never been run.

Until then the answer to the title is its own two halves. On Claude.ai, high-wage work is delegated somewhat more often than low-wage work, and the whole of that excess is coding; take the coding family out and high-wage work is delegated less. Whichever half a reader carries away, what these weeks measure is the kind of work. The wage is only how it was sorted.

## Recommendations to Anthropic

**Publish the collaboration facet crossed with the wage quartile, and with the occupational group, in the release itself.** One extra pair of intersections in each release bundle would do it: the five collaboration patterns by wage quartile, and by major occupational group, at the grain the facets already ship in. What calls for it is that the cross changes the reading rather than decorating it: the top-minus-bottom difference is +1.4, +7.4 and +0.7 points as the facets are published and −4.2, −1.9 and −6.2 points once the occupational group is held fixed, and no reader of the release can get from the first three numbers to the second three. The stake is large because the delegation share is the Index's closest signal of whether AI substitutes for work or complements it, and a signal whose sign turns over with one grouping variable should not reach a model of the economy without that variable attached to it. It would not settle the question where it stays open: the intersections are published at global grain only, so the cross would still blend countries inside a task.

**Report the exposure measure's automation weight twice, once with Computer & Mathematical tasks included and once with them excluded.** The published exposure number would then come as a pair rather than a point, with one line of text saying which family the difference between them belongs to. What calls for it is the leave-one-group-out series: removing any single occupational group other than Computer & Mathematical moves the wage difference by at most 3.2, 2.3 and 2.9 points, while removing that one group moves it by 14.4, 17.9 and 12.6. Observed exposure is the anchor for how much of the economy's work AI is treated as affecting in Anthropic's own scenario models, so a single wage-blind weight lets one occupational family set the exposure of a whole workforce without the reader seeing that it did. It would not help inside that family, where the exclusion leaves nothing to compare, and it is not a substitute for a within-occupation contrast.

**State which average a published delegation share is, whenever one is quoted as a model input, and print the wage-weighted figure beside it.** Two numbers and a clause, wherever the share is currently given as one: the conversation-weighted share, the wage-weighted share, and the note that no hours enter either. What calls for it is that the two differ by −0.02, +0.84 and −0.15 points in these three weeks — small, but not of one sign, and not knowable in advance without the cross. The gap between a wage-blind and a wage-weighted delegation share is exactly the gap between the number the Index publishes and the number a model of labour's share of income wants, so publishing both retires a guess that is currently made silently. It would not close the gap for a model that weights tasks by labour payments: the correction measured here re-weights an hourly rate, and no count of hours enters the cut this post uses.

## Limitations

The design observes how work was actually done, at task resolution, on more than eight hundred thousand classified conversations a week. Its costs follow from the same choice, in the order a referee would reach for them.

1. **Composition, not pay — and this withdraws a claim.** The best-paid quarter is two thirds to three quarters Computer & Mathematical by conversation volume, and the difference reverses without it, within occupational groups, and when the ten largest tasks are dropped. The wage attached to a task is the wage of the occupation the task resembles, so the design cannot separate the wage of the work from the kind of work at any grain. The post is therefore not entitled to the sentence "delegation depends on the wage of the work", in either direction, and does not write it. Direction of the bias: unsigned — the confound could as easily be masking a pay relation as manufacturing one.

2. **The country mix inside a task.** A task's global rate is a blend over the countries that sent conversations on it, and the intersections are published at global grain only, so the blend cannot be cleaned and a task's rate cannot be rebuilt from the country files. Anthropic's own country regression reports lower-usage countries delegating more; if top-quartile tasks are drawn relatively more from high-usage countries — which the global grain cannot confirm — that would push the top quartile's share *down*, so the likelier direction of this bias is against the reported sign. One case can be bounded: Seychelles in November, worth up to three points on the difference, not enough to account for that week's excess.

3. **The size is unstable between weeks.** Two thirds of a point in one week, more than seven in another, against intervals inside a week of about three tenths of a point. Those intervals are the wrong uncertainty for any statement that spans weeks; the pre-registered power calculations assume a constant true difference, which these three estimates refute, and no probability of the observed outcome under a varying difference has been computed or is quoted. Every interval here is also a lower bound, because no release carries a user, session or account identifier to cluster on.

4. **The top quarter is small, and the conclusion flips at known thresholds.** Its Kish effective number of tasks is 10.6, 8.9 and 16.4; the two largest software-modification tasks are a quarter to two fifths of its conversations; and the third-quartile boundary sits on a single computer occupation's wage, so sort order places 5 to 7 percentage points of the top quarter's conversations. Two flips are already on the table: the order-free quartile rule takes August below one point, and pricing a task at its single largest holder's wage takes February below zero.

5. **The second wage source is not a second wage source.** The Employment Projections table is keyed on a later occupational vintage and prices about a tenth of the coding family's conversations, so its rebuild of the difference is the Computer & Mathematical exclusion again rather than an independent test. No independent wage source has confirmed the sign, and none is available while the shipped O\*NET file carries 2010 occupation codes.

6. **The working-versus-personal rival is under-tested.** It can be tested in two weeks, sits exactly on the halfway line in one of them, and cannot be tested in August at all. The composition rule's power against a test that loses three quarters of the difference is low in this range, so a test that does not fire is nothing shown: the use-case mix has not been ruled out, and the work-dominant restriction is not independent of the coding exclusion.

7. **The construct.** "Delegated" is directive plus feedback loop, and in the exploratory split the two components move in opposite directions across the pay scale, so the positive difference is a feedback-loop difference rather than a difference in complete hand-off. Delegation is not autonomy and is not displaced labour, and the wage-weighting correction re-weights an hourly rate with no hours in it.

8. **The wage level is not Anthropic's.** The conversation-weighted wage level built here does not reproduce Anthropic's published task-value series on matched weeks, which is why the design is rank-based, why the headline is a quartile contrast rather than a dollar slope, and why no dollar level in this post should be read as Anthropic's.

9. **The generalisation bound.** Treated as a statement about tasks rather than about these weeks' conversations, the design resolves nothing below about 14 to 20 percentage points — twice the largest difference reported here and twenty to thirty times the smallest.

## Methodology

**Data.** Three Anthropic Economic Index releases, one week each: `release_2025_09_15` (4–11 August 2025, Claude AI Free and Pro), `release_2026_01_15` (13–20 November 2025) and `release_2026_03_24` (5–12 February 2026, Claude AI Free, Pro and Max). The cut is the `onet_task::collaboration` intersection at `geography = global`, with the same wave's `onet_task_pct` as the conversation weight. Tasks are priced through the shipped O\*NET 20.1 task statements to `release_2025_02_10/wage_data.csv`, joined on the full ten-character O\*NET-SOC code, with `MedianSalary` filtered above 100 and divided by 2,080 for an hourly rate and averaged over holding occupations by employment. The BLS Employment Projections table is the second wage source, used for rank and sign and never for a level. Analysis set: the 1,802 / 2,075 / 2,188 named global tasks with a wage and at least one classified collaboration cell — 97.15 / 96.23 / 96.52% of the conversations mapped to a named task, on 818,673 / 854,432 / 848,716 classified conversations, Kish effective 94 / 83 / 126 tasks; 805 / 1,079 / 1,056 tasks with a wage and no classified cell and 5 / 12 / 12 with a cell and no wage are dropped, never zeroed. The wage is attached on the shipped 2010 O\*NET-SOC codes, the only vintage the wage file joins to; any occupational grouping is taken on the 2019 recode with the 2010 grouping reported beside it.

**Measures, as the brief fixed them.** The outcome is Anthropic's own column: "**automation_pct**: Percentage of classifiable collaboration that is automation-focused (directive, feedback loop patterns)" (`release_2025_09_15/data_documentation.md`), built on the classifier's own definition, "Directive – Human delegates complete task execution to AI with minimal interaction" (Economic Index paper, February 2025, Appendix F.3, p. 25). It is not a count of tasks automated and it is not autonomy — "high automation (directive, minimal back-and-forth) but low AI autonomy" (January 2026, p. 19) — and Anthropic's bounds on what one dialogue's shape can mean travel with it: "users might edit and adjust the response they receive from Claude outside the chat window"; "even automation of simple tasks can serve to enhance human capabilities when embedded within broader human-directed workflows" (February 2025 paper, p. 9). The price of a task is Anthropic's construct too — "the average hourly wage of US workers who perform that task" and, where several occupations do it, "we average their wages weighting by employment and the fraction of time spent on that task" (March 2026, p. 8 and footnote 5, p. 11) — and the quartile convention is Anthropic's: "Wage quartiles are calculated using BLS data, weighted by number of transcripts" (June 2026, Figure 1.3 caption, p. 7). The design is rank-based because the wage *level* does not reproduce; this post reports a quartile contrast and a re-weighting, not a dollar level.

**Models.** The difference and the composition legs are estimated with the task mix held fixed, on a conversation-level binomial; intervals are two-sided 95% and are lower bounds under within-task dependence, since no clustering unit exists in any release. A design-based variance that resamples tasks is reported beside them as the generalisation bound. The wage-weighting correction is the conversation-weighted covariance of wage and delegation share divided by the mean wage. The slope is a conversation-weighted regression of the per-task share on the hourly wage, reported for sign and significance only.

**Stress tests.** Twenty-four robustness entries and sixteen descriptive entries, all pre-registered: two alternative rules for pricing tasks that several occupations hold, two alternative quartile allocations, a minimum-conversation floor, two Seychelles cuts, a design-based variance model, a permutation placebo, the second wage source, a fourth task-level week on the unchanged taxonomy, a leave-one-group-out series over all twenty-two major groups, and a ten-largest-tasks leave-out. Every confirmatory quantity is produced twice by two independent code paths; the largest disagreement in the headline difference between them is 1.8 × 10⁻¹⁴ points, the bootstrap standard errors are within 0.9% of the closed form, and their intervals cover 95.2–95.3%.

**Reproduction of the published numbers first.** Each wave's own five-pattern delegation share reproduces at 51.07 / 46.74 / 45.55% against the published 49 / 45 / 44% on the all-conversation base, and Anthropic's released country regression returns its published figure exactly (−3.112, partial R² 0.394, N 111).

## What was set in advance

The pre-registration is `posts/post1/prereg/prereg.md`, committed at `066b761` before any primary test: before the quartile difference, before the wage-weighting correction, before any quarter's delegation share, before the continuous slope, before any composition leg, before any robustness re-estimate and before any exploratory test. One script had been run at that point and it loads no data — the power calculation on the standard errors the feasibility work had already published. Nobody had computed or seen the difference, any quartile's delegation share, any leg, or the permutation null. What had been seen, and is listed in the pre-registration, was the shape of the quarters without their outcome: the boundaries, the coding family's share of the top quarter, the use-case mix by quarter, the Kish effective counts and the Seychelles figures.

The confirmatory set was named and counted in advance: **17 confirmatory estimates** — the difference, the wage-weighting correction and the slope in three weeks each, the coding-excluded and within-group legs in three each, the work-dominant leg in two — **of which 8 are the leg tests**, plus **3 exploratory tests** labelled as such. Seventeen and three are what was run; nothing else was run as a test. The six outcomes of the ordered rule are the rows of the table above; their internal names, and the order in which the rule reads them, are in the pre-registration.

The assumed effect and its provenance: a smallest effect of interest of one percentage point on the difference, chosen so that the rule could fail, with a realised minimum detectable effect of 0.40 / 0.39 / 0.39 points per week at 80% power. The separate materiality line for the wage-weighting correction — 0.11 to 0.12 points of correction per point of quartile difference — came from the referee's arithmetic on the brief and is reported here as an assumption, not as a realised relation: the correction depends on all four quarters, and the arithmetic holds in one week of three.

Three deviations were logged, each with the registered rule and the corrected rule both run and both reported.

- **The quartile rule at the $43.40 wage mass point.** The registered sentence does not say which of the tasks sharing the boundary wage fall in the top quartile; the registered rule was made reproducible by fixing the tie order on (wage, task text), an order-free fractional rule was run beside it, and both declare the same outcome.
- **The two-percent agreement tolerance on the retained fraction.** A ratio with a denominator of two thirds of a point is heavy-tailed and no implementation could meet it; twenty-five percent is asserted for the ratio, and the half-retention judgement is read from the linear contrast the pre-registration already required beside every leg, which does meet two percent.
- **The permutation placebo's size band**, widened from plus or minus one to plus or minus two percentage points, with the Monte Carlo error printed beside the estimate; the realised size falls inside the original band, so the widening did no work. The placebo is in no decision rule.

Three places where the pre-registration completed a rule the brief left open — the persistence of the composition rule, the identified set for the within-group leg, and the declared-power figure for the null — are marked in the document, with the literal reading reported beside the completed one.
