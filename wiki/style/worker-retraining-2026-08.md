# worker-retraining-2026-08 — style annotation

## Source

- **Title, five ways, and two of them disagree.** The PDF cover reads "An evidence review of worker retraining" (H1, under the Anthropic wordmark); the PDF's own document metadata `Title` field is the same string; the arXiv listing's `citation_title` is the same string. The web page's H1 is different — "Reviewing the evidence on worker retraining programs" — and the browser/OG/Twitter title is a question that appears nowhere in the paper: "How well do job retraining programs work?". The page's BibTeX block names the paper a fourth thing, `title = {An Evidence Review of Subsidized Job Training}`, where the PDF's own BibTeX block on printed p. 85 says `title = {An evidence review of worker retraining}`. Per `room/director-2026-09-16-pdf-web-ruling.md` the PDF is the document of record and both versions of the disagreeing citation are recorded here side by side; neither is treated as governing. The practical lesson is narrower than the ruling: **a citation block is a quotable object and drifts like any other text; the same piece cannot carry two BibTeX titles.**
- **Date:** "August 12, 2026" on the cover, under a "Published" label in a two-column block with "Authors"; "Aug 12, 2026" on the web page under the H1; arXiv `citation_date` 2026/09/07, submitted 7 Sep 2026 — so the arXiv version is a later deposit of an August paper, and the corpus slug's date is the publication date, not the deposit.
- **Primary URLs fetched:** the PDF, https://www-cdn.anthropic.com/169e4adceebbab066b91e0b45127d73cb2bda24b/WorkerRetraining.pdf (124 pages, 5.0 MB), as the document of record; the page, https://www.anthropic.com/research/reviewing-the-evidence-on-worker-retraining-programs; the arXiv abstract page, https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.07011, for its metadata and abstract only.
- **Page numbering.** The cover is unnumbered and printed page 1 is the first page of the Executive summary, so **printed page = PDF page − 1** throughout. Every page reference in this file is to the *printed* number.
- **Document type.** A commissioned evidence review — the corpus's only one. The suggested citation on printed p. 85 calls it "The Anthropic Institute, Working Paper 2026-01"; the page calls it "a review of the evidence on worker retraining programs"; the register is an economist's review essay, not a series report and not a blog post. It carries the full apparatus of a monograph: 124 pages, roughly 45,700 words including references, nine numbered sections, seven appendices (A–G), 15 numbered figures plus one appendix figure (B1), six numbered tables plus Table A.1 and Tables G1–G2 and one unnumbered table in Appendix F, footnotes numbered continuously from 1 to 73 straight through the appendices, a 16-page reference list, and a public code-and-data repository and interactive results interface linked on the cover.
- **A hidden line on the cover.** The text layer of the cover page contains one string that does not appear in the rendered page: "Anthropic Economic Index report: Cadences". It sits at the foot of the cover and is invisible when the page is drawn — presumably a leftover from the template the cover was built from. Recorded, not adjudicated: **the corpus notes what a document's text layer says as well as what its page shows,** because the text layer is what a machine reader quotes.
- **Authors, and why the register is different because of them.** "David Roodman, Independent" and "Maxim Massenkoff, Anthropic", in that order, on the cover. The lead author is external to Anthropic and is named as such on the cover; nothing beyond that is inferred here, but the consequences of an outside-led review run through the whole piece:
  1. **The paper reviews other people's work and says what it thinks of it.** "But while the paper's title contains the word 'sensitivity,' the text does not precisely define the term." "Despite much investigation, Orr et al. (1994, note 33) finds no convincing explanation for the latter." "It arrives unflinchingly at disappointing conclusions." No Economic Index report evaluates a third party this directly, because none of them has a literature to evaluate.
  2. **Judgment is claimed as the method, not apologised for.** "Our primary approach is to make intensive use of our judgment to think critically about the findings and claims in individual studies and the portrait they paint."
  3. **Econometrics is glossed in plain words at the point of use** — ITT and LATE with a worked arithmetic example, attrition, Ashenfelter's dip, fixed versus random effects, funnel plots, publication bias — so a policy reader can follow, while the footnotes and appendices carry the machinery for an economist. The gloss runs toward *method*, the inverse of the April 2025 software report, which glossed the *subject matter*.
  4. **Acknowledgements are a 30-name list on the cover**, including named evaluators at MDRC and the programs themselves, and three conversations are cited in footnotes as sources ("Conversation with Tamara Johnson and Sang Lee, Per Scholas, April 28, 2026"). **An interview used as evidence gets a dated footnote like any other source.**
  5. Massenkoff is the recurring in-house co-author (also on `coding-agents-social-sciences-2026-05`), and the shared habit is visible: a framing question the evidence cannot fully answer, stated plainly as such.
- **Audience.** Policy readers, funders and economists at once, addressed as people who may have to decide something. The paper says so: "We hope to help calibrate people's expectations for a large scale-up of retraining programs in the event of rapid and disruptive AI diffusion, and to identify the most pressing areas for research."
- **Share-card metadata.** `description`, `og:description` and `twitter:description` are all the same nine words — "An evidence review from Anthropic's Economic Research team" — and carry no number and no finding. The OG image is a generated illustration whose `og:image:alt` is "Large hand-shaped network diagram with abacus-like nodes and interconnected beads representing data processing". So the share card states provenance only, in contrast with the April 2025 report's share card, which carried a result.
- **AI in the question, Claude in the method, and nowhere else.** "AI" is the phenomenon throughout — "labor market disruption from AI", "an AI-disrupted future", "AI exposure index". **"Claude" appears exactly five times in 124 pages, and every one of them is the research instrument**: "Claude is used to extract traits and impacts"; "in which training is a primary feature according to Claude's reading of reports"; "We deploy Claude Opus—with engaged oversight—to extract the needed information on each experiment from the thousands of pages of reports"; "we instruct Claude to: a) produce a 'reasoning table'…"; "while having Claude assess whether job training was a primary feature in each program". This is the cleanest instance in the corpus of the split our house rule states — the question says AI, the measurement says Claude — and it is worth noting that here Claude is the *instrument of the literature search*, not of the data, which is a use our own posts will want.

## Section order

Headings in order, with what each does and roughly how long it runs (printed pages).

1. **Cover (unnumbered).** Wordmark, title, "Published" / "Authors" columns, a 30-name Acknowledgements paragraph, and two links: "Code and data for this report are at github.com/droodman/job-training-meta-analysis. A web interface onto many more meta-analytical results is at droodman.github.io/job-training-meta-analysis." **Reproduction is on the cover, above the abstract-equivalent, not in a back-matter note.**
2. **Executive summary** (pp. 1–2, ~500 words). Two framing paragraphs, then "Overall, we find:" and five bullets with bold lead-in sentences; the fourth bullet carries three italic-lead sub-bullets. This is a summary block, and unlike the coding-agents Summary it is written *against* its own headline (see `## Opening move`).
3. **Overview** (pp. 3–9, ~2,600 words). Unnumbered heading, but its subsections are numbered **1.1–1.7** — so the report has a section 1 that never prints its own number. Four framing paragraphs, a five-bullet statement of what the review did, then the seven numbered findings, each headed by a full declarative sentence, then a roadmap paragraph. **Figure 1 sits inside this section**, on p. 9, before any of the body evidence.
4. **2 Background** (pp. 10–11). A nine-item numbered taxonomy of interventions under three sub-heads, then the scope decision: "We will generally limit our review of job training programs to items 3–6." **Scope is stated as a range over an enumerated list, so a reader can see exactly what was excluded.**
5. **3 Critical themes** (pp. 11–15). Five subsections — randomization, imperfect compliance, attrition, academic vs non-academic publication, "Success is ephemeral". This is the limitations apparatus, placed *before* the evidence and written as instructions for reading it (see `## Limitations`).
6. **4 Major American randomized evaluations** (pp. 16–35). Four studies in historical order — National Supported Work Demonstration, JTPA, Job Corps, WIA Gold Standard — each with design, uptake differential, figures, and a critical reading; then **4.5 a named summary subsection**.
7. **5 "Judge randomization" studies** (pp. 36–46). Two studies, each headed with its full citation and paper title.
8. **6 Meta-analyses** (pp. 47–64). Four prior meta-analyses reviewed one per subsection, each headed with citation and paper title (6.1–6.4); then **6.5, the paper's own new meta-analysis**, in five numbered parts: the sample, average impacts, benefit and cost, correlates of impact, and a summary.
9. **7 Zeroing in on sector programs** (pp. 64–78). History in seven numbered episodes (7.1.1–7.1.7); 7.2 "Some takes on the history"; 7.3 a critical re-examination of Katz et al. (2022)'s sample; 7.4 summary.
10. **8 From past evidence to an AI-disrupted future** (pp. 79–83). Scope limitation, an eight-bullet "The major findings:", the framing of the AI question through Manning and Aguirre (2026) and Figure 15, then **four recommendations as declarative sentence headings, 8.1–8.4**.
11. **9 Discussion** (pp. 83–84, ~450 words). Five short paragraphs: the historical frame, the uncertainty, what the report did, what it found, what to do.
12. **Back matter.** Suggested citation and BibTeX (p. 85); References (pp. 86–101); Appendix A "Other themes" (six sub-themes including the history of US workforce legislation and Ashenfelter's dip), B "Additional figures" (one figure), C "Experiments in Europe", D "Discontinuity-based studies", E "Conceptual preliminaries for meta-analysis", F "Meta-analysis with persistence term", G "Benefit-cost analysis" (pp. 102–123).

**Where the findings sit: five depths, and each one is shorter than the last.** The Executive summary bullets; the seven numbered declarative headings of §1; the per-section summaries (4.5, 6.5.5, 7.4) that close each body of evidence; the eight-bullet "The major findings:" list at the head of §8; and two sentences in §9. A reader can stop after any one of them and have a defensible version of the paper. The device our template can take directly is the **named summary subsection at the end of each evidence block** — "4.5 Major American randomized evaluations: summary", "6.5.5 Randomized job training experiments in the US: summary", "7.4 Sector programs: Summary" — which is how a long piece stays navigable without a summary block at the top.

**Headings that are sentences.** Both §1 and §8 use them, and in both cases the heading is the claim:

- "1.1 In the US, average impacts are positive but not transformative."
- "1.2 Monetary benefits exceed costs on average, under reasonable but debatable assumptions."
- "1.3 The impacts of large-scale US programs are as small."
- "1.4 Average effects are higher per trainee than per person offered training."
- "1.5 The evidence on programs to help dislocated workers—whose unemployment or low pay is more attributable to large-scale economic changes such as deindustrialization—is sparser and mixed."
- "1.6 Some 'sector programs' stand out, with large and sustained benefits."
- "1.7 The sector approach can probably help society respond to AI's disruption of work. But the approach is not formulaic and will probably fail if treated as such."
- "8.2 Short-duration programs are ill-matched to the needs of more-skilled workers."

Three things to copy. **The qualifier is inside the heading**: "positive but not transformative", "under reasonable but debatable assumptions", "probably … But the approach is not formulaic". **A heading may be two sentences** when the second one is the limit on the first (1.7). And **the unit of the claim is in the heading** where it is the finding (1.4, "per trainee" against "per person offered training"). Compare `coding-agents-social-sciences-2026-05`, where the "but" clause in a heading was the corpus's most compressed caveat; here the same device runs over seven consecutive headings, and one of them (8.2) is a heading that argues *against* the paper's own subject.

**Where the method sits.** Distributed by function: §3 for how to read the literature (before the literature); the §6.5 preamble for how the sample was built, how Claude extracted it and how missing standard errors were imputed; Appendix E for the meta-analytic concepts; Appendix G for the benefit-cost model; Appendix F for the persistence robustness. **The method that the reader needs in order to disbelieve the paper is at the front; the method that the reader needs in order to rebuild it is at the back.**

**Where the limitations sit.** There is no Limitations section. Four locations, annotated in full under `## Limitations`.

**How the page compresses the paper, and how the arXiv abstract compresses it differently.** The web page is an announcement of about 400 words in five paragraphs: what it is and who wrote it; the policy question; the lineage (four in-text links — the Economic Index, the labour-market framework, the Economic Policy Framework, the Economic Futures Research Fund); the numbers; the sector-programme result and its replication problem; the recommendation, with the Fund named as the way to act on it. It has no figures, no captions, no footnotes, and one citation block. Three compressions of one result, all published by the same institution, differ:

| | employment | earnings | cost | fiscal recovery | sector programmes |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| PDF exec. summary | "increased employment by 1.7 percentage points, compared to a baseline employment rate of 63% in the control group" | "$800 per year on average" | "roughly $13,000 per person" | "about three-quarters of their costs" (§1.2) | "boost earnings 10 times as much" |
| Web page | "employment rises by two to three percentage points" | "roughly $1,000 a year" | "about $13,000" | "the government recovers more than half of what it spends" | "produce gains several times larger" |
| arXiv abstract | "employment rises 1.7 percentage points in years 3-5" | "roughly \$800 per person offered training" | "\$13,598 per participant" | "about three-quarters of their cost" | "raise earnings roughly ten times as much" |

Four observations, all writing observations. **The page silently switches time horizon**: the PDF's headline pair is the long-term (years 3–5) estimate, and the page's is the medium-term (year 2) one, without saying so — both are in Table 2, neither is wrong, and a reader who quotes the page and the summary together has two different numbers for one finding. **The page rounds a qualification downward**: "more than half" for a figure the paper gives as "some 76%". **The arXiv abstract breaks the paper's own denominator rule**: it says "\$13,598 per participant" where the paper is emphatic — "at a cost of $13,598 per person offered treatment (in 2025 dollars; not per person trained)" — and the parenthesis exists precisely to forbid the abstract's phrasing. **The page's vaguest number is the paper's largest claim**: "several times larger" against "10 times as much". The last of these is the only one that errs toward caution. The lesson for us is the one the corpus keeps producing in new forms: **one entry per number, quoted identically in every place the institution publishes it** — including the page, the abstract and the citation block, which are written last and checked least.

## Opening move

**The Executive summary, first two paragraphs and the first bullet, verbatim:**

> Among experts and the public, worker retraining is the most popular policy for mitigating labor market disruption from AI.[1] In this report, we ask if these efforts would work. We present a targeted review of the evidence on job training and a brief history of key programs.

> We screened the literature for high-quality randomized trials, drawing on 146 impact estimates from 56 randomized trials conducted in the United States from 1973 to the present. The programs trained job seekers—mostly low-income adults and youth—for positions such as nursing aides, IT support technicians, and welders. On average, they run six months and cost roughly $13,000 per person.

> ● **Job training programs have small impacts**. Across randomized trials in the US, programs on average increased employment by 1.7 percentage points, compared to a baseline employment rate of 63% in the control group. They increase earnings by $800 per year on average. These figures are per person offered *treatment*, which matters because compliance in these studies is often low. The programs pass a cost-benefit test, but this relies on uncertain assumptions about how long the benefits last.

Footnote 1, in full: "Karger et al. (2026), Figure 16."

**The Overview, first three paragraphs and the fourth, verbatim:**

> Americans' top concern about AI is job loss (Anthropic 2026). If current data on AI usage are any guide, college-educated workers—software developers, paralegals, accountants—are most at risk (Massenkoff and McCrory 2026). But this is highly uncertain. Fast advances in robotics and autonomous vehicles would affect different jobs. And the employment of less-educated white-collar workers, like those in data entry or customer service, might fall sooner (Richmond 2026).

> In the event of widespread labor market disruption, displaced workers, regardless of their occupation, would need to quickly find jobs outside of their experience and training. An ambitious government, acting swiftly as in the COVID pandemic, would likely consider expanding retraining programs. Indeed, in a recent survey of economists, AI experts, and superforecasters, "Retraining support" was the most favored policy response to AI-driven job loss, beating out enhanced unemployment insurance, a jobs guarantee, and universal basic income (Karger et al. 2026).

> This report takes this possibility seriously by reviewing what we know about how well training has worked in the past. We hope to help calibrate people's expectations for a large scale-up of retraining programs in the event of rapid and disruptive AI diffusion, and to identify the most pressing areas for research.

> The body of evidence and experience is of course an imperfect match for an AI-disrupted future. If layoffs mainly affect white-collar workers, the new raft of long-term unemployed would be the most educated and highly paid in history. […] The evidence gathered here is about training programs that last weeks or months and mostly target low-income people on the margins of the job market. […] Still, we see durable lessons in the programs we study here—and the bulk of retraining efforts may yet concentrate on workers in exposed lower-wage roles.

**And the method-scope disclosure, the paragraph that follows, verbatim in its opening:**

> This project is not purely a systematic review, nor more specifically a meta-analysis. It was not structured from the start to mechanically filter and synthesize findings—though we do ultimately conduct a meta-analysis. Our primary approach is to make intensive use of our judgment to think critically about the findings and claims in individual studies and the portrait they paint.

**Annotation.**

- **Why it matters is one sentence, and it is about what other people believe, not about what the authors found.** "Among experts and the public, worker retraining is the most popular policy for mitigating labor market disruption from AI." The stake is that a popular policy may not work — which makes the paper's value independent of the sign of its results. **When the motivation is a decision other people are about to make, a null is as publishable as a positive**, and the opening should be written so that it is. This is the most transferable opening in the corpus for a post whose findings may be small.
- **The question is asked in seven words and it is a yes/no.** "In this report, we ask if these efforts would work." No construct, no hedging, no preview of the answer. The next sentence bounds the answer's scope — "a targeted review of the evidence on job training and a brief history of key programs" — with "targeted" doing the work of a scope caveat in one adjective.
- **The evidence base is described before any result, in one sentence with four facts.** "146 impact estimates from 56 randomized trials conducted in the United States from 1973 to the present" — count of estimates, count of studies, country, and vintage span. Then the population ("mostly low-income adults and youth"), the occupations trained for, the duration and the cost. **A reader knows what kind of programme is being talked about before knowing whether it works**, and the three occupations ("nursing aides, IT support technicians, and welders") do more for that than any category label would. Copy the move: **name three concrete instances of the unit of analysis in the paragraph that describes the sample.**
- **The headline number arrives with its baseline attached, in the same sentence.** "increased employment by 1.7 percentage points, compared to a baseline employment rate of 63% in the control group." The corpus has been repeating this rule against pieces that break it (`coding-agents-social-sciences-2026-05` published two headline ratios with neither of their rates); here it is kept, and kept in the *summary*, which is where it is hardest.
- **The denominator caveat is a full sentence inside the headline bullet, and it says why it matters.** "These figures are per person offered *treatment*, which matters because compliance in these studies is often low." Not a footnote, not a methods note: the sentence after the number, with the word "treatment" italicised so the contrast with "trained" is visible. This single sentence is the paper's most repeated move — the offer/trainee distinction is restated at least seven times across the report, in the Executive summary, §1.1, §1.4, §6.5.1, §6.5.2, §6.5.3 and Appendix G. **A construct that a reader will get wrong is restated at every point of use, not defined once.**
- **The first bullet ends by conceding the second finding's weakness.** "The programs pass a cost-benefit test, but this relies on uncertain assumptions about how long the benefits last." The strongest positive claim in the paper arrives already qualified by the assumption that carries it, and Appendix G later names the threshold at which the claim fails. **A summary-level finding carries the assumption that would overturn it in the same sentence** — the same rule the coding-agents Summary satisfied once and broke three times.
- **The Overview opens on the reader's world and states the uncertainty in its third sentence.** "Americans' top concern about AI is job loss… If current data on AI usage are any guide, college-educated workers… are most at risk. **But this is highly uncertain.**" Then two rival scenarios with citations. The paper's subject is a contingency, and the opening refuses to assert the contingency. Compare the February 2025 report's lineage opening and the coding-agents literature opening: this is a third kind, **the decision-under-uncertainty opening**, and it is the right one when the post's finding is about what to do if something happens.
- **The mismatch between the evidence and the question is conceded before the findings, at length, and then answered.** The fourth paragraph is entirely a concession — the trials studied low-income people in short programmes, and an AI shock might displace the highly paid — and it ends with the two reasons the paper proceeds anyway ("durable lessons"; "the bulk of retraining efforts may yet concentrate on workers in exposed lower-wage roles"). It is repeated at the head of §8. **The external-validity problem in a post like this is not a limitation, it is the opening**, because a reader who has not been told it will think of it before the first finding.
- **The anti-pre-registration disclosure is voluntary, first-person and early.** "It was not structured from the start to mechanically filter and synthesize findings." For our template's "what was set in advance" section this is the model of honesty and also the exact thing our pre-registration exists to avoid having to say. Note what makes it acceptable here: the paper immediately tells the reader what it did instead ("intensive use of our judgment"), then makes the judgment auditable — five bullets of procedure ("Impose a high evidentiary standard", "Aggressively search for studies meeting this standard", "Synopsize the US experiments … with a new meta-analysis", "Individually review the most important national studies", "Engage in more depth with the research on 'sector programs'"), a footnote describing what "aggressive" searching meant in practice (the Library of Congress, retired authors, the 2005 Digest of Social Experiments), and a public repository. **A judgment-based method is disclosed as a judgment-based method and then made checkable; it is never described as if it were a protocol.**
- **How soon the first number appears.** In the Executive summary, the second paragraph: 146 and 56, sample sizes, before any result; the first result is 1.7 percentage points in the first bullet. In the Overview the first number is the money the policy already costs — "In 2017, the US government spent $14 billion per year on job search assistance, counseling, and worker retraining, and reached an estimated 10.7 million people (GAO 2019, Figure 4)" — which sizes the decision before sizing the effect. **A number that establishes why the question is worth asking outranks a number that answers it.**
- **The roadmap paragraph, and why it earns its place in a 124-page document.** "The next section of this report provides background information. Section 3 then delineates some cross-cutting critical themes. Section 4 gives special attention to a historical sequence of four major American randomized evaluations of job training… Section 8 concludes with thoughts on how to extrapolate from this evidence base to a future in which the world of work is disrupted by AI." One sentence per section, in order, each naming the section's job rather than its conclusion. Our posts are far shorter and do not need one; the device to take is the *sentence shape* — section, verb, object — for the rare case where a post has more than five findings.

## Findings and their caveats

The paper has one grammar for a quantitative finding and it holds for 124 pages: **the estimate, its unit, its baseline, the population it is per, and the timeframe — then the qualification, in the next clause or the next sentence.** What changes across the report is only which of those five is at risk.

### The average impact, and the sentence that separates significance from size

Verbatim, §6.5.2 (printed p. 58):

> If "work" means to increase employment and earnings on average, and to a statistically significant degree, then job training has worked in the US. In Table 2, 11 of the 12 mean impact estimates are 2–4 times their standard errors, making them highly significant. (All are ITTs, expressed per person offered treatment.) The exception is for the short-term impact of training-primary programs on earnings, which may reflect temporary earnings losses while in training.

> However, the average training program does not make a big difference. From a base employment rate of about 60%, it boosts the chance of having a job by 2.6-2.8 points in the medium term (year 2) and 1.7–1.8 points in the longer term (the pairs of numbers being for all programs and training-primary ones). The average program lifts pre-tax earnings by 7.8% in the medium term, meaning by $1,000–1,100/year from $12,500 in the full sample and $14,700 in the training-primary sample. The long-term earnings impacts are 4.6%, i.e., $700–800 from bases of $14,400 and $16,100 (all figures in 2025 dollars).

**Annotation.**

- **The verdict is conditional on a definition the sentence supplies.** "If 'work' means to increase employment and earnings on average, and to a statistically significant degree, then job training has worked in the US." The criterion is stated, then met, then — in the next paragraph — declared insufficient: "However, the average training program does not make a big difference." **Two paragraphs, two questions: is the effect real, and is it big. Never the same paragraph.** This is the single most copyable passage in the file, and it is the correct answer to the fault the corpus keeps finding, where a significant estimate is reported in language that implies a large one.
- **Every number carries four things.** Base ("From a base employment rate of about 60%"), unit ("points", "$/year", "%"), timeframe ("medium term (year 2)", "the longer term"), and which sample each end of the range belongs to ("the pairs of numbers being for all programs and training-primary ones"). The percentage and the level are both given, in that order, with the level's base named: "lifts pre-tax earnings by 7.8% in the medium term, meaning by $1,000–1,100/year from $12,500 in the full sample and $14,700 in the training-primary sample". **A ratio is published with both of its levels** — the rule the corpus derived from a paper that broke it, kept here without exception.
- **The exception to a general claim is named in the sentence that makes the claim, with its likely cause.** "11 of the 12 mean impact estimates are 2–4 times their standard errors… The exception is for the short-term impact of training-primary programs on earnings, which may reflect temporary earnings losses while in training." Counted, located, explained, hedged ("may").
- **Ranges rather than point estimates are used wherever two specifications disagree**, and the two specifications are named each time. The paper never picks one; it prints "2.6-2.8", "1.7–1.8", "$1,000–1,100", "$700–800" and says which end is which. **Where a finding is robust to a specification choice, publishing the range is shorter than publishing the sensitivity analysis.**

### The small-impact finding and the compliance explanation

> One cause of the low impacts on labor market outcomes—but also a source of hope in some scenarios—is the low impact on participation in training. In training-primary interventions, the experimental offer of entry is accepted 75% of the time, while only 9% of the control group manages to access the treatment. Dividing that difference, 66%, into the ITTs quoted just above produces what we call "LATE-program" estimates 50% larger. Similarly, the impact on participation in any training program is only 28%, because many control group members find alternative trainings. If these substitutes are equally effective, then the impacts of training per se—the values of "LATE-training"—are 1/0.28 ≈ 4 times the ITTs reported in the lower rows of Table 2.

**Annotation.** The mechanism is quantified rather than asserted: the small effect is explained by a number (the 66-point and 28-point participation differentials) that is itself a finding, and the arithmetic that converts one into the other is shown in the sentence ("1/0.28 ≈ 4"). The scaling is fenced twice — by a conditional ("If these substitutes are equally effective") and by a name that flags its own construct ("LATE-training"). In §1.4 the same result is fenced a third time: "We find that this estimate, likely an upper bound, is about 2–4 times higher than our main estimates, although it does not change the benefit-cost picture." **An adjustment that makes the finding look better is published with the assumption it needs, the direction of its error, and a statement of what it does not change.** The last clause is the discipline: a favourable re-expression is immediately told what it may not be used for.

### The nulls

> An evaluation of the Workforce Investment Act, the successor to the JTPA, returned essentially zeros for the programs for low-income adults and for dislocated workers (Fortson et al. 2017). Estimates were as often negative as positive and lacked statistical significance. One challenge was a low uptake differential: many people offered training did not take it, while many in the control group found training anyway. As a result, the treatment-control difference in participation was only 15 percentage points.

> The Job Corps is an intensive residential program for young people that was enacted as part of the 1960s "war on poverty." It did not affect employment or earnings in follow-ups extending 20 years, except for a transitory 1–2 percentage point employment bump in years 3–5 (Schochet 2021).

> Unfortunately, the strong signal from Figure 5—essentially no impact overall—looks like the main verdict from the Job Corps evaluation.

> A major reason may have been lack of statistical power, as many people offered training didn't take it, and many not offered found it elsewhere.

**Annotation.**

- **A null is stated in the indicative and then given its reason.** "returned essentially zeros"; "It did not affect employment or earnings in follow-ups extending 20 years". Not "we find no evidence" — the paper is willing to say a programme did not work, because the follow-up is 20 years long and it says so in the same sentence. **The length of the follow-up is what licenses the stronger verb**, and it travels inside the claim.
- **The exception inside a null is kept, with its transience marked.** "except for a transitory 1–2 percentage point employment bump in years 3–5".
- **Low power is named as the competing explanation and quantified by its cause, not by an MDE.** "the treatment-control difference in participation was only 15 percentage points". This is as close as the paper comes to a minimum detectable effect, and it is not one: nowhere does the report state what effect size any of these studies could have ruled out. **Naming the mechanism that destroyed the power is better than nothing and worse than an MDE**; our template requires the MDE beside every null, and this is the corpus's clearest illustration of what is missing without it.
- **A null is refused the excuse it could have taken.** "But low uptake is not a failure mode we want to abstract from" (§4.5). The paper declines to treat its most convenient explanation as exculpatory, because in the real world the uptake problem is part of the programme. **When a design flaw is also a feature of the thing being studied, say so and keep the finding.**

### Findings driven by a few studies

> Other statistically strong results are associated with small numbers of programs. The sole program with long-term results that expressly targeted people with drug problems or past involvement with the law—the National Supported Work Demonstration—entirely drives the impressive impacts on the "justice-involved." The two programs that served people with intellectual disabilities boosted employment about 8 points in the long term. And the apparently powerful impact of an employer committing to hire graduates—$8,000–9,000 in year 2 and beyond—owes to three programs: the Wildcat program that inspired the NSWD, Year Up, and the Wisconsin Regional Training Partnership.

> We also find suggestive evidence that some programs that bundle other features alongside training perform well. Trainings backed by a hiring commitment from employers increase the earnings of admitted students an order of magnitude more than the average program, although this is driven by just three programs.

**Annotation.** The most striking coefficient in the paper — a hiring commitment worth $8,000–9,000 a year — is published **three times, and all three times the sentence names the number of studies behind it** ("owes to three programs", "driven by just three programs", "The result is driven by a small number of studies but is intriguing"). The named programmes are given, so a reader can go and look. This is the practice our noise checks are for, expressed in prose: **a heterogeneity result states its cell count in the sentence that states its magnitude.** Note also the ordering in §6.5.5: the caveat and the interest are in one sentence, with the caveat first — "The result is driven by a small number of studies but is intriguing in light of the excitement about 'sector programs'" — so the reader cannot quote the interest without the caveat.

### Correlates, conjecture, and how a conjecture gets tested on the spot

> Intriguingly, the local unemployment rate is also associated with the effectiveness of training programs. […] Where the unemployment rate is 1 point higher, a training program boosts employment by 1.2 points more in the medium term (year 2 after randomization) and earnings by $250/year. However, the association is much weaker in the longer term (years 3–5).

> Several theories could explain the medium-term association between the local unemployment rate and the impact of training. When unemployment is high, the marginal participant may be someone who just lost a job… It could also be that mismatch unemployment is higher when unemployment is higher… A final explanation is that when unemployment spikes, competition for training increases. That reduces the alternatives available to the control group…

> Evidence not shown in the table supports the latter theory. In the initial block-by-block regressions, a 1-point increase in unemployment is associated with 3.4 more points of program participation; the standard error is 2.8, meaning that the finding, while suggestive, does not meet the statistical significance "survival" criterion applied here. But when we switch from participation in the program of interest to participation in any training program, the medium-term association with the unemployment rate becomes even bigger and quite statistically strong, at 4.7 points (standard error 1.8).

> Overall, the results relating to the unemployment rate suggest that training helps people bounce back faster after losing their jobs. However, the lack of longer-term association suggests that local job market conditions do not matter for whether training puts people on a permanently better work track.

**Annotation.**

- **Three candidate mechanisms are named, then one of them is tested.** The paper does not stop at "several theories could explain" — it goes and finds a discriminating statistic and reports it with its standard error, *including the one that failed its own significance rule* ("3.4 more points… the standard error is 2.8… does not meet the statistical significance 'survival' criterion"). **A conjecture that can be checked against the same data is checked in the paragraph that raises it, and the check is reported whichever way it comes out.** This is the strongest observation-versus-conjecture practice in the corpus so far: the boundary is marked not by a modal but by a number.
- **The inconvenient pattern is kept and interpreted twice.** The medium-term association is strong and the long-term one is not; the paper offers both readings ("might just owe to the shrinkage of sample and the loss of statistical power. Or it may be…"), runs a further specification in Appendix F, and then states the consequence in plain words in the summary sentence: training "helps people bounce back faster" but "local job market conditions do not matter for whether training puts people on a permanently better work track". **Where two horizons disagree, the finding is the disagreement, and it is stated as a sentence about the world.**
- **"Intriguingly" is the whole of the editorialising**, and it sits in front of an observation rather than an inference.

### The method caveats that sit with the numbers they threaten

> The humble warning in Card, Kluve, and Weber (2010)—"There are likely to be measurement errors and errors of interpretation in the extraction of information from the studies."—applies as well to this AI-accelerated effort. The collection and collation of studies involves many judgment calls—some here made by humans and some by AI. Does an intervention involve training enough that it belongs in the sample? Is training primary in the intervention? If an impact is reported for months 7–18 since randomization, is that a short-term (year-1) or medium-term (year-2) impact?

> To improve the extraction, we instruct Claude to: a) produce a "reasoning table" documenting sources and logic for extracted values and b) perform an adversarial review of all extractions, by reading the reasoning table and returning to sources. As in analyses done without the aid of AI, as we learn our way around the data and analyze it, we spot-check and revise the extraction.

> Another limitation is typical in this literature: many non-academic impact studies do not report continuous metrics of statistical significance, such as standard errors. […] for the continuous outcome of earnings, we follow Greenberg, Michalopoulos, and Robins (2006, note 9) in imputing p = 0.005, 0.03, 0.075, and 0.55 for p < 0.01, p < 0.05, p < 0.1, and p > 0.1. These uncertain imputations influence the meta-analytic, random-effects results.

**Annotation.**

- **The AI-assisted step is disclosed where it happens, inherits a predecessor's warning verbatim, and is followed by its controls.** Borrowing CKW's sentence rather than writing a new one is a deliberate move: it says the new risk is the old risk. The three questions that follow are the actual judgment calls, written as questions a sceptic would ask. Then the controls — reasoning table, adversarial re-read against sources, human spot-checks. **An automated extraction step is described by what it was asked to produce and what was done to catch it being wrong.** This is the paragraph our own posts should imitate whenever Claude touches the data, and it is more useful than any assistance-disclosure boilerplate.
- **An imputation is disclosed with its exact values and its consequence.** "imputing p = 0.005, 0.03, 0.075, and 0.55" and then, flatly, "These uncertain imputations influence the meta-analytic, random-effects results." No estimate of how much. **A necessary fudge is published with its numbers and an admission that it matters**; what is missing, and what a referee asks for, is the same analysis without it.

## Comparisons

The paper is built on comparisons of comparisons: within a trial (treatment against control), across trials (programme type against programme type), across time horizons, and between its own evidence base and its predecessors'. The house rule — the comparison is the finding — is satisfied in six distinct ways here.

### Sector programmes against the average programme — the spine

> As a group, the sector programs cost no more than training-primary programs—$11,602 versus $13,598 per treatment group member, on average. Yet the discounted present value of their impacts on pre-tax earnings comes to $60,319 instead of $14,146. The 7x benefit-cost ratio is not as large as that for the most successful ones. But it is still enough that if the government successfully ran such programs, they would pay for themselves through higher tax revenues and lower use of public benefits.

> Although sector programs' benefits greatly exceed those of the average training-primary intervention, their costs do not: $11,602 per treatment group member instead of $13,598. As a result, we estimate the benefit-cost ratio of sector programs at 7.18, almost five times the 1.44 for the larger group.

**Annotation.** Both levels, both times, in the same units, with the denominator ("per treatment group member") restated in both places. The comparison is constructed so that the finding is a *ratio of two ratios* and the reader can see every input: two costs, two benefits, two B/C ratios, and the factor between them ("almost five times"). Nothing is left for the reader to divide except as a check. **A comparison whose inputs are all published is a comparison a reader can audit**, and this is the corpus's best instance. Note also that the sentence deflates itself twice — "cost no more than", "is not as large as that for the most successful ones" — so the headline factor arrives already bounded from both sides.

### The control groups — the comparison that qualifies the comparison

> The control-group means in Table 6 also capture differences in the populations served, reflecting the selectivity of sector programs. For experiments incorporating training, the long-term employment rate among controls is 60% compared to 80% for sector programs. The control groups from sector programs also earn 50–75% more depending on the time horizon. **Sector programs draw from populations that are higher paid even without training.**

**Annotation.** The paper's most quotable result is immediately undercut by comparing the two *control* groups — that is, by showing that the two treatment effects are estimated on different populations. The evidence for the qualification is the same table that produced the finding. **The selection story is told with the untreated arms, which is the only place it can be seen**, and it makes the qualification quantitative rather than rhetorical. For our posts this is the model for any comparison across samples we did not assign: **publish the control-side levels next to the effects, and let the reader see who the two groups are before comparing what happened to them.**

### Intention-to-treat against local average treatment effect — one estimate under two definitions

> Which is more helpful for understanding these impacts under labor market disruption? ITTs might be the best measure of a program's overall impact in its context… But the LATE could indicate a program's potential to help in a context where it is more needed and wanted… We default to the ITT in what follows since it's easier to define and seems just as likely to fit the future labor markets we imagine given the high uncertainty. But we also report the average increase in participation to convey how available training was in these different contexts, and so that interested readers can estimate the benefits in a world of fuller compliance.

**Annotation.** A choice between two estimands is made explicitly, argued on the grounds of the *question being asked* rather than on statistical convenience, and then made reversible by publishing the ingredient a reader needs to compute the other one. **A defensible estimand choice names the alternative, says why the alternative might be right, states the choice, and publishes the conversion factor.** Our pre-registrations should read like this paragraph.

### This review's sample against the prior meta-analyses' — contribution stated in counts

> The sample covers its niche more thoroughly than previous meta-analyses. Thirty-seven of the 56 studies are in Smedslund et al. (2006), zero are in Haelermans and Borghans (2012), 2 are in Card, Kluve, and Weber (2018), and 26 are in Peck et al. (2021).

**Annotation.** Four overlap counts, one per predecessor, in one sentence — including the zero. This is what "states plainly what it adds and where it overlaps" looks like when it is done with arithmetic instead of adjectives, and it is directly copyable into our posts' relationship to Anthropic's published work. **Overlap with prior work is reported as a count of shared units, not as a characterisation.** The §6 preamble does the qualitative half of the same job in one sentence per predecessor ("Smedslund et al. (2006) looks at randomized evaluations of welfare-to-work programs, almost all from the US; Haelermans and Borghans (2012) at non-randomized studies of on-the-job training worldwide; …"), ending with the paper's own.

### The paper's evidence base against Katz et al.'s — re-drawing someone else's reference class

> Katz et al. (2022) is not a meta-analysis or systematic review. Its purpose is to use the results from certain encouraging evaluations to test economic theories about why they work. Nevertheless, because Katz et al. has become a proof point for the idea that sector programs work, it is worth thinking critically about how well its 9 studies of 8 programs represent the body of evidence on sector programs.

> This exercise confronts us with a distinction between intention and execution. […] One can argue that the low-fidelity sites should be deleted from the sectoral strategy evidence record. Loosely speaking, they don't count because they didn't do it, or didn't do it right. This makes sense when the goal is to learn from success. But it is sometimes more meaningful to generalize from what was planned than what was done. For a decision-maker pondering whether to greenlight a project to bring sector-based training to more people, the decision is not exactly whether to do that but whether to back people who plan to do it. The proper reference class for predicting impacts is then the set of past projects with similar stated plans.

> It is of course arguable whether the trait we have singled out as definitional—employer engagement in curricula—truly is. Nevertheless, we believe this exercise expands the reference class of sector programs in a way that is reasonably meaningful and minimally arbitrary.

> In sum, this critically motivated expansion of the collection of studies in Katz et al. fundamentally reinforces the optimism about sector programs. Even when averaging in programs that did not manage to work closely with employers, the earnings impacts are 3–5 times higher than for the average job training program.

**Annotation.**

- **The critique names what the criticised paper was trying to do before saying what it does not do.** "Katz et al. (2022) is not a meta-analysis or systematic review. Its purpose is to…" — and then the reason it is nonetheless fair to re-examine it: "because Katz et al. has become a proof point". **A re-examination is justified by how a paper is used, not by what it claims.**
- **The sample-definition choice is argued from the decision the reader faces.** Intention versus execution, resolved by asking what reference class predicts the outcome of *funding a plan*. This is a pre-registration argument in disguise: it decides an inclusion rule on grounds that are independent of the results, and it says so.
- **The added studies are enumerated and the arbitrariness of the criterion is conceded in the sentence that concludes the exercise.** The test of an expansion like this is whether the conclusion survives a hostile expansion, and the paper reports that it does, in its own units ("3–5 times higher"). **An adversarial robustness check is designed by widening the sample in the direction that should hurt.**
- **And the expansion's result is a number that disagrees with the paper's own headline** — "3–5 times higher" here, "10 times as much" in the Executive summary, "an order of magnitude more" in §6.5.5 and §8. The three are computed on different bases (the selected high-performers, the hiring-commitment coefficient, the expanded sector family) but no sentence reconciles them. Recorded as a writing fault to avoid: **when the same relationship is reported at three magnitudes, one sentence has to say which is the headline and why.**

### Self-report against official records — the comparison that impeaches a measure

> Because, in the right panel, self-reported income in 1996–98 rises especially steeply in the treatment group, the 1998 treatment-control difference is much larger going by self-report than by Social Security data, at $972 versus $218. […] As a first step, SBM recomputes the Social Security–based averages just for the self-reporting sample. That nearly doubles the Social Security treatment-control difference, to $393, and strongly suggests that attrition bias is present in the self-reports.

> The contrast between the two is extraordinary: –$4 versus –$4,209. Despite much investigation, Orr et al. (1994, note 33) finds no convincing explanation for the latter. Perhaps it is best seen as a data point on the unreliability of self-reported income.

**Annotation.** The same quantity measured two ways, with both values printed and the gap decomposed into the part explained by sample composition ($218 → $393) and the part left over. The second instance shows what to do when the gap cannot be explained: print both, report that the original authors could not explain it, and draw the only safe conclusion. **Two measurements of one quantity are the strongest available check, and when they disagree the disagreement is published in both numbers.** The report later uses the distinction as a study trait in Table 1 ("Outcome data from official records (%)") and as a regressor in Table 3, so the methodological worry becomes a variable — a move worth copying: **a data-quality concern that can be coded becomes a covariate, not a caveat.**

### Randomized against non-randomized — the comparison that is the premise

> For years, cardiologists took vitamin E because careful observational studies found a persistent correlation between dietary vitamin E and heart health; randomized trials ultimately ruled this out… And even one of the shining sectoral programs we review has put out annual reports with non-causal estimates suggesting an 8x larger wage gain than was recorded in its randomized trial.

> This is why we devote most of our attention to randomized evaluations, and a few observational studies with a claim to pseudo-randomization. There are some newer programs with suggestive observational results. But without randomized evidence, and given the long trail of initially promising failures, we might default to "ineffective until credibly proven otherwise."

**Annotation.** The evidentiary standard is justified by three instances of it mattering — one from medicine, one from cash transfers, one from *this very literature* — and the third is the one that does the work because it is on-topic and quantified ("an 8x larger wage gain"). Then the standard is stated as a prior in six words. **An inclusion rule is argued with cases where the excluded evidence was wrong, and the most persuasive case is the one inside the reader's own field.**

### Cross-group comparison as a standing caveat, re-invoked at every use

> Even when the individual studies are compelling as to causation—thanks, say, to randomization—comparisons across them are not. If by chance effective approaches were evaluated in New York and not in New Jersey, that does not prove that training works better in New York. (Appendix E)

> That said, the caveat about cross-group comparisons bears repeating: no matter how rigorous the individual studies, meta-analytical comparisons among them are not as dispositive as to which factors cause training to be more effective as distinct from merely being correlated with them. (§6.5.4)

> But that statement makes a cross-group comparison (as defined in Appendix E), and so should be interpreted gingerly. (§6.1)

> As with the CKW meta-analysis, it is hard to know how much weight to put on these non-experimental correlations, even as they look broadly plausible. (§6.4)

> We would be surprised if serving women rather than men truly causes programs to lift employment by 6 more points. (§6.3)

**Annotation.** One caveat, defined once in an appendix with a worked example, then re-invoked by name at each of its four uses — including against the paper's own results. The last quotation shows the sharpest version: rather than restating the caveat abstractly, the paper says which specific finding it does not believe, and why the reader should not either. **A standing caveat is defined once, named at each use, and applied to the author's own numbers at least as hard as to other people's.**

## Figure captions

Fifteen numbered figures in the body and one in Appendix B; six numbered tables in the body and three in the appendices. The pattern is uniform and it is **the most template-like in the corpus**: below the exhibit, a bold "Figure N." or "Table N." followed by a sentence-case descriptive title, then an italic paragraph opening with the word "Notes:". Verified visually on two rendered pages (printed pp. 9 and 57).

### The title is a description, not a finding

> **Figure 1. Earnings impacts in 56 randomized evaluations of job training programs in the US**

> **Figure 7. Employment and earnings by quarter in the three arms of the Workforce Investment Act Gold Standard evaluation, in the adult and dislocated-worker programs**

> **Figure 13. Employment impact of sector programs**

**Annotation.** Not one of the 15 figure titles asserts a result. Each names the outcome, the population and, where it matters, the design ("in the three arms of…"). ### The notes carry provenance to the table number

> *Notes: Based on Schochet, Burghardt, and McConnell (2008), Table 4, and Schochet (2021), Tables A.1–A.2.*

Some in the core-and-intensive group were offered short-term prevocational training. The core group was not offered training.*

**Annotation.** Every figure that redraws someone else's numbers says whose, from which table, and — critically — whether it was **"Copied from"**, **"Based on"**, **"Reproduced from"** or **"Adapted from"**. Four verbs, used consistently: copied means the image is theirs, based on means the data are theirs and the drawing is the paper's, reproduced means an excerpt, adapted means redrawn with changes. **A one-word provenance verb in the caption tells the reader how much of the exhibit is the author's.** This is cheap, and our evidence drawers should adopt it. Note too that Figure 8's notes state the currency year and the interval width (90%, not 95%) because they are the source's, not the paper's; **where a borrowed figure's conventions differ from the paper's, the caption says so.**

### The notes define the exhibit for a reader who arrives at it cold

> *Notes: This figure shows the share employed in the treatment and control groups in the National Supported Work Demonstration by quarter after enrollment, separately for the four target groups. The dashed line shows the share of the treatment group employed in a supported-work job. Estimates are regression-adjusted for age, sex, race, education, prior work experience, household composition, site, and length of site operation. The shaded band marks the maximum permitted stay in supported work—12 or 18 months, varied experimentally across sites. The dotted vertical line marks each group's average actual stay. AFDC follow-up interviews ended at 27 months (quarter 9); the other groups were followed to 36 months. Source: employment rates from MDRC (1980), Tables 4-1, 5-1, 6-1, and 7-1; maximum stay from p. 35; average length of participation from Table 2-7.* (Figure 2)

**Annotation.** The longest caption in the report and the model of the form: what is plotted → what each line type means → the adjustment set, enumerated in full → what each shaded or dotted element marks → the follow-up windows, including the one group whose window is shorter → source, itemised to the table and page for each component. Eight jobs, one paragraph, no wasted clause. **Every graphical element that is not a data point is explained** — band, dashed line, dotted line — which is what makes the figure liftable. The one thing absent here and everywhere in the report: **no caption states an n.** Sample sizes live in Table 1, in the study-level tables, and in the forest plots' own panels; a figure lifted out of the paper carries no sample size with it.

### The notes carry the estimator, the uncertainty and the star convention

The numbers in parentheses are standard deviations of control-group values and standard errors of impact estimates. Sample sizes vary because of missing data. Short-term outcomes have a ~1-year follow-up, medium-term ~2 years, and long-term 3–5 years.* (Table 2)

> *Notes: REML estimates. Employment impacts are measured in percent, and earnings impacts are in 2025 dollars. Standard errors in parentheses, clustered by study. Results in MI columns (✓) are pooled across 10 imputations; other columns use complete cases. > *Notes: "RE" signifies the mean effect from random-effects meta-analysis. The lengths of whiskers and diamonds depict 95% confidence intervals, which are also reported numerically on the right. All results are extracted from sources cited in Table 5. All are intention-to-treat (ITT) impacts.* (Figure 13)

**Annotation.** Three things worth stealing. First, **the parentheses are explained even when the convention is obvious**, and here the explanation is necessary because two different things sit in parentheses in one table (standard deviations for control means, standard errors for impacts). Second, **"Sample sizes vary because of missing data"** appears in every table that has a varying N, so the reader never has to guess whether a changing N signals a changing sample. Third, Table 3's notes pre-empt the reader's sharpest question — why two columns with identical N give different results — and answer it in the caption rather than the text. **A caption that anticipates the objection an attentive reader will raise is doing the work of a paragraph.** The timeframe definitions are restated in every caption that uses them, exactly as the corpus's other pieces restate their frequency thresholds.

## Limitations

**There is no Limitations section and no Caveats heading.** The qualification is heavier than in any other piece in the corpus and it is distributed across five layers, each with a distinct job.

**Layer 1 — a critical-themes section *before* the evidence (§3, printed pp. 11–15).** Five subsections, each a way the literature can mislead, written as instructions for reading what follows: 3.1 The importance of randomization; 3.2 Imperfect compliance; 3.3 Attrition; 3.4 Academic vs non-academic publication; 3.5 Success is ephemeral. Its own framing sentence:

> Most of the studies we cover exploit randomization in order to rigorously estimate the impact of job training on employment and earnings. But even the methodological armor of randomization has chinks. In learning from the research, it is important to think critically about the ways that studies may fail to measure what they aspire to measure (what is called internal validity) and how well their results generalize (external validity).

And its one section that is a limitation on the whole enterprise rather than on a statistic:

> Unlike, say, unemployment insurance, training programs constitute a variegated and nebulous class. They differ in where they operate, how they recruit, who they teach, what they teach, how they teach, how long they teach, and who is teaching.

> Thus, it is a live question for this review: if private charities, working with corporations and foundations, strike upon a promising system, can governments bring it to far more people without denaturing it? If key traits for success are not only ephemeral but fragile, then they can be distorted away by a new administrative and incentive regime.

**Layer 2 — the scope limitation, at the head of the concluding section (§8, printed p. 79):**

> A key limitation of this report, however, is that it only covers stand-alone job training programs. Those are only one channel through which people gain job skills. Beyond our scope are high school vocational programs, apprenticeships, community colleges, four-year colleges, professional schools, and online education. A vast literature estimates how much other forms of education boost earnings, some using promising research designs like lotteries (e.g., Grosz 2020). In the US, job training programs have been unusually amenable to randomized experiments because they are publicly funded yet not considered a public right. That makes them subject to pressure to prove effectiveness, yet discretionary enough to—for the sake of research—randomly deny or defer access.

**Layer 3 — finding-level qualifications, in the sentence or the section they qualify:**

> The programs pass a cost-benefit test, but this relies on uncertain assumptions about how long the benefits last. *(Executive summary)*

> In light of the great uncertainties in the projection of impacts, this result should be read as a little better than break-even. *(§1.2)*

> We find that this estimate, likely an upper bound, is about 2–4 times higher than our main estimates, although it does not change the benefit-cost picture. *(§1.4)*

> The result is driven by a small number of studies but is intriguing in light of the excitement about "sector programs," to which we will soon turn. *(§6.5.5)*

> Subject to the caveat that the sample of studies shifts from plot to plot, sector programs on average cause a partially transient employment bump… *(§7.3)*

> While more research is needed to determine which traits are key to their success—and not all sector programs have produced such impressive results—several factors probably matter… *(§1.6)*

> It is appropriate to theorize post hoc about why certain programs work. But it is also tricky. The sample of studies is small. It is tempting to explain away failures as not being true sector programs. Comparisons across trials are subject to the caveat that correlation is not causation. *(§7.2)*

> It is unclear—and a question for further research—exactly how important each of these components is. *(§7.4)*

**Layer 4 — method-level limitations at the method** (the Claude extraction paragraph and the imputation paragraph, both quoted under `## Findings and their caveats`), and in a footnote at the evidentiary standard:

> We do not claim to have perfectly applied our "high evidentiary standard." Notably, we do not cover any matching studies even though some are arguably as credible as research we include. […] A good recent example of such matching, which we do not include, is the Rothstein et al. (2022) evaluation of training programs in California. Such studies arguably deserve as much weight as Hyman (2018), which we cover in §5.1, and whose (partial) credibility rests in part on a placebo check. *(footnote 3)*

**Layer 5 — the limitations of the benefit-cost model, at the end of Appendix G (printed pp. 122–123), numbered and signed:**

> These benefit-cost calculations are subject to several limitations. First, we assume that the income gains from these programs decay slowly, following the literature on income shocks (Karahan and Ozkan 2013). This matters less for sector programs, since they pay for themselves even looking only at the years where subjects are tracked. But for the training-only programs, the bulk of the benefit accrues in the extrapolation beyond the studies' follow-up windows. **Without that persistence, they would have a benefit-cost ratio under 1.** When available, the evidence does find persistent gains: among the 28 training-primary estimates with a medium-term gain of at least $200 and a longer follow-up (typically years 2 and 4), long-term impacts totaled 110% of medium-term impacts, with a median ratio of 0.90.

> Second, jobs have benefits outside of wages. They might decrease crime or improve mental health. Including these would increase the benefits side of the ledger, but we opt not to because the research is so uncertain on this. Finally, we assume away general equilibrium or displacement effects. If trainees displace other workers seeking the same jobs, the benefits we measure are inflated.

**Annotation.**

- **The best limitation sentence in the corpus is the one that names the threshold at which the conclusion flips.** "Without that persistence, they would have a benefit-cost ratio under 1." The headline "programs pass a cost-benefit test" rests on an extrapolation past the end of every study's follow-up; the limitation says so, says which programmes it matters for and which it does not, states what the conclusion becomes if the assumption fails, and then supplies the evidence for the assumption with its n and both its mean and median. **A limitation is worth most when it tells the reader what number would have to be different, and by how much, for the finding to reverse.** Our template's limitations section should be written to this standard; nothing else in the corpus reaches it.
- **Every limitation in Layer 5 is signed.** Persistence — the benefit is overstated if absent. Non-wage benefits — "would increase the benefits side of the ledger", so the estimate is conservative. General equilibrium — "the benefits we measure are inflated". Three limitations, three directions, one of them favourable to the paper. **A limitations list where every item cuts the same way is an apology; a list with directions is an error budget.**
- **The scope limitation explains why the scope exists.** It does not merely list the channels left out; it says why they are missing from the evidence base at all: job training is over-represented in the randomized literature *because* it is publicly funded and discretionary, and everything else that teaches skills is under-represented for the same reason. **A selection-into-the-literature argument is the limitation a review most needs**, and it is the analogue of the sample-selection paragraph our posts owe. It is also raised again, from the other side, in Appendix A.5 ("The placement of randomized studies is not random") and in §7.2 ("The placement of randomized studies is indeed non-random. Several programs got evaluated more than once…"), where the paper notes that the programmes willing to be evaluated twice are the ones whose managers expected to survive it.
- **Placing the critical themes before the evidence changes what they can do.** A limitations section at the end tells a reader how much to discount a conclusion they have already formed; §3 tells them how to read the 60 pages that follow, and the report then invokes its themes by number at each use ("see §3.3", "As discussed in section 3.2"). The cost is the one the coding-agents file identified in a different form: **a reader who skims the table of contents sees "Critical themes" and cannot tell that it is the limitations.** Our template keeps Limitations as a named heading; the device to borrow is the *cross-reference* — a caveat defined once and named at each point of use.
- **What a referee would raise first, in order.** (1) **No minimum detectable effect beside any null.** The WIA and Job Corps nulls are central to the report's policy conclusion and no sentence states what effect those trials could have excluded; the nearest thing is the uptake differential. (2) **No publication-bias test on the new meta-analysis.** The report explains funnel plots and publication bias carefully (§3.4, Appendix E), reports other meta-analyses' funnel asymmetry, and argues that this literature should have little bias because it is government-funded and non-academic — but it argues rather than tests, on a sample where 7% of estimates come from academic publications and the trait is coded in Table 1. (3) **The "survivor" selection procedure is a multiple-comparisons device with no correction.** Block-by-block regressions, retention at p < 0.1 plus a standardized-coefficient threshold, then a final regression on the survivors; inherited from Peck et al. and disclosed in the caption, but the reported standard errors do not account for the selection. (4) **Imputed p-values drive the earnings results** wherever standard errors are missing, and no complete-case comparison is shown. (5) **The cost side rests on a subsample** — "the 67% of training-primary interventions whose write-ups include cost information" — and the benefit-cost ratios are computed as if the missing third were like the rest. (6) **The sector-programme definition is chosen by the authors** ("We therefore provisionally equate 'sector program' with employer involvement in curricula"), and the headline sector result depends on it; the paper concedes this in one sentence and does not show the result under a competing definition. (7) **Three magnitudes for one relationship** — 10×, order-of-magnitude, 3–5× — with no reconciling sentence. Every one of these is visible because the report disclosed the practice that produces it; none is hidden.
- **What the report qualifies that a weaker piece would not.** That its own headline is an extrapolation past the data. That its most exciting coefficient rests on three programmes. That its central construct has no agreed definition and that the definition it uses is its own. That the evidence base was not assembled to a protocol. That an AI system did the extraction and might have got it wrong. That the populations in its two compared groups differ before treatment. That its policy conclusion is drawn from programmes that mostly served people unlike the workers AI would displace.

## Close

**Verbatim, the four recommendation headings of §8 (printed pp. 82–83) and the whole of §9 Discussion (pp. 83–84):**

> **8.1 The sort of short-duration programs that are prominent in this review could help some "low-skill" workers displaced by AI.**
> **8.2 Short-duration programs are ill-matched to the needs of more-skilled workers.**
> **8.3 The Trade Adjustment Assistance (TAA) approach is worth considering for people who are demonstrably fired because of AI.**
> **8.4 Public and private funders should aggressively support demonstrations, evaluations, and scaling of sector programs.**

> ## 9 Discussion

> Over the decades, the occupational mix in the US has shifted dramatically (Autor 2015). Many jobs have disappeared. These shifts are not unusual, and they are not always associated with large-scale economic distress. Cavounidis et al. (2023) show, for example, that wages rose for horse-driving teamsters even as employment in the occupation declined. But the rollout of trucks was gradual. A major economic worry with AI is the combination of immense capability and compressed timing.

> It's difficult to find evidence of any large impacts of AI on employment yet. But rapid automation would not be outside of the range of possibility. People working as elevator operators, film projectionists, and telephone switchboard operators went through condensed periods of disemployment.

> While plenty of observers do not predict increases in unemployment in the near-term, the uncertainty seems great enough to want a firm handle on the policy options. This report studied what we know on one of the most popular ones, worker retraining.

> We find that, on average, the programs have clearly positive but small impacts. Our current suite of training programs may be a defensible government expenditure, in that the investments ultimately cover their initial cost over a recipient's working life. But the impacts are small enough that they would not leave a dent in a persistently high unemployment rate. We see promise in the cluster of sector programs, although these have not been tested at scale.

> The best response would likely be a combination of partial responses. In that spirit, and in light of the potentially great need, we think it's best for governments and other funders to aggressively explore and develop multiple responses at once. That includes job training, and especially sector programs. Some of these investigations are evergreen: yielding helpful evidence regardless of AI's impact on unemployment. Whether or not AI disrupts the labor market, we will not regret having learned how to best help workers adapt.

And the last sentence of §1.7, the Overview's version of the same close:

> But that risk implies that we should supercharge effective experimentation now, rather than wait for an employment emergency. If effective training requires organizational legitimacy and deep ties to employers, it is better to start scaling successful programs now than wait for a single, top-down federal response. A rigorous approach would pay dividends even by cataloging the failures.

**Annotation.**

- **What was learned, in one paragraph, with exactly one number-free sentence per finding.** "clearly positive but small impacts"; "may be a defensible government expenditure, in that the investments ultimately cover their initial cost over a recipient's working life"; "would not leave a dent in a persistently high unemployment rate"; "We see promise in the cluster of sector programs, although these have not been tested at scale." Four findings, four fences — "on average", "may be", "small enough that", "although these have not been tested at scale" — and no figure reproduced from the body. **The close states what was learned in words and leaves the numbers in the findings**, which is now unanimous across every piece in the corpus.
- **The close answers the opening question in the opening's own words.** The Executive summary asked "we ask if these efforts would work"; the close says "the programs have clearly positive but small impacts" and then converts that into the answer to the policy question that motivated it: not whether the programmes work, but whether they would be *enough* — "would not leave a dent in a persistently high unemployment rate". **A post that opens on a decision closes on the decision, not on the estimate.**
- **The strongest sentence in the close is the one that makes the recommendation robust to the paper's own uncertainty.** "Some of these investigations are evergreen: yielding helpful evidence regardless of AI's impact on unemployment. Whether or not AI disrupts the labor market, we will not regret having learned how to best help workers adapt." The paper's premise is a contingency it cannot forecast, and rather than betting on the contingency the close identifies the action that is right under both branches. **When the motivating uncertainty cannot be resolved, the close recommends what is worth doing under either resolution** — and that is the rarest and most useful thing in this file. Compare §1.7's version, which does the same job with a different argument: "A rigorous approach would pay dividends even by cataloging the failures."
- **Recommendations are named, addressed and bounded.** §8.4 names the actor ("Public and private funders"), the action ("support demonstrations, evaluations, and scaling"), the object ("sector programs") and the instrument ("One potential option is a 'fire drill' that attempts to rapidly scale one of these programs for a targeted population"). §8.3 names a mechanism and its failure mode in the same subsection: "the TAA's trigger-based approach only works for people who can make the case that they lost their jobs to a disruptive force such as AI. When the labor market transforms not through firings, but by firms not hiring new people in certain roles… trigger-based aid will not fire." **A recommendation states who acts, what they do, and the circumstance in which it would not work.** This is the model for our posts' recommendations section — and note the contrast with `economic-index-2025-02` (which declined to prescribe) and `coding-agents-social-sciences-2026-05` (which prescribed nothing at all).
- **One of the four recommendations is against the paper's own subject.** §8.2 says short-duration programmes — the entire evidence base of the review — are "ill-matched to the needs of more-skilled workers", and supports it with three numbered reasons, one of which is an outside study showing that deep reskilling took "a typical four years of college". **A recommendation section that only recommends the thing the paper studied is a sales document**; this one names the population its evidence cannot help, which is what makes the other three credible.
- **The conjecture in the close is marked by being made about the past.** The Discussion's opening paragraph argues by historical analogy — teamsters, elevator operators, film projectionists, telephone switchboard operators — and every speculative claim about AI is carried by a counterfactual verb: "rapid automation would not be outside of the range of possibility", "A major economic worry with AI is the combination of immense capability and compressed timing". The paper never asserts that AI will displace anyone. **Where the premise is a forecast, the close writes the forecast as other people's worry and the evidence as the author's own.**
- **Title against ending.** The PDF's title — "An evidence review of worker retraining" — is a genre label with no verb and no finding, and the close does not conjugate it; what the close answers is the *page's* title, "How well do job retraining programs work?", which is a question the PDF asks only in the Executive summary's second sentence. So the match between title and ending exists, but it is spread across two documents: **the web title is the question, the PDF is the evidence, and the Discussion is the answer.** Recorded as a structural observation about a publication that ships in three forms; our posts ship in one, and the rule stands — the title matches the ending.
- **First person throughout, in three grammatical persons that our style has to separate.** The research "we" ("we ask", "we find", "We screened the literature"); the authorial-judgement "we" ("we think it's best", "We see promise", "we believe this exercise expands the reference class"); and the collective-humanity "we" of the last sentence ("we will not regret having learned"). The first two de-personalise cleanly — "this review finds", "the evidence suggests" — but the third does not: "we will not regret" is a claim about a shared future that no impersonal construction reproduces without loss. The substitute is to name the beneficiary of the knowledge, which is weaker, and knowing that it is weaker is the point of recording it.
- **No summary block at the end, and the report does not need one**, because §8's "The major findings:" bullets have already done it 1,500 words earlier and the Discussion restates them in words. The test the corpus keeps applying holds here: if the close can stand as the summary, the summary block was never load-bearing. This report has one anyway, at the front, and it is the one place where its numbers drift (see the compression table in `## Section order`).
