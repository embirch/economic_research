**Where the findings sit.** Three times, at three depths, in the same order each time: the numbered summary, then one question-headed section each, then one sentence of the close. The three section headings are a **how / what / who ladder** — "How do developers interact with Claude?", "What are developers building with Claude?", "Who is using Claude for coding?" — which is a different organising device from the February report's single "Results" with bold lead-ins, and a better one for a reader skimming a table of contents: each heading is the question its section answers, and the three together enumerate the design.

**Two placements worth noting as deliberate.** (1) **Limitations is promoted** out of the results and given its own section between the last finding and the close, so the close is read through it — same effect as February's placement, achieved with a heading of equal rank rather than a sub-heading. (2) **The appendix finding sits after the close.** The software-versus-non-software comparison is a real result with real numbers, and it is placed downstream of "Looking ahead" and of the recruiting block. A reader who stops at the close never learns that coding's automation premium over non-coding work is driven by Feedback Loop and *offset* by Directive. If we run a supplementary analysis that qualifies a headline, it goes under the finding it qualifies.

This is the thinnest method section in the corpus so far. The reader meets "500,000 coding-related interactions" in the opening, "our privacy-preserving analysis tool" in the method paragraph, and the eight-day window, the plan restrictions and the weighting rule only if they follow a superscript.

## Opening move

Verbatim, the three opening paragraphs and the numbered list (hyperlink URLs stripped; anchor text marked below):

> Jobs that involve computer programming are a small sector of the modern economy, but an influential one. The past couple of years have seen them changed dramatically by the introduction of AI systems that can assist with—and automate—significant amounts of coding work.

> In our previous Economic Index research, we found very disproportionate use of Claude by US workers in computer-related occupations: that is, there were many more conversations with Claude about computer-related tasks than one would predict from the number of people working in relevant jobs. It's the same in the educational context: Computer Science degrees—which involve large amounts of coding—show highly disproportionate AI use.

> To understand these changes in more detail, we conducted an analysis of 500,000 coding-related interactions across Claude.ai (the "default" way that most people interact with Claude) and Claude Code (our new specialist coding "agent" that can independently accomplish chains of complex tasks using a variety of digital tools).

> We found three key patterns:
>
> 1. **The coding agent is used for more automation.** 79% of conversations on Claude Code were identified as "automation"—where AI directly performs tasks—rather than "augmentation," where AI collaborates with and enhances human capabilities (21%). In contrast, only 49% of Claude.ai conversations were classified as automation. *This might imply that as AI agents become more commonplace, and as more agentic AI products are built, we should expect more automation of tasks.*
> 2. **Coders commonly use AI to build user-facing apps.** Web-development languages such as JavaScript and HTML were the most common programming languages used in our dataset, and user interface and user experience tasks were among the top coding uses. *This suggests that jobs that center on making simple applications and user interfaces may face disruption from AI systems sooner than those focused purely on backend work*.
> 3. **Startups are the main early adopters of Claude Code, while enterprises lag behind.** In a preliminary analysis, we estimated that 33% of conversations on Claude Code served startup-related work, compared to only 13% identified as enterprise-relevant applications. *The adoption gap suggests a divide between nimbler organizations using cutting-edge AI tools, and traditional enterprises.*

Anchor text carrying links, in order: "previous Economic Index research" → the February report; "the educational context" → the education report; "Claude.ai" → a claude.ai redirect; "Claude Code" → the Claude Code docs.

**Annotation.**

- **Why it matters, in eleven words, as a concession.** "Jobs that involve computer programming are a small sector of the modern economy, but an influential one." The opening move is *concede the objection, then elevate*: the reader's first reaction to a coding study — this is a niche — is stated by the author before the reader can state it, and then overturned in four words. This is the most efficient opening in the corpus and the one to copy when a post studies a narrow population. Compare the February report, which opened on a forecast about everyone ("AI systems will have a major impact on the ways people work"); a special report cannot borrow that stake, so it earns a smaller one honestly.
- **The puzzle is inherited, not posed.** Paragraph two spends its whole length on two prior findings — the series' own over-representation result and the education report's — and neither is given a number. Both are cited by hyperlink. The effect is to establish that *whether* coding is AI-intensive is settled, so this report's question can be *how*. The transition sentence says exactly that: "To understand these changes in more detail". No question mark appears anywhere in the opening, or anywhere in the report before the close.
- **The comparison is set up before any finding.** The third paragraph defines both arms in one sentence, each in parentheses at first mention, with the contrast built into the glosses: Claude.ai is "the 'default' way that most people interact with Claude"; Claude Code is "our new specialist coding 'agent' that can independently accomplish chains of complex tasks using a variety of digital tools". Default versus specialist, chat versus chains of tasks. A reader who reads only that sentence knows what is being compared and why the comparison could matter.
- **How soon the first number appears.** "500,000" arrives about 120 words in, and it is the *sample size*, not a finding. The first finding number, 79%, is about 190 words in, in bullet 1. So: stakes, prior work, design, sample, then results — two paragraphs of why-it-matters, one of design, and no result until the list.
- **The typography separates observation from conjecture.** Every one of the three bullets is built the same way: a **bold** claim sentence, then roman prose carrying the numbers, then an *italicised* sentence carrying the inference. Roman is what was measured; italic is what it might mean. The italic sentences are the only italics in the body of the report. This is the single most copyable device on the page — a reader can find every conjecture in the summary by looking at the slant of the type — and it is stronger than the February report's reliance on "This likely reflects" alone, because it is visible before it is read.
- **Each bullet's hedges, in place.** Bullet 1: "were identified as", "were classified as" (attribution to a classifier, not to the world), then *"might imply"*, *"we should expect"*. Bullet 2: "in our dataset" (the fence inside the clause, as in February), "were among the top coding uses" (rank, not magnitude), then *"suggests"*, *"may face"*. Bullet 3: "In a preliminary analysis, we estimated that" — the hedge placed at the *head* of the sentence, ahead of the number, so the number cannot be quoted out of the sentence without it — then *"suggests"*.
- **Bullet 1's complement is stated.** "79% ... rather than 'augmentation' ... (21%)" gives both halves so the reader sees the partition rather than doing the subtraction, and "In contrast, only 49%" supplies the comparator in the same breath. "only" is the interpretation, embedded in the number.
- **Bullet 2 is the weakest-written of the three and shows why.** It is the only bullet with no number: "Web-development languages such as JavaScript and HTML were the most common ... and user interface and user experience tasks were among the top coding uses." The body section then supplies 31%, 28%, 14%, 6%, 12% and 8%. A summary bullet that names no magnitude cannot be checked against its section, and its italic conjecture ("may face disruption ... sooner") is then the most load-bearing sentence in the summary with the least support beneath it.
- **Bullet 3 states a number that its own section restates differently.** The bullet gives "33% of conversations on Claude Code served startup-related work, compared to only 13% identified as enterprise-relevant applications". The "Who is using Claude for coding?" section gives "32.9% of Claude Code conversations" for startup work and "only 23.8% of Claude Code conversations" for enterprise work. Startup is the same figure rounded; enterprise is a different figure. Both are in prose, neither is in alt text, and the page carries no erratum. Recorded here as a fact about the writing, not as a judgement on the analysis, because the writing lesson is exact and general: **a finding written twice at two precisions is a finding written twice, and the two versions have to be reconciled by whoever writes the second one.** Our house rule follows — every quantitative sentence maps to one entry in `results.json`, and the same entry is quoted the same way everywhere it appears, captions included.

## Findings and their caveats

### Finding 1 — the automation gap between the two surfaces

Summary version, verbatim:

> 1. **The coding agent is used for more automation.** 79% of conversations on Claude Code were identified as "automation"—where AI directly performs tasks—rather than "augmentation," where AI collaborates with and enhances human capabilities (21%). In contrast, only 49% of Claude.ai conversations were classified as automation. *This might imply that as AI agents become more commonplace, and as more agentic AI products are built, we should expect more automation of tasks.*

Body version, verbatim, the four paragraphs of "How do developers interact with Claude?":

> In our previous Economic Index reports, we separated out "automation," where AI directly performs tasks, from "augmentation," where AI collaborates with a user to perform a task. Here, we found that Claude Code showed dramatically higher automation rates—79% of conversations involved some form of automation, compared to 49% on Claude.ai.

> We also split automation and augmentation into several subtypes (as discussed in our previous work). "Feedback Loop" patterns, where Claude completes tasks autonomously but with help of human validation (for example, where the user sends any errors back to Claude), were nearly twice as common on Claude Code (35.8% of interactions) as Claude.ai (21.3%). "Directive" conversations, where Claude completed a task with minimal user interaction, were also higher on Claude Code (43.8%, versus 27.5% on Claude.ai). All the patterns of augmentation—including "Learning," where the user acquires knowledge from the AI model—were substantially lower on Claude Code than on Claude.ai.

> These results illustrate the differences between specialist, coding-focused agents (in this case, Claude Code) and the more "standard" way that users interact with large language models (i.e., through a chatbot interface like Claude.ai). As more agentic products are released, we might see differences in the way AI is integrated into people's jobs. At least in the case of coding, this might involve more automation of tasks.

> This raises questions about the extent to which developers will still be involved as AI use becomes more common. Importantly, our results do show that even within automation, humans are still very often involved: "Feedback Loop" interactions still require user input (even if that input is simply pasting error messages back to Claude). But it's by no means certain that this pattern will persist into the future, when more capable agentic systems will likely require progressively less user input.

**Annotation.**

- **Number-plus-caveat.** "79% of conversations involved some form of automation" — three fences in eleven words. "of conversations" states the unit (not of developers, not of tasks, not of time); "some form of" widens the predicate so the 79% is not a claim that 79% of the work was done unsupervised; and "involved" is weaker than "were". The summary version used the stricter "were identified as 'automation'", which attributes the label to a classifier instead. Both constructions put the qualification inside the clause that carries the number; neither appends it.
- **Interpretation before magnitude.** "Claude Code showed dramatically higher automation rates—**79%** ..." — the adjective arrives before the figure, so the reader is told how to read the gap before seeing it. Same device as February's "a slight lean towards augmentation" ahead of 57/43, used here in the opposite direction, and the device is only safe because both numbers follow immediately.
- **The definitions are re-stated at the point of use and attributed to the prior work.** "In our previous Economic Index reports, we separated out 'automation,' ... from 'augmentation,' ..." The construct is not re-derived and not renamed; it is quoted from the series with a link. That is the right handling of an inherited construct, and it is also what makes the second limitation bullet legible when it arrives.
- **Every subtype is a pair, and the agent always comes first.** "nearly twice as common on Claude Code (35.8% of interactions) as Claude.ai (21.3%)"; "also higher on Claude Code (43.8%, versus 27.5% on Claude.ai)". Consistent ordering across the section, so the direction of every comparison is the same and the reader never has to check which way round it is. Two different connectives are used for the identical shape ("as", "versus"), which is variety at the cost of parallelism.
- **Where the verbal ratio runs ahead of the arithmetic.** "nearly twice as common" precedes 35.8% and 21.3%. The words are doing what a stated ratio would do, and they are more generous than it. This is a writing observation, not a check of the analysis: **if a sentence characterises a ratio in words, the ratio itself belongs in the sentence**, because a reader who trusts the adjective never computes it and a reader who computes it stops trusting the adjective.
- **A claim about all five subtypes, with one named and none quantified.** "All the patterns of augmentation—including 'Learning,' ...—were substantially lower on Claude Code than on Claude.ai." The universal quantifier is asserted, the exemplar is glossed, and "substantially" substitutes for three numbers that exist only inside the stacked-bar image. The February report did the same thing with its automation subtypes. The pattern across the corpus: **shares that support a headline are in prose; shares that merely complete a distribution are left in the figure.** For our posts the completion belongs in a table in the evidence drawer, and the sentence says so.
- **Observed versus conjectured, and where the paragraph break falls.** Paragraph two is observation only, with no interpretive word in it. Paragraph three begins with a descriptive reading ("These results illustrate the differences between ...") and then turns modal twice in two sentences: "we might see differences", "this might involve more automation". Paragraph four opens on a question the data cannot answer ("This raises questions about the extent to which developers will still be involved"). The escalation from measured to speculative happens across paragraph boundaries, never inside a sentence.
- **The finding is deflated immediately, in the same section.** "Importantly, our results do show that even within automation, humans are still very often involved: 'Feedback Loop' interactions still require user input (even if that input is simply pasting error messages back to Claude)." This is the most important structural improvement over the February report, where the challenge to the 57/43 split sat 400 words downstream in a caveats list. Here the strongest available misreading of 79% — that the agent works alone — is corrected four sentences after the number, with a mechanism ("still require user input") and a worked example ("pasting error messages back"). Then the correction is itself fenced against over-reading in the other direction: "But it's by no means certain that this pattern will persist into the future". Claim, deflation, deflation of the deflation, all inside 300 words.
- **Claude versus AI.** The bold summary line says "The coding agent"; the definitional clauses say AI ("where AI directly performs tasks", "where AI collaborates with a user"); the measured sentences say Claude ("79% of conversations on Claude Code", "Claude completes tasks autonomously", "pasting error messages back to Claude"); the forward-looking sentences say AI again ("the way AI is integrated into people's jobs", "as AI use becomes more common"). The rule holds without exception in this section and is sharper than February's because the two surfaces *are* Claude products, so there is no way to say the finding without naming the product.

### Finding 2 — what is built with Claude

Summary version, verbatim:

> 2. **Coders commonly use AI to build user-facing apps.** Web-development languages such as JavaScript and HTML were the most common programming languages used in our dataset, and user interface and user experience tasks were among the top coding uses. *This suggests that jobs that center on making simple applications and user interfaces may face disruption from AI systems sooner than those focused purely on backend work*.

Body version, verbatim, the five paragraphs of "What are developers building with Claude?" (the superscript footnote marker on HTML is shown as "[2]"):

> Overall, we found that developers commonly use Claude for building user interfaces and interactive elements for websites and mobile applications. Although no single language dominated, the primarily web-focused development languages of JavaScript and TypeScript together accounted for 31% of all queries, and HTML[2] and CSS (other languages for user-facing code) together added another 28%.

> Back-end development languages (used for behind-the-scenes logic, databases, and infrastructure, as well as API and AI development) were also represented: notably, Python was at 14% of queries. However, Python serves dual purposes—both for back-end development and data analysis. Combined with SQL (another data-focused language, making up 6% of queries), these languages likely included many data science and analytics applications beyond traditional back-end development.

> These patterns further extend to the types of common coding tasks involving Claude. Two of the top five tasks were focused on user-facing app development: "UI/UX Component Development" and "Web & Mobile App Development" each accounted for 12% and 8% of conversations, respectively. Such tasks increasingly lend themselves to a phenomenon known as "vibe coding"—where developers of varying levels of experience describe their desired outcomes in natural language and let AI take the wheel on implementation details.

> Conversations that related to more generic uses, such as "Software Architecture & Code Design" and "Debug and Performance Optimization" were also highly represented in both Claude.ai and Claude Code.

> Speculatively, these findings suggest that jobs that center on making simple applications and user interfaces might face earlier disruption from AI systems if increasing capabilities cause "vibe coding" to shift more into mainstream workflows. As AI increasingly handles component creation and styling tasks, these developers might shift toward higher-level design and user experience work.

**Annotation.**

- **The concession comes first, in a subordinate clause.** "Although no single language dominated, the primarily web-focused development languages of JavaScript and TypeScript together accounted for 31% of all queries". The reading the numbers might invite — one language runs away with it — is refused before the numbers appear, in the same sentence, in a position that cannot be quoted away. The September report uses the identical construction ("While computer and mathematical tasks still dominate..."), which makes this a house device: **put the thing your number is not in the subordinate clause of the sentence that gives the number.**
- **Aggregation is the argument.** The finding is not "JavaScript is 16%, TypeScript 15%, HTML 15%, CSS 13%"; it is 31% and 28%, grouped by what the language is *for*, with the grouping justified inline ("primarily web-focused development languages", "other languages for user-facing code"). Two numbers instead of four, each carrying a category definition. When the claim is about a category, the sentence reports the category total and defines the category in the same breath.
- **The caveat on a specific number lives in a footnote attached to that number.** The "[2]" sits on HTML, and footnote 2 reads: "The HTML numbers for Claude.ai are likely inflated slightly because Artifacts leverage HTML. While we filter out Artifacts that are unrelated to coding, we don't explicitly filter out Artifacts that contain coding-related content from the analysis because significant coding usage happens within Artifacts." Direction of bias ("inflated"), magnitude ("slightly"), mechanism (Artifacts are HTML), and the processing decision with its reason. Four things in two sentences, placed where the number is rather than in the limitations list.
- **The caveat that changes the interpretation rather than the number.** "However, Python serves dual purposes—both for back-end development and data analysis. Combined with SQL ..., these languages likely included many data science and analytics applications beyond traditional back-end development." The 14% is not withdrawn; the label "back-end" is. "likely included" marks the reallocation as inference. This is the move to copy whenever a measured category is broader than the construct it is standing in for: keep the number, shrink the claim.
- **Unit drift inside one section.** The language shares are "of all queries" (31%, 28%, 14%, 6%); the task shares are "of conversations" (12%, 8%). "Interactions" is the unit in the opening and in the subtype sentences, and footnote 1 adds "sessions" for Claude Code. Four nouns for the denominator across the report, never reconciled. Recorded as a writing fault worth avoiding absolutely: **one denominator, one noun, defined once.**
- **Rank without magnitude, twice.** "Two of the top five tasks were focused on user-facing app development" and "were also highly represented in both Claude.ai and Claude Code" — the first supplies its magnitudes in the next clause, the second never does. The fourth paragraph is the only finding paragraph in the report with no number in it at all; it exists to stop the reader concluding that Claude is used only for front-end work, and it does that job on the strength of "also highly represented" alone.
- **Observed versus conjectured, and the hedge stack.** The conjecture is quarantined in a paragraph of its own and flagged by its first word: "**Speculatively**, these findings suggest that jobs ... **might** face earlier disruption from AI systems **if** increasing capabilities cause 'vibe coding' to shift more into mainstream workflows." Adverb at the front, "suggest", "might", and a stated antecedent the reader can check later. Note that this is *more* hedged than the summary bullet's version of the same conjecture ("may face disruption ... sooner"), which has no conditional clause: hedging increases with depth, as precision does in the February report. The second sentence adds a consequence for the affected workers ("these developers might shift toward higher-level design and user experience work") — an inference about who is affected, kept modal, offered without evidence and marked as offered.
- **"Vibe coding" is defined where it is introduced** — "where developers of varying levels of experience describe their desired outcomes in natural language and let AI take the wheel on implementation details" — and then reused in the conjecture as a named mechanism. A borrowed term is glossed once, in an em-dash apposition, and thereafter treated as defined.
- **Claude versus AI.** "developers commonly use Claude for building user interfaces"; "the types of common coding tasks involving Claude"; "both Claude.ai and Claude Code" — Claude wherever the sentence is measured. "let AI take the wheel", "disruption from AI systems", "As AI increasingly handles component creation" — AI wherever the sentence is about the phenomenon or the future. The summary bullet's bold line says "use AI to build user-facing apps" while the body's first sentence says "use Claude for building user interfaces": the same finding, AI in the headline, Claude in the measurement.

### Finding 3 — who is using Claude for coding

Summary version, verbatim:

> 3. **Startups are the main early adopters of Claude Code, while enterprises lag behind.** In a preliminary analysis, we estimated that 33% of conversations on Claude Code served startup-related work, compared to only 13% identified as enterprise-relevant applications. *The adoption gap suggests a divide between nimbler organizations using cutting-edge AI tools, and traditional enterprises.*

Body version, verbatim, the four paragraphs of "Who is using Claude for coding?" (footnote marker shown as "[3]"):

> We also analyzed which groups of developers might be using Claude. We used our analysis system to identify the type of project (e.g. a personal project vs. a project done for a startup) that best described users' coding-related interactions. Because we don't know the real-world context in which Claude's responses were being used, these analyses rely on uncertain inferences from incomplete data. We therefore treat these findings as more preliminary than the ones described above.

> Startups appear to be the primary early adopters of Claude Code, and enterprise adoption lags behind. Startup work accounted for 32.9% of Claude Code conversations (nearly 20% higher than their Claude.ai usage), whereas enterprise work represented only 23.8% of Claude Code conversations (slightly below their 25.9% share on Claude.ai[3]).

> In addition, uses involving students, academics, personal project builders, and tutorial/learning users collectively represent half of the interactions across both platforms. In other words, individuals—not just businesses—are significant adopters of coding assistance tools.

> These adoption patterns mirror past technology shifts, where startups use new tools for competitive advantage while established organizations move more cautiously and often have detailed security checks in place before adopting new tools company-wide. AI's general-purpose nature could accelerate this dynamic: If AI agents provide significant productivity gains, the gap between early and late adopters could translate into substantial competitive advantages.

**Annotation.**

- **The caveat is the section's opening paragraph, and it downgrades the whole section.** "Because we don't know the real-world context in which Claude's responses were being used, these analyses rely on uncertain inferences from incomplete data. We therefore treat these findings as more preliminary than the ones described above." No number has appeared yet. This is the most distinctive structural move on the page: a **section-level downgrade written as a preamble**, giving the reason ("we don't know the real-world context"), the consequence ("uncertain inferences from incomplete data") and the resulting status relative to the rest of the post ("more preliminary than the ones described above"). It is better than a bullet in a list because it is unavoidable, and better than a parenthetical because it ranks this finding against the others. Copy this wherever a post carries findings of unequal strength.
- **The evidentiary verb appears in the first sentence too.** "which groups of developers **might** be using Claude" — the hedge is in the statement of what was analysed, not only in the results.
- **Provenance before result.** The classifier and its unit are named, with an example of the categories, before any share is given: "We used our analysis system to identify the type of project (e.g. a personal project vs. a project done for a startup) that best described users' coding-related interactions." Same ordering as the February report's O\*NET-salary paragraph.
- **"appear to be" carries the headline.** "Startups **appear to be** the primary early adopters of Claude Code, and enterprise adoption lags behind." The topic sentence of the strongest paragraph in the section is hedged in its main verb, and the following sentence supplies the numbers unhedged. Hedge the interpretation, not the measurement.
- **Every number is paired, and the comparator is in parentheses.** "32.9% of Claude Code conversations (nearly 20% higher than their Claude.ai usage)"; "only 23.8% of Claude Code conversations (slightly below their 25.9% share on Claude.ai)". The parenthesis is where the other surface goes, so each sentence carries the comparison rather than requiring the reader to hold two sentences at once. "only" and "slightly below" are the interpretations, inside the clauses.
- **A comparison whose units are not stated.** "nearly 20% higher than their Claude.ai usage" — the sentence does not say whether the 20% is percentage points or a relative increase, and the Claude.ai startup share it is measured against is never given in prose. This is the one comparison in the report a reader cannot reconstruct from the page. The lesson is a rule we already hold and this is the clean illustration of why: **a difference is reported in its units, and both levels are given.**
- **The directional bias on the enterprise number is in a footnote at the number.** Footnote 3: "Claude.ai usage does not include Claude For Work (Team and Enterprise plans) usage, which implies that enterprise numbers for Claude.ai specifically are likely undercounted because a significant amount of enterprise usage on Claude.ai occurs within the Claude For Work product." Which number ("for Claude.ai specifically"), which direction ("undercounted"), and the mechanism — attached to the one comparison it threatens, which is exactly where the February report's memo example says a limitation should sit.
- **The restatement in the reader's register.** "In other words, individuals—not just businesses—are significant adopters of coding assistance tools." The share is "half of the interactions across both platforms" — a fraction in words, not a percentage — and the "In other words" sentence converts a four-category sum into the distinction a reader cares about. Same device as February's "That is," sentence; it is used here to *raise* the salience of a number rather than to deflate it, and the category list that produces the half ("students, academics, personal project builders, and tutorial/learning users") is enumerated in the sentence immediately before, so the reader can see what was added up.
- **Observed versus conjectured.** The fourth paragraph is entirely conjecture and says so by its construction rather than by an adverb: an analogy offered without evidence ("These adoption patterns mirror past technology shifts, where ..."), then a conditional whose antecedent is unmeasured ("**If** AI agents provide significant productivity gains, the gap between early and late adopters **could** translate into substantial competitive advantages"). The mechanism attributed to established organisations — cautious adoption, "detailed security checks" — is asserted about the world, not observed in the data, and nothing in the paragraph is presented as a result.
- **Claude versus AI.** Measurement says Claude throughout ("using Claude", "Claude's responses", "Claude Code conversations", "their 25.9% share on Claude.ai"). The conjecture says AI ("AI's general-purpose nature", "If AI agents provide"), and the summary bullet's italic says "cutting-edge AI tools". The generalisation from one product to a class happens only in sentences that are marked as conjecture.

### Finding 4 — the appendix comparison (software versus non-software)

Verbatim, the appendix:

> As a supplementary analysis, we also compared our results for software-related automation and augmentation patterns to patterns in interactions that did not involve software. We conducted this analysis exclusively in Claude.ai, because Claude Code specializes in software applications.

> Compared to use cases that don't involve software, software development is more automative. A significant increase in Feedback Loops (+18.3%) drives this and, notably, offsets a clear *decrease* in Directive behaviors (-11.2%). In other words, AI-assisted coding currently requires a lot of human reviewing and iteration relative to non-coding tasks, even when Claude does the bulk of the work.

**Annotation.**

- **The scope restriction is stated with its reason, before the result.** "We conducted this analysis exclusively in Claude.ai, because Claude Code specializes in software applications." One sentence gives the restriction and the rationale, and it pre-empts the obvious objection (why not compare on the agent too?) by answering it.
- **Signed differences, not levels.** "(+18.3%)" and "(-11.2%)" are reported as changes with no base and no pair of levels, and the sign convention (software minus non-software) is implied by the sentence's word order rather than stated. Whether these are percentage points or relative changes is not said. The corpus lesson stands: a delta is reported in its units and beside its levels.
- **The italic marks the counter-direction.** "offsets a clear *decrease* in Directive behaviors" — the only italic outside the summary bullets, used to flag that one component moves against the headline. The word "offsets" does the analytical work: the composition of the automation share changes even as its level rises, and that is the actual finding.
- **"significant" is used colloquially.** "A significant increase in Feedback Loops (+18.3%)" — no test, no interval, no n anywhere in the report. In our posts the word is reserved for a stated test or not used.
- **The "In other words" gloss carries the interpretation and its own concession.** "AI-assisted coding currently requires a lot of human reviewing and iteration relative to non-coding tasks, even when Claude does the bulk of the work." "currently" is a temporal fence, "relative to non-coding tasks" keeps the comparison attached, and the subordinate clause concedes the opposite reading in the same sentence. Third instance of the restatement device on this page, and the most useful one, because the restated version is what a reader will remember.

**Naming discipline across all four findings.** The title says AI; the meta description says Claude; every measured sentence says Claude, Claude Code or Claude.ai; every definitional clause says AI ("where AI directly performs tasks"); every forward-looking or conjectural sentence says AI. Where a sentence contains both, the measured half takes Claude and the interpretive half takes AI, in that order: "AI-assisted coding currently requires a lot of human reviewing and iteration ..., even when **Claude** does the bulk of the work."

## Comparisons

The whole report is one comparison run four ways. Claude Code against Claude.ai is the spine; software against non-software is the appendix; the two prior reports are the premise; past technology shifts are the analogy.

### The design comparison, set up in the opening

> To understand these changes in more detail, we conducted an analysis of 500,000 coding-related interactions across Claude.ai (the "default" way that most people interact with Claude) and Claude Code (our new specialist coding "agent" that can independently accomplish chains of complex tasks using a variety of digital tools).

**Annotation.** Both arms defined in one sentence, each in its own parenthesis, with the contrast built into the glosses rather than asserted: "default" against "specialist", "the way most people interact" against "independently accomplish chains of complex tasks". The quotation marks around "default" and "agent" concede that both words are loose. No claim is made yet; the sentence's whole job is to make the later pairs legible. **A comparison that carries a post is defined before the first finding, in the sentence that introduces the data.**

### The headline pair

> Here, we found that Claude Code showed dramatically higher automation rates—79% of conversations involved some form of automation, compared to 49% on Claude.ai.

**Annotation.** Interpretation, em dash, both levels, comparator named. The em dash rather than a colon keeps it one sentence, so the 79% is never available without the 49%. The summary version uses a different connective for the same shape — "In contrast, only 49% of Claude.ai conversations were classified as automation" — and adds "only", which the body version drops. The body is the more neutral of the two.

### The subtype pairs

> "Feedback Loop" patterns, where Claude completes tasks autonomously but with help of human validation (for example, where the user sends any errors back to Claude), were nearly twice as common on Claude Code (35.8% of interactions) as Claude.ai (21.3%). "Directive" conversations, where Claude completed a task with minimal user interaction, were also higher on Claude Code (43.8%, versus 27.5% on Claude.ai). All the patterns of augmentation—including "Learning," where the user acquires knowledge from the AI model—were substantially lower on Claude Code than on Claude.ai.

**Annotation.** Three comparisons, three grammars: a ratio in words with both levels; a direction with both levels; a direction with no levels at all. Each subtype is glossed at the point where its number is given, so the reader never has to hold a taxonomy in mind — the definition arrives with the comparison every time. The third sentence shows what a comparison looks like when its numbers stay in the figure: "substantially lower" is the entire quantitative content, and a reader cannot tell whether the augmentation gap is 5 points or 25.

### The project-type pairs

> Startup work accounted for 32.9% of Claude Code conversations (nearly 20% higher than their Claude.ai usage), whereas enterprise work represented only 23.8% of Claude Code conversations (slightly below their 25.9% share on Claude.ai).

**Annotation.** "whereas" carries a comparison of comparisons: the startup gap is positive and the enterprise gap is negative, and the sentence's shape is what makes the divergence visible. Both halves keep the same subject-first order (Claude Code level, then the Claude.ai comparator in parentheses), so the reader reads two differences without re-orienting. The second parenthesis gives both levels; the first gives only the difference. Inconsistent, and the inconsistent half is the one a reader cannot check.

### The comparison delegated to the figure

> *The distance between the dots indicates the gap in the prevalence of each type of project on Claude.ai (blue) and Claude Code (orange).*

**Annotation.** A dumbbell chart whose caption names the gap as the encoded quantity — the visual channel *is* the comparison, and the caption says so in one clause before naming the colour key. Compare the February report's usage-against-workforce-share chart, whose caption named two series and left the reader to see the gap; this caption names the gap first and the series second. The stronger form when the finding is a difference.

### What makes the pair legitimate, stated twice in captions and once in a footnote

> *Percentages represent total percentages of coding-related tasks across both platforms. Because Claude Code and Claude.ai are equally weighted, the portions of the bars that correspond to each of the platforms represent half of that platform's usage.*

> To account for the filter, we renormalized analyses to equally weight Claude Code and Claude.ai interactions, where applicable.

**Annotation.** The weighting rule that makes every pooled number a 50/50 average of the two surfaces is disclosed in footnote 1 and restated, in near-identical words, under both of the pooled-share figures. That is the right instinct — the rule is repeated wherever a number depends on it, rather than stated once and forgotten — and it is the one place on the page where a caption carries a load-bearing method sentence. What is *not* in either caption or the footnote is any statement of what the unweighted or usage-weighted shares would be, so a reader cannot tell how much of "JavaScript and TypeScript ... 31%" is about developers and how much about the weighting.

### The comparison that is the premise, not the finding

> In our previous Economic Index research, we found very disproportionate use of Claude by US workers in computer-related occupations: that is, there were many more conversations with Claude about computer-related tasks than one would predict from the number of people working in relevant jobs. It's the same in the educational context: Computer Science degrees—which involve large amounts of coding—show highly disproportionate AI use.

**Annotation.** Two prior comparisons — conversation share against employment share, and course share against enrolment — restated in words with no numbers and hyperlinked to their sources, purely to license the choice of population. The "that is," clause restates the technical comparison in plain terms in the same sentence it is made. "It's the same in the educational context" is the cheapest possible corroboration: one independent setting, same direction, one sentence. **A premise inherited from prior work is stated as a comparison, in words, with a link, and without a number that the new post would then have to own.**

### The analogy

> These adoption patterns mirror past technology shifts, where startups use new tools for competitive advantage while established organizations move more cautiously and often have detailed security checks in place before adopting new tools company-wide.

**Annotation.** A comparison to unnamed prior episodes, with no citation and no example — weaker than the February report's Spinning-Jenny-to-car-robots lineage, which named its two extremes and thereby told the reader which literature it was standing in. "mirror" asserts the resemblance rather than demonstrating it, and the clause after "where" is a description of firm behaviour presented as common knowledge. This is the comparison in the report that would be cut first.

### The third arm

> Compared to use cases that don't involve software, software development is more automative.

**Annotation.** A within-Claude.ai comparison that supplies the missing benchmark: without it, 49% automation on Claude.ai has nothing to be high or low against. The report runs this comparison and then puts it in an appendix after the close. **A benchmark that tells the reader whether the headline is large belongs beside the headline, not after the conclusion.**

## Limitations

Verbatim, the whole section:

> ### Limitations

> Our analysis is grounded in real-world AI use—how developers are actually using Claude in their workflows. Although this approach gives our findings practical relevance, it also brings inherent limitations. These include:

> - We analyzed data from Claude.ai and Claude Code only. We excluded Team, Enterprise, and API usage that might show different patterns, particularly in professional settings;
> - The boundary between automation and augmentation becomes increasingly blurred with agentic tools like Claude Code. For example, the "Feedback Loop" pattern differs qualitatively from traditional automation, because it still requires user supervision and input. We will likely need to extend the automation/augmentation framework to account for new agentic capabilities;
> - Our categorization of who is using Claude for coding relied on inference from limited context. When categorizing conversations as "startup" versus "enterprise" work, or "personal" versus "academic" projects, our analysis tool made educated guesses based on incomplete information. Some classifications might therefore be incorrect. Additionally, we included an option for 'Could Not Classify', which Claude opted for in 5% of Claude.ai conversations and 2% of Claude Code conversations. We excluded this category from analysis and renormalized the results;
> - Our dataset likely captures early adopters. These users might not represent the broader developer population, and this self-selection could skew usage patterns towards more experienced or technically adventurous users;
> - Due to privacy considerations, we only analyzed data within a specific retention window, potentially missing cyclical patterns in software development (such as sprint cycles or release schedules);
> - The representativeness of Claude usage is unclear, relative to overall AI coding assistance adoption. Many developers use multiple AI tools beyond Claude, meaning we present only a partial view of their AI engagement patterns;
> - We only studied what developers delegate to AI—not how they ultimately use AI outputs in their codebase, the quality of the resulting code, or whether these interactions effectively improved productivity or code quality.

**Annotation.**

- **Where it sits.** Its own section, at the same heading level as every finding, placed after the last finding and before the close — so "Looking ahead" is read through it. The heading is "Limitations", not February's conversational "Caveats", and the promotion out of the results is a change in the series' practice that our template follows.
- **The caveats list is unusually long, and its length is not in this list.** The report's caveat apparatus runs on **three layers**, and the allocation between them is consistent enough to be a rule:
  1. **Design-level limits → the seven-bullet list.** Coverage, construct validity, classifier reliability, sample selection, time window, external validity, and outcomes not measured. Nothing in the list attaches to a single number.
  2. **Finding-level downgrades → a preamble or a clause inside the finding.** "We therefore treat these findings as more preliminary than the ones described above" (head of "Who is using Claude for coding?"); "In a preliminary analysis, we estimated that" (summary bullet 3); "However, Python serves dual purposes"; "But it's by no means certain that this pattern will persist"; "Speculatively"; and in the close, "Although we can't assume that the lessons we draw from software development will directly carry over to other types of occupation".
  3. **Number-level bias statements → footnotes at the number.** Footnote 2 on HTML being "likely inflated slightly" by Artifacts; footnote 3 on Claude.ai enterprise numbers being "likely undercounted" because of Claude For Work. Both give a direction.
  A reader who reads only the Limitations section gets the design; a reader who reads the findings gets the downgrades; a reader who follows the superscripts gets the biases. **The rule: a caveat that applies to one number goes at that number; a caveat that applies to one finding goes at the head of that finding; only design-level limits go in the list.** That is why a report with seven bullets carries more qualification than the February report's six.
- **The preamble derives the limitation from the design's virtue.** "Our analysis is grounded in real-world AI use—how developers are actually using Claude in their workflows. Although this approach gives our findings practical relevance, it also brings inherent limitations." No "as with all studies". The concession is specific: observational data on real use buys relevance and costs control, and every bullet that follows is a cost of that same choice. This is the stronger form of the ritual sentence and should replace it. "These include:" still concedes non-exhaustiveness, as February's "Some of these include" did; a referee would still ask what was left out.
- **How the list is ordered.** Coverage of the sample (1) → validity of the central construct (2) → reliability of the secondary classifier (3) → who is in the sample (4) → when the sample is from (5) → what the sample represents (6) → what was not measured at all (7). Roughly outward from the data to the world, ending on the widest. The strongest bullet is last, as in February.
- **How specific.** Six of seven name a mechanism; five name a direction or a consequence. Bullet 1 enumerates the exclusions individually (Team, Enterprise, API) and says where they would bite ("particularly in professional settings"). Bullet 2 names the construct, the offending category, and *why* it offends ("because it still requires user supervision and input"). Bullet 4 names the selection mechanism and the direction of the skew ("towards more experienced or technically adventurous users"). Bullet 5 names two concrete cyclical patterns. Bullet 6 names the competing tools problem and what the report is therefore a view of ("only a partial view of their AI engagement patterns").
- **Bullet 2 is a limitation that commits to future work.** "We will likely need to extend the automation/augmentation framework to account for new agentic capabilities." It concedes that the framework carrying the report's headline is strained by the very surface the report is about, and discharges the concession as a methodological promise rather than a withdrawal. Note that this is the *only* commitment anywhere in the report — the close makes none — and it sits in the limitations list.
- **Bullet 3 is a limitation that discloses a processing decision, with its numbers.** "we included an option for 'Could Not Classify', which Claude opted for in 5% of Claude.ai conversations and 2% of Claude Code conversations. We excluded this category from analysis and renormalized the results." The only numbers in the section, and they are about the analysis rather than the world. Stating the size of a dropped category and the handling of the remainder, inside the limitation, is exactly right; what is not said is what the excluded conversations looked like.
- **Bullet 7 withdraws four claims in one sentence.** "We only studied what developers delegate to AI—not how they ultimately use AI outputs in their codebase, the quality of the resulting code, or whether these interactions effectively improved productivity or code quality." Three named non-findings after a dash. It is this report's equivalent of February's "we don't argue that the uses in our dataset are a representative sample", and it is the construction to copy: **a limitation is worth most when it names the sentence the post is not entitled to write.**
- **Punctuation.** Six bullets end in semicolons, the seventh in a full stop: a proper series, unlike the February report's mixed list. A small tell that this list was written as a list.
- **What a referee would still raise first.** No n per cell and no per-surface n anywhere on the page (footnote 1 gives only "split evenly"); no uncertainty of any kind, so no interval on any pair and no minimum detectable effect beside "All the patterns of augmentation ... were substantially lower"; no accuracy, agreement or audit statistic for either classifier, including the coding-relevance filter that defines the entire Claude.ai arm; and the bullets are not ranked by severity, so the construct-validity problem in bullet 2 — which threatens the headline directly — sits level with the retention window.
- **What is not in the list, and is elsewhere.** The internal disagreement between the summary's 13% and the body's 23.8% enterprise share is nowhere on the page, in any layer. A three-layer caveat apparatus does not catch an inconsistency between two statements of the same finding; only a single source for every number does.

## Close

Verbatim, the four paragraphs of "Looking ahead":

> ### Looking ahead

> AI is fundamentally changing the ways developers work. Our analysis implies that this is particularly true where specialist agentic systems like Claude Code are used, is particularly strong for user-facing app development work, and might be giving particular advantages to startups as opposed to more established business enterprises.

> Our findings raise many questions. Will the prevalence of "feedback loops," where humans are still involved in the process, persist as AI capabilities advance, or will we see a shift toward more complete automation? As AI systems become capable of building larger-scale pieces of software, will developers shift to mostly managing and guiding these systems, rather than writing code themselves? Which software development roles will change the most, and which might disappear entirely?

> The increasing coding skills of AI might also be especially consequential for AI development itself. Since so much of AI research and development relies on software, it's possible that advancements in AI-assisted coding help to speed up breakthroughs, creating a positively-reinforcing cycle that accelerates AI progress even further.

> In the grand scheme of things, AI systems are extremely new. But in a relative sense, coding is among the most developed uses of AI in the economy. That makes it worth watching. Although we can't assume that the lessons we draw from software development will directly carry over to other types of occupation, software development might be a leading indicator that gives us useful information about how other occupations might change with the rollout of increasingly capable AI models in the future.

**Annotation.**

- **What was learned, in one sentence, with no numbers.** All three findings are restated in a single sentence of three parallel predicates — "is particularly true where specialist agentic systems like Claude Code are used, is particularly strong for user-facing app development work, and might be giving particular advantages to startups" — with the modal weakening across the list in the order of the findings' own strength. Findings 1 and 2 get "is"; finding 3, the one the report called preliminary, gets "might be giving". The close ranks its findings by the same ordering the sections established. No number appears anywhere in the close, exactly as in February: **the close states what was learned in words and leaves the numbers in the findings.**
- **What comes next is a set of questions, not a set of commitments.** "Our findings raise many questions", then three, all open, none answered, each naming an object the series could measure: whether feedback loops persist, whether developers shift to managing rather than writing, which roles change and which disappear. This is the reverse of the February close, which promised specific repeated measurements ("we'll repeat many of the analyses above", "we'll be able to monitor changes in the depth of AI use"). Here the only commitment on the page is in limitations bullet 2. Worth noting as a genre difference: a wave report promises the next wave; a special report hands the questions on. Our template asks for "what comes next" — the honest version of this close is questions *plus* the one thing the post commits to.
- **Why it matters is the last sentence, and it is hedged front-loaded.** "Although we can't assume that the lessons we draw from software development will directly carry over to other types of occupation, software development might be a leading indicator that gives us useful information about how other occupations might change with the rollout of increasingly capable AI models in the future." The caveat is the subordinate clause and comes first; the claim is the main clause and comes second; "might be" and "might change" keep both ends modal. The report therefore ends on a hedged claim rather than on either a bare claim or a bare caveat, and this is the sentence the whole piece exists to earn: a small sector matters because it is early. Read against the opening — "a small sector of the modern economy, but an influential one" — the close discharges the first sentence exactly. **The opening's concession and the closing's claim are the same argument, twenty-two hundred words apart.**
- **The three-beat scaling move before it.** "In the grand scheme of things, AI systems are extremely new. But in a relative sense, coding is among the most developed uses of AI in the economy. That makes it worth watching." Zoom out, zoom back in, state the consequence in five words. The short third sentence does the work of a paragraph.
- **The one conjecture with no data behind it is marked twice and given its own paragraph.** "The increasing coding skills of AI **might** also be especially consequential for AI development itself. Since so much of AI research and development relies on software, **it's possible that** advancements in AI-assisted coding help to speed up breakthroughs, creating a positively-reinforcing cycle that accelerates AI progress even further." The most dramatic claim available to this report — AI accelerating AI — is quarantined in its own paragraph, prefixed by its premise ("Since so much of AI research and development relies on software"), and double-hedged. It is not in the summary bullets, not in any finding, and not in the last paragraph.
- **No recommendation, and no refusal either.** The February close made a point of declining to prescribe policy. This one does not raise the question: there is no policy sentence, no recommendation to any actor, and no statement of what the findings are not for. Our template requires recommendations; nothing in this close models them, so the closest model in the corpus remains February's explicit refusal plus displaced invitation.
- **First person, and what it is used for.** "Our analysis implies", "Our findings raise", "we see", "we can't assume", "the lessons we draw", "gives us useful information". Unlike the February report, where first person was reserved for institutional acts, here it also carries *interpretation* ("Our analysis implies", "the lessons we draw"). House style forbids it. The substitute for "Our analysis implies that this is particularly true where ..." is to attribute the implication to the evidence rather than to the authors, and the substitute for "we can't assume" is to state the limit of transfer as a property of the finding.
- **Title against ending.** Title: "Anthropic Economic Index: AI's impact on software development" — series name, then AI, then a sector. Ending: software development as a leading indicator for other occupations. The match is by *scope* rather than by finding: the title promises an impact assessment of one sector and the close explains why that sector was the one to assess. Neither the 79% nor Claude Code is in the title, and neither is in the close — the post's three findings are the middle of the piece, and its frame is the sector's informativeness. For a post whose title names a finding, the inverse holds: the close must end on that finding's consequence.
- **Two things follow the close.** "Work With Us" (boilerplate, and the only signal of who wrote the report) and then the Appendix, which contains a fourth finding with numbers. Ending a piece and then adding a result after it costs the result its readers.
