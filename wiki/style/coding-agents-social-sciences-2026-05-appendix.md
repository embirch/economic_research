# coding-agents-social-sciences-2026-05-appendix — style annotation

## Source

- **Title on the cover:** "Appendix: Coding Agents in the Social Sciences" — set in three lines of large serif under the ANTHROP\C wordmark. Title case, with a colon after the genre word. Note the construction against the Economic Index appendices, which title themselves `Appendix to "…"` with the parent's title inside quotation marks: here the appendix carries the parent's *title* directly, and only the leading word "Appendix:" distinguishes the two documents. A reader holding this file alone cannot tell which piece it appends to except by matching titles.
- **Date and byline on the cover:** two labelled fields side by side, "**Published** / May 27, 2026" and "**Authors** / Thomas Lyttelton (MIT), Maxim Massenkoff, and Nathan Wilmers (MIT)". This is the first appendix in the corpus with a byline. Affiliations are parenthesised for the two external authors and absent for the third, so institutional membership is signalled by omission. No corresponding-author address, no acknowledgements, no funding note, no "how to cite".
- **URL fetched:** https://cdn.sanity.io/files/4zrzovbb/website/403415e54964751190003985896630e56829e797.pdf — 6 pages. The same URL is the appendix's only published location; no second build was found.
- **Parent piece:** "Coding agents in the social sciences", slug `coding-agents-social-sciences-2026-05` (https://www.anthropic.com/research/coding-agents-social-sciences). Not fetched for this file; its style entry is a separate slug. The appendix names no URL for it and never names its title in prose — the link is made by two bare cross-references, "Figure 5b" and "Figure 5", and by the shared cover title.
- **Document type:** survey methodology appendix. It carries the sampling frame, the recruitment ladder, the variable constructions, one estimating equation, one unadjusted-results figure and one selection check. It states no headline finding of its own; the two results it does report are the ones the parent report chose not to show.
- **Production:** PDF producer "Skia/PDF m150 Google Docs Renderer" — a Google Doc printed to PDF, not an InDesign layout. The consequences are visible throughout and are worth recording, because they shape the register: body text in Lora, headings in Poppins SemiBold, the equation in Cambria Math, page numbers bottom-right, **no running footer of any kind**, no cover rule, no section numbering, no footnotes, and not a single hyperlink in the file. Where the Economic Index appendices are typeset objects with furniture that tells a detached reader what they belong to, this one is a document.
- **Length:** 1,205 words of extractable text across six pages, of which roughly 1,050 are body prose. Two headings, one displayed equation, one figure, two tables, five paragraphs in the first section and four in the second.
- **Audience:** a referee, not a reader. Every paragraph answers a question someone would ask about the survey — who was invited, who was let in, how the discipline variable was made, what the regression was, what the raw numbers look like, and whether the sample is selected. Nothing is explained for interest's sake and there is no sentence anywhere about why the study matters.

## Section order

Headings in order, with what each does and where it runs. There are only two.

1. **Cover (p. 1)** — wordmark, title, then the Published/Authors pair. ~21 words. No abstract, no contents, no summary of the parent, no date of the survey (that is the first line of p. 2).
2. **"Data description" (pp. 2–5)** — the whole of the method, in five paragraphs and three exhibits, in this internal order:
   - *Frame and recruitment* (p. 2, one paragraph): field dates, then the five recruitment channels.
   - *Eligibility and consent* (p. 2, one paragraph): the targeting rule, the screener, what respondents were told, the incentive, and — as the paragraph's last clause — the analytic n with a pointer to Table A1.
   - Table A1 (p. 3), **caption above the table**.
   - *Variable construction* (pp. 3–4, three paragraphs, one per variable): discipline, gender, institution rank.
   - *The estimating equation* (p. 4): a one-line lead-in, the displayed equation, then one paragraph unpacking every term.
   - *Why the raw numbers are not the headline* (p. 4, one paragraph), then Figure A1 (p. 5) with its **caption below**.
3. **"Pilot sample" (pp. 5–6)** — the selection check, in four paragraphs: the question; why the pilot is a valid comparison; what the comparison shows; and Table A2 with a *Notes* line. The section ends with the table and its notes and nothing else.
4. **No third section.** No conclusion, no references, no appendix-to-the-appendix, no data availability statement, no instrument. The questionnaire is not reproduced anywhere.

**What the order tells you.** The document is built as *frame → sample → variables → estimator → unadjusted result → threat to validity*. That is the order a referee reads in, and the last section is the one a referee would reach for first, placed last because it is the concession. Compare the Economic Index appendices, whose order is *sample → vocabulary → instrument → output*: those hand over the machinery and stop, while this one ends by arguing that its own sample is usable. A survey appendix has a defendant's job that a log-analysis appendix does not.

**Where exhibits sit relative to prose.** Consistently: the prose that introduces an exhibit is always before it, and every exhibit is named in prose before it appears ("described by discipline and career stage in Table A1", "shown in Figure A1", "Table A2 shows results comparing…"). No exhibit in the document is unannounced, and no exhibit is discussed after the fact. The placement of the caption differs by exhibit type — **above for tables, below for the figure** — which is a convention held consistently across both tables and worth copying, because it lets a table be read top-down in one pass.

**What is shown and what is not.** Shown in full: field dates, the five recruitment channels, the exact screener criteria, the exact wording of what respondents were told about the experiment, the incentive, the n, the discipline consolidation rules one by one, the named Python library used for gender, the named ranking used for institutions and all twenty-five institutions in it, the estimating equation with every control enumerated, and the standard-error choice. Not shown anywhere: the questionnaire, any item wording, the response rate against the 44,700 invitations, the wording of the "detailed coding agent question", the definition of "coding agent user", the outcome-variable wording, the expansion of "WFL", and the contents of Figure 5. The rule this document follows is *everything about who is in the sample, nothing about what they were asked.*

## Opening move

Verbatim, the first prose in the document — the heading and the whole of the first paragraph:

> ## Data description
>
> The survey was fielded from February 20 to March 24, 2026. We identified respondents in five ways. First, we scraped faculty and student contact information from sociology, political science, management, economics, psychology, education, and public health department websites at R1 and major Canadian research universities. Second, we used OpenAlex, an open source repository of academic research, to identify research-active social scientists based on publication and working paper topics. We restricted to researchers who had published within the last year, in hopes of identifying active, working researchers. We also used academic conference programs for the same purpose. We directly emailed 44,700 academics we identified in these ways. Additionally, we asked directors of graduate studies in sociology, political science, and economics to distribute invitations to their graduate students, and we distributed invitations via the message boards and email lists of scholarly communities such as the Academy of Management and the American Sociological Association.

**Annotation.**

- **Two sentences of eleven and eight words, then everything else.** "The survey was fielded from February 20 to March 24, 2026" is a date and nothing else — no stakes, no motivation, no restatement of the parent's question, no "this appendix accompanies". "We identified respondents in five ways" is a promise of structure. The document's whole opening apparatus is those nineteen words. This is the genre's opening move and it is the one to copy: **an appendix opens with the fact that dates the evidence, and then with the shape of the section.**
- **The counted list that stops counting.** "in five ways. First… Second… We also used… Additionally… and we distributed…" The enumeration is marked for the first two channels and then abandoned, so the reader must count the remaining three unaided and cannot be certain which clause is channel four and which is channel five. It is the one structural flaw in the paragraph, and it is instructive precisely because the promise ("five ways") is what exposes it. If a sentence announces a count, every member of the count carries its ordinal.
- **Each channel comes with its purpose, in the same sentence.** "in hopes of identifying active, working researchers" is attached to the publication-recency restriction, and "for the same purpose" carries that reason across to the conference programs without repeating it. A frame-construction sentence that says what the filter was *for* can be argued with; one that only says what the filter *was* cannot.
- **Where the first number sits.** Sentence one, as a date range; the first count, "44,700 academics", arrives in the paragraph's sixth sentence and is the denominator of the whole study. It is stated once, in passing, and never used again: the appendix gives the number invited and the number analysed but never puts them in the same sentence, so no response rate appears anywhere. A referee computes it in their head, and our own posts should do the arithmetic on the page.
- **Hedged verbs for the frame's completeness.** "scraped", "used", "restricted to", "directly emailed", "asked… to distribute", "distributed… via" — note the last two. Two of the five channels are *indirect*, and the verbs say so plainly, which means the appendix concedes in its first paragraph that it cannot enumerate who was actually invited. The concession is carried entirely by the choice of verb.
- **Register.** First person plural in every sentence but the first, and only for methodological acts: "we scraped", "we used", "We restricted", "we directly emailed", "we asked", "we distributed". The one agentless sentence is the one about the survey itself ("The survey was fielded"). Our house style forbids the first person; the substitute is the construction the opening sentence already demonstrates — passive with the act named and the date attached — and the reasons stay where they are.
- **Tense discipline.** Past throughout for the fielding of this survey, present only for standing constructions later in the document ("Economics includes its applied subfields"; "We consolidate these into eight substantive discipline buckets"). The two tenses separate what was done once from what is true of the dataset.

## Findings and their caveats

This is a methodology appendix: it reports no finding about the economy, and its two results — the unadjusted output gaps in Figure A1 and the pilot comparison in Table A2 — are presented as diagnostics of the main analysis, not as results in their own right. What follows is how the method is phrased, in the order the document phrases it.

### How recruitment is written

The second paragraph, verbatim and in full:

> Throughout these recruitment efforts, we targeted researchers based in the US or Canada actively doing quantitative, empirical research. In an opening screener for the survey, we further restricted participants to those who work with quantitative empirical data, and, among doctoral students, those who have finished at least 2 years of their training and who plan to enter academia. Invitations were sent by email. Respondents were informed that the study concerns academic workflows and that participants would be eligible for a randomized experiment providing access to Claude Max accounts. They were also offered a $10 gift card for completing the survey. After applying the screener and limiting to respondents who completed the full survey, the analytic sample consists of 1,260 respondents, described by discipline and career stage in Table A1.

**Annotation.** Six sentences, and each one is a different kind of fact about who is in the sample: the targeting rule, the screening rule, the mode, the disclosure, the incentive, the survivorship rule and the resulting n. The paragraph is a funnel written as a list, and the order is the order the funnel ran in.

- **Two filters, named separately.** "we targeted" (a property of the invitation list) and "we further restricted" (a property of the instrument) are kept apart, so the reader can tell which respondents never saw the survey from which respondents saw it and were screened out. "further" is the hinge word. Most write-ups collapse these into one sentence about "the sample"; keeping them apart is what makes the frame auditable.
- **The screener is given as criteria, not as wording.** "those who work with quantitative empirical data", "those who have finished at least 2 years of their training and who plan to enter academia" — the thresholds are exact (two years; plan to enter academia) but the item wording is not shown. The reader can check whether they would qualify and cannot check what the respondent read. For our own posts the screener text goes in the appendix verbatim; this document shows what is lost when it does not.
- **The disclosure sentence is the most important sentence in the appendix and is written flatly.** "Respondents were informed that the study concerns academic workflows and that participants would be eligible for a randomized experiment providing access to Claude Max accounts." Reported speech, passive, no comment. It is a plain statement of what was said to respondents — and it is also the whole mechanism of the selection problem that the "Pilot sample" section exists to address, three pages later. The appendix does not flag the connection here; it states the fact where it belongs chronologically and lets the later section pick it up. That separation is defensible and it is also a cost: a reader who stops after p. 2 reads a recruitment detail where a referee reads a threat to validity. The house treatment is to state the fact where it belongs *and* name its consequence in the same paragraph.
- **The incentive is a separate sentence.** "$10 gift card for completing the survey" — not folded into the disclosure, because it conditions completion rather than enrolment, and the sentence that follows is the completion filter. Adjacency is doing the work of an explanation.
- **The n arrives last and arrives conditioned.** "After applying the screener and limiting to respondents who completed the full survey, the analytic sample consists of 1,260 respondents" — the two conditions precede the number in the same sentence, so the figure cannot be lifted without them. The sentence then hands off to the exhibit ("described by discipline and career stage in Table A1"). This is the cleanest sentence in the document and the pattern to copy exactly: **conditions, then the count, then the exhibit that decomposes it.** What it omits is the count at each stage of the funnel, so the 44,700 and the 1,260 are the only two rungs a reader gets.
- **Naming discipline.** The products appear under their own names where they are the object of a real-world act — "Claude Max accounts" in the disclosure, "Claude Code access" in the selection section, "Claude classification" in the discipline paragraph — while the measured construct is "coding agent user" and the survey's belief items are about "AI". The document therefore says AI for the attitude, Claude for the thing people were given, and "coding agent" for the behaviour it regresses on. That is a three-way split, and it is consistent: **a product name is used only where a product was actually handed over or run.**

### How the variables are constructed

Three paragraphs, one per variable, each built the same way: the raw material, the operation, the rule, the residual.

> Respondents provided their discipline via free text entry. The raw responses contain 318 unique values, many of which are idiosyncratic subfield descriptions. We consolidate these into eight substantive discipline buckets plus a residual "Other" category, using a mix of Claude classification and hand coding. Economics includes its applied subfields (health economics, development economics) and political economy. Political science includes public administration and law. Sociology includes urban studies, geography and social work. Management sciences includes all applied disciplines typically housed in business schools (including finance, accounting, and organizational behavior). Public health includes epidemiology. "Other" is a residual category, including the small number of respondents doing quantitative research in anthropology, archaeology, history and linguistics.

**Annotation.** The paragraph gives the cardinality of the raw variable (318 unique values) before the recode, so the reader knows how much collapsing happened; then it gives the target scheme; then it walks the merge rules one bucket at a time in identical grammar — "*X* includes *Y*" — five times. Only the buckets whose boundaries are contestable get a rule; Psychology, Education and Communication appear in Table A1 and are never defined in prose, which is a silent claim that nobody would draw those boundaries differently. The residual is defined last, by exclusion, and populated with examples rather than left abstract. The pattern to copy is the grammar: **one construction rule per sentence, the same verb each time, the residual last and exemplified.**

Two things the paragraph does not do. It does not say how the work split between "Claude classification and hand coding" — which cases went to which, in what order, whether the hand coding was a check on the model or a fallback for its failures, or who did it. And it does not report any agreement between the two. A free-text variable recoded partly by a model and partly by a person, with no stated division of labour and no reliability check, is the first thing a referee would ask about; the document names the two instruments and stops.

> We infer gender from each respondent's self-reported first name using the gender_guesser Python library. We collapse "male" and "mostly_male" into a single "typically male name" category, and "female" and "mostly_female" into "typically female name." Unknown or androgynous names are left out of the analysis.

**Annotation.** The best-written paragraph in the document, and the shortest. Three sentences: instrument, recode, exclusion. Three things to copy. First, the **named tool** — "the gender_guesser Python library" — which is the only place in the corpus where a construct's implementation is pinned to a package a replicator can install. Second, the **honest label**: the collapsed categories are called "typically male name" and "typically female name", not "male" and "female". The variable is named after what it measures, which is a property of the name, not of the person, and the caveat is therefore carried by the variable name itself in every later use. This is the strongest single move in the document and the one most worth copying: **when a proxy is unavoidable, put the proxy into the name of the variable.** Third, the exclusion is stated as a consequence for the analysis ("are left out of the analysis"), not as a property of the data.

What is missing is a number: how many respondents the exclusion removes. A silent drop whose size is unstated cannot be assessed, and the variable itself appears nowhere else in this document — it is constructed here and never used, so a reader cannot tell what it was for.

> We classify respondents' institutions as top 25 in the US and Canada using the Nature Index 2025 Leading Institutions ranking. 28% of respondents are from the top 25 institutions, which include Harvard University, Stanford University, MIT, National Institutes of Health, University of Michigan, Yale University, University of Pennsylvania, University of Toronto, UCLA, UC Berkeley, Johns Hopkins University, University of Washington, UC San Diego, Columbia University, Cornell University, Northwestern University, University of Chicago, Washington University in St. Louis, Princeton University, Caltech, UC San Francisco, UT Austin, University of Wisconsin-Madison, Duke University, and University of Minnesota.

**Annotation.** An external classification is pinned to a named, dated source ("Nature Index 2025 Leading Institutions ranking") and then *enumerated in full in body prose* — twenty-five names run together in a single sentence, the same presentation choice the June 2026 Economic Index appendix makes with its twenty-one cluster names. The effect is the same in both: the list is complete and unusable. A reader cannot scan it, cannot count it without effort, and cannot tell that "National Institutes of Health" is not a university until they notice. The rule the two cases together suggest is plain: **a membership list of more than about eight items is a table, not a sentence** — but the discipline of publishing it at all is right, because a "top 25" cut with an unpublished membership is not reproducible. Note also that the one share in the paragraph ("28%") is a description of the sample, not a result, and that "which include" hedges the enumeration as partial when it is in fact complete.

### How the regression specification is written

> The adjusted output comparisons in Figure 5b use OLS regressions of the form:

> *Output*ᵢ = β × *CodingAgentUser*ᵢ + γ × *X*ᵢ + ϵᵢ

> where *X*ᵢ includes fixed effects for career stage (five categories: PhD student, postdoc, assistant professor, associate professor, full professor), discipline bucket (nine categories), and the calendar week the respondent completed the survey. Standard errors are heteroskedasticity-robust. The coefficient beta is the adjusted difference in mean output between coding agent users and non-users, conditional on career stage, discipline, and survey timing. We fit this model with OLS, and then divide by the control group's mean of each outcome to back out percent differences. A Poisson model specification gives similar results.

**Annotation.** This is the passage the corpus needs, because it is the only estimating equation in any appendix here. Five features, in order of how much they are worth copying.

1. **The lead-in names the exhibit the equation produced.** "The adjusted output comparisons in Figure 5b use OLS regressions of the form:" — the sentence binds a specific figure in the parent report to a specific specification before the specification appears. A reader of Figure 5b who wants the model knows they are in the right paragraph by the end of the first line. Every equation in our own posts should be introduced by the exhibit it explains, not by its own name.
2. **The variables are named in words, not letters.** *Output*, *CodingAgentUser*, *X*, ϵ — two of the four are readable English and carry their meaning into the equation, so the prose that follows can discuss "the coefficient beta" without a legend. There is no notation table and none is needed.
3. **Every control is enumerated with its cardinality.** "career stage (five categories: PhD student, postdoc, assistant professor, associate professor, full professor), discipline bucket (nine categories), and the calendar week". Career stage gets both the count and the level names; discipline gets the count only, because the levels were given three paragraphs earlier and appear in Table A1; survey week gets neither, because it is self-describing. The rule being followed is *state the count always, state the levels once*. Note that "nine categories" silently reconciles the earlier "eight substantive discipline buckets plus a residual" — the reader has to do the addition, and the two sentences would be better written to agree.
4. **The standard-error choice is a sentence of its own.** "Standard errors are heteroskedasticity-robust." Four words, no justification, no alternative considered, no clustering discussed. Giving it its own sentence rather than a subordinate clause is right; leaving the clustering question unaddressed in a survey with discipline and career-stage strata is the referee's second question.
5. **The transformation from coefficient to reported quantity is spelled out.** "We fit this model with OLS, and then divide by the control group's mean of each outcome to back out percent differences." This is the sentence most write-ups omit, and it is the sentence a reader needs to know what the percentages in the figure *are*: not log points, not a Poisson semi-elasticity, but a level difference rescaled by a base. It also, in passing, exposes the paragraph's one vocabulary slip — "the control group" for what the previous sentence correctly calls "non-users". There is no control group; this is an observational contrast, and the word imports an experiment that has not happened. **A causal noun in a descriptive sentence is the kind of thing the claims list exists to forbid.**

The caveat is the last sentence and it is seven words long: "A Poisson model specification gives similar results." A count-outcome robustness check is asserted, its results are not shown, "similar" is not defined, and no table, appendix row or supplementary file backs it. Compare the construction to the June 2026 appendix's "tend to": both state less than they could, but this one states less than a reader can verify. **Copy the instinct — a robustness check belongs in the specification paragraph, one sentence long — and do not copy the execution: in this house the variant is tabulated and the agreement is quantified.**

### How the unadjusted result is phrased

> The raw differences in output among respondents are more difficult to interpret, as they reflect differences between disciplines in practices like posting working papers, timing of conference submissions, and expected number of journal publications per year. These unadjusted differences are shown in Figure A1 and show that agent users post more working papers and submit more grants, but actually submit fewer papers to journals. However, as Figure 5 shows, this lower rate of submitting to journals reflects the distribution of coding agent use across disciplines and career stages.

**Annotation.** Three sentences with three jobs: the interpretation warning, the description, the resolution — and the warning comes *first*, before the reader is told what the figure shows. That ordering is the whole technique. By the time the awkward result arrives, the reader has already been given the mechanism that will explain it away, and the mechanism was stated in concrete, checkable terms ("practices like posting working papers, timing of conference submissions, and expected number of journal publications per year") rather than as a generic appeal to composition.

Two observations about the prose itself. **No number appears in any of the three sentences.** The directions are given as words — "post more", "submit more", "submit fewer" — and every magnitude is left to the figure's data labels. The prose therefore claims strictly less than the exhibit shows, which is the direction the June 2026 appendix also errs in and the safe one; but the cost here is real, because the unadjusted estimates are the only estimates in the document and none of them is quotable from text. A verification script of the kind this team runs would have nothing to match against.

And "actually" is doing rhetorical work that a flat sentence would not. "but actually submit fewer papers to journals" signals surprise, which is a reading of the result rather than a statement of it; then "However, as Figure 5 shows" resolves the surprise by pointing outside the document. The two adverbs together are the appendix's only editorialising, and they surround the one result that is inconvenient. The honest version drops both: the unadjusted contrast is negative, the adjusted contrast in the parent is not, and the difference is composition.

### How the pilot-versus-main selection check is phrased

The section opens with a question — the only question in the document:

> ## Pilot sample
>
> How much is selection into the survey biasing results? The main sample we use here suffers from its function as recruitment for an experiment on Claude Code access and research productivity. To assess how important this distortion is, we compare results in this sample to those from a pilot we ran in early February. This pilot was much smaller, explicitly excluded full professors, and only targeted sociologists, political scientists and management scholars. In the main sample, we expanded disciplines and career stage to draw in a broader range of respondents. The pilot survey also had some different questions on it, as we piloted and improved the survey. Crucially, it is missing our detailed coding agent question.

> But the pilot data are useful because we did not advertise the subsequent experiment in recruitment for it. Instead we simply described a study on changes in research workflows.

> This means we can use the pilot data to assess whether our results in the main sample are driven by selection into the experiment.

*(The last two quotations run across the page 5 / page 6 break; they are one continuous passage in the document.)*

**Annotation.** This is the best-constructed passage in the appendix and the reason this file is in the corpus. Five moves, in this order:

1. **The threat is put as a question in the reader's own words, as the first sentence of the section.** "How much is selection into the survey biasing results?" Not "a potential limitation is selection"; not "results should be interpreted with caution". A referee's question, asked in the referee's register, with a quantitative shape ("how much"). Everything after it is an answer to a question the reader has already seen asked, which is why the section reads as evidence rather than as a disclaimer.
2. **The concession is unhedged and comes second.** "The main sample we use here **suffers from** its function as recruitment for an experiment on Claude Code access and research productivity." The verb is the strongest in the document. There is no "may", no "could potentially", no "to some degree". The mechanism is named exactly — recruitment for an experiment about the very technology being measured — and the product is named because a product really was offered.
3. **The test design is stated before its result, with its own identifying assumption visible.** "we compare results in this sample to those from a pilot we ran in early February… because we did not advertise the subsequent experiment in recruitment for it. Instead we simply described a study on changes in research workflows." The pilot is not merely an earlier sample; it is a sample drawn *without the contaminating inducement*, and the sentence that establishes that is the sentence the whole check rests on. It is stated plainly, in fourteen words, as a fact about what was and was not said to people.
4. **Every way the comparison is imperfect is listed before the comparison is run.** Smaller; excluded full professors; three disciplines only; different questions. Four differences, in one sentence and a half, none minimised. And then the fifth, set apart in its own sentence with an adverb that refuses to soften it: "**Crucially**, it is missing our detailed coding agent question." The check cannot be run on the study's own headline measure, and the document says so in nine words and does not return to it. Placing the disqualifying limitation of a robustness check *inside the paragraph that introduces the check*, flagged as the most important one, is the move to copy.
5. **The comparison is made comparable by construction, and the construction is stated.** "To make the recruitment logic of the two samples comparable, we narrow the main survey sample to sociologists, political scientists and management scholars and exclude full professors." The main sample is cut down to the pilot's frame rather than the pilot being weighted up to the main sample's; the reason is given as a purpose clause at the head of the sentence; and the same restriction is then restated in the table's own *Notes* line so the exhibit travels with it.

The result paragraph is where the phrasing is most worth study:

> There is little difference in overall rates of AI use across the two samples, but the full sample uses AI for more research tasks and is more optimistic about its impact. However, the key gaps persist: coding and editing are the most common uses in both samples and drafting prose is rare in both. On the 1 to 10 slider, the full sample is one point more optimistic about field impacts and a half point more optimistic about paper productivity effects. But the gap between paper productivity and field impact beliefs persist. Overall, the full sample is composed of more pro-AI respondents, but the key gradients that we can measure are similar in the pilot sample drawn without AI-related recruitment materials.

**Annotation.** Five sentences, and the structure is *concede, then rescue, then concede, then rescue, then summarise as both*. Note what is conceded and what is defended, because the distinction is the point: **levels differ, orderings do not.** "There is little difference in overall rates" is a null stated without a bound; "the full sample uses AI for more research tasks and is more optimistic" concedes the direction of the bias; "the key gaps persist" then shifts the claim from the level of each quantity to the gap between quantities, which is the thing the parent report's argument actually rests on. The same move is made twice more, in the same shape, with the slider items and then in summary. A selection check that cannot show *no* bias can still show that the bias does not run along the dimension the finding uses, and this paragraph is the model for saying so.

The magnitudes are given in words at the coarsest grain the claim can bear: "one point more optimistic", "a half point more optimistic", on a scale the sentence names in passing ("On the 1 to 10 slider"). Rounding a difference to the nearest half point is a way of claiming only the direction and the order of magnitude, and it matches the fact that no confidence interval is given for either. The closing sentence then states both halves of the verdict in one line — "the full sample is composed of more pro-AI respondents, but the key gradients that we can measure are similar" — with the fence "that we can measure" doing the work that a limitations section would otherwise do. Four words that concede the whole of point 4 above without repeating it.

What a referee would still ask of this passage: "little difference" has no interval and no minimum detectable effect against a pilot of roughly a hundred respondents; "similar" is never defined; the gaps that "persist" are not tested for equality across the two samples; and the sign convention of the table's difference column is nowhere stated, so a reader cannot tell from the text which sample each difference is subtracted from.

## Comparisons

The appendix makes three comparisons and no others. None is a robustness variant in the sense this team means, and the document contains **no sensitivity analysis, no alternative specification with results shown, no leave-one-out, and no validation of any constructed variable against an external benchmark.**

### Users against non-users, unadjusted against adjusted

> These unadjusted differences are shown in Figure A1 and show that agent users post more working papers and submit more grants, but actually submit fewer papers to journals. However, as Figure 5 shows, this lower rate of submitting to journals reflects the distribution of coding agent use across disciplines and career stages.

**Annotation.** The comparison that carries the appendix's purpose, and it is a comparison *of two estimates of the same quantity*, one in this document and one in the parent. The unadjusted version is shown here precisely because it disagrees with the adjusted version on one outcome, and the disagreement is named rather than buried. The construction — publish the raw contrast in the appendix, the conditioned contrast in the report, and state in the appendix which outcome changes sign and why — is exactly what a robustness appendix is for, and it is the strongest structural lesson in this file. Its weakness is that the "why" is asserted ("reflects the distribution of coding agent use across disciplines and career stages") rather than decomposed: no contribution of each control is shown, and a reader cannot tell whether discipline, career stage or survey week is doing the work.

### Main sample against pilot sample

> Table A2 shows results comparing the pilot sample to the full survey. To make the recruitment logic of the two samples comparable, we narrow the main survey sample to sociologists, political scientists and management scholars and exclude full professors.

**Annotation.** A comparison used to defend the sample rather than to establish a result, and the only one in the corpus so far that holds a *recruitment design* constant instead of a measure. Both sides are described symmetrically, the restriction is stated as a purpose clause, and the restriction is repeated in the exhibit's notes. Note the vocabulary shift: everywhere else in the appendix the two things compared are "coding agent users" and "non-users", but the entire pilot comparison is conducted in terms of "AI use", because the pilot instrument lacked the coding-agent item. The change of construct is visible in the wording and is never named as one. When a robustness check has to fall back on a coarser measure, **say that the construct changed, not only that the question is missing.**

### The specification against a Poisson alternative

> A Poisson model specification gives similar results.

**Annotation.** One sentence, no table, no coefficient, no definition of "similar". Recorded here as the document's third comparison because it is offered as one; it is also the clearest example in this corpus of a robustness claim a reader cannot check. The sentence is worth keeping as a *shape* — the alternative estimator named at the end of the specification paragraph, in one line — on the condition that the line points at a table.

## Figure captions

One figure and two tables. **Table captions sit above the exhibit; the figure caption sits below it.** Numbering is A1, A2 with no point separator, which distinguishes the appendix series from the parent report's plain numerals (Figure 5, Figure 5b) without the "A." convention the Economic Index appendices use.

### Table A1

Above the table, bold, two lines, no gloss and no notes:

> **Table A1: Sample of respondents to the WFL Baseline Survey, by career stage and discipline**

**Annotation.** Fifteen words: label, colon, what the exhibit is a sample *of*, then the two dimensions it is broken out by, joined by "by". No n in the caption (it is in the prose sentence that points at the table, and again in the table's own Total row), no date, no source line, no explanatory sentence at all. The caption is doing only one job — naming the crosstab — and the paragraph above it has already done the rest, which is what the caption-above-table placement buys: the reader arrives having been told what to expect.

The one defect is the acronym. "WFL Baseline Survey" is the survey's internal name, it appears exactly once in the document, and it is never expanded — not in the caption, not in the prose, not on the cover. A reader cannot cite the instrument by name. **Never let an exhibit introduce a proper noun that the text does not define**, particularly not the name of the dataset.

The table's own structure, transcribed from the page render (it is a raster image with no text layer): two independent panels side by side, each with the columns *Career stage | N | %* and *Discipline | N | %*, five career-stage rows and nine discipline rows, each panel closed by a *Total* row. Per `room/director-2026-09-16-figure-values-ruling.md`, no cell values are recorded in this file. The design point that matters for style is that the two panels are not a crosstab of each other: the table gives two marginal distributions side by side and no joint distribution, so a reader cannot tell how career stage and discipline co-vary — the exact thing the regression conditions on.

### Figure A1

Below the figure. The label and the title sentence are bold; everything from "Outcomes are self-reported" onward is italic:

> **Figure A1: Unadjusted research productivity differences between coding agent users and other researchers.** *Outcomes are self-reported by respondents. Working papers posted, journal submissions and journal resubmissions are mutually exclusive categories. See Figure 5 for estimates adjusted by discipline, career stage and survey week. Whiskers show 95% confidence intervals calculated using the robust standard errors scaled by the outcome mean. * p < 0.05, ** p < 0.01.*

**Annotation.** Sixty-seven words, the longest caption in the corpus, and the most complete. Read it as five clauses each answering a different reader question, in a strict order worth copying:

1. **What is plotted, and the qualifier first.** "**Unadjusted** research productivity differences between coding agent users and other researchers." The word that limits the exhibit is the first word of the title, before the quantity. A reader who reads nothing else cannot mistake this for the adjusted result. Note also "other researchers" rather than "non-users": the comparison group is described by who they are, not by what they lack.
2. **Provenance of the outcome.** "Outcomes are self-reported by respondents." Five words, stated as a property of the data rather than as a limitation, and placed before anything else about the exhibit. This is the survey-appendix equivalent of the March 2026 appendix's habit of putting the awkward fact inside the definition.
3. **A construction rule the chart cannot show.** "Working papers posted, journal submissions and journal resubmissions are mutually exclusive categories." Without it a reader would double-count three of the six rows. The caption discloses the partition and nothing else about the coding of the outcomes — and note that only three of the six plotted outcomes are covered by the sentence, so the status of the other three is left open.
4. **A pointer to the adjusted version.** "See Figure 5 for estimates adjusted by discipline, career stage and survey week." The caption sends the reader out of the appendix to the result that supersedes it, and enumerates the adjustment set while doing so. An exhibit that is deliberately inferior to another exhibit says where the better one is, in its own caption.
5. **The uncertainty, with its estimator and its rescaling.** "Whiskers show 95% confidence intervals calculated using the robust standard errors scaled by the outcome mean." The caption names what the whiskers are, at what level, from which standard errors, and — crucially — that they have been divided by the same base as the point estimates, which is what makes them commensurate with a percentage axis. This is the clause most captions omit and the one to imitate verbatim in shape.

Then the star key, "* p < 0.05, ** p < 0.01", which defines two levels. The rendered chart carries a three-star marker on one estimate, a level the key does not define; the markers were read from the page render and no value is recorded here. Two stated levels and three used is the sort of internal inconsistency a build should refuse.

In-figure text, read from the render and quoted here as in-figure, never as caption: the panel title "Raw gaps in productivity"; the two half-plane labels "Agent users produce fewer" and "Agent users produce more"; the six row labels "Empirical projects started", "Grant proposals submitted", "Working papers posted", "Conference submissions", "Journal submissions", "Journal resubmissions"; and the x-axis label "Coding agent user — non-user (% of non-user mean)". Three things follow. The in-figure title is plainer than the caption title ("Raw gaps" against "Unadjusted… differences"), so the exhibit reads colloquially when lifted and precisely when read in place. The half-plane labels translate the axis sign into a sentence at each end, which removes the commonest misreading of a coefficient plot and costs eight words — copy this. And the axis label uses an em dash where it means a minus sign, which at small sizes is ambiguous with the dash in "non-user"; the estimand of a difference plot should be written so the subtraction cannot be misread.

### Table A2

Caption above, bold, one line and eight words; italic notes below the table:

> **Table A2: Comparison of main and pilot samples**

> *Notes: The main sample is restricted to include only sociology, political science, and management and exclude full professors, for clearer comparison to the narrow pilot sample. Use case shares are conditional on AI use.*

**Annotation.** The division of labour between the two is the lesson. The caption names the comparison and nothing else — no sample, no restriction, no n, no date. The *Notes* line carries the two facts without which the columns cannot be read: the restriction applied to one side of the comparison and its purpose, and the denominator of one block of rows ("conditional on AI use"). Both are restatements of sentences in the prose above, and the repetition is correct, because a table travels. The italic "Notes:" convention is standard journal practice and appears nowhere else in the corpus; it is the right place for a denominator.

What the notes omit: the sign convention of the *Difference* column, and any key for the significance stars printed in it. The table's structure, transcribed from the render: a *Main sample* block and a *Pilot sample* block, each with *Mean* and *N* columns, then a single *Difference* column; rows grouped under three italic sub-headings, *AI adoption*, *Beliefs about AI (1-10 scale)* and *AI use cases (among AI users)*, with an *N use cases (mean)* row closing the last group. Per the figure-values ruling no cell values are recorded here. Two style points from the structure alone: the per-row *N* columns let a reader see that the use-case rows have a smaller base than the adoption rows, which is what makes the "conditional on AI use" note verifiable; and the scale of the belief items is declared inside the row-group heading, "(1-10 scale)", which is where a scale belongs when it applies to a block of rows rather than to the whole table.

**The caption pattern in summary.** Bold label with an "A"-prefixed number and a colon; a noun phrase naming the exhibit and, for the figure, the qualifier first; for a figure, the gloss runs on in italic and gives provenance, the partition rule, the pointer to the superseding exhibit, and the uncertainty with its estimator, in that order; for a table, the caption is the noun phrase alone and every reading instruction goes into an italic *Notes* line below. Captions above tables, below figures. No caption states a date, and none states an n.

## Limitations

**There is no limitations section and no limitations heading.** The words "limitation" and "caveat" do not occur. But this document is unlike the other appendices in the corpus in one respect that matters: it devotes an entire named section, a third of its length, to a single threat to validity, and that section is the strongest writing in it. Limitation work is therefore done in two places — inline, at the point of construction, and once at length.

Collected, verbatim, in order of appearance:

> Respondents were informed that the study concerns academic workflows and that participants would be eligible for a randomized experiment providing access to Claude Max accounts.

> Unknown or androgynous names are left out of the analysis.

> The raw differences in output among respondents are more difficult to interpret, as they reflect differences between disciplines in practices like posting working papers, timing of conference submissions, and expected number of journal publications per year.

> *Outcomes are self-reported by respondents.*

> The main sample we use here suffers from its function as recruitment for an experiment on Claude Code access and research productivity.

> This pilot was much smaller, explicitly excluded full professors, and only targeted sociologists, political scientists and management scholars.

> Crucially, it is missing our detailed coding agent question.

> Overall, the full sample is composed of more pro-AI respondents, but the key gradients that we can measure are similar in the pilot sample drawn without AI-related recruitment materials.

**Annotation.**

- **The one that is given a section is the one that cannot be answered inline.** Selection into the survey has no fix available in the main data, so it gets a heading, a question, a design, a table and a verdict. Everything else — self-report, the gender exclusion, the composition problem in the raw gaps — is a clause where the variable is defined. The sorting rule is worth adopting: **a limitation that can be bounded goes next to the thing it qualifies; a limitation that requires new evidence gets its own section and that evidence.**
- **The register is flat and the verbs are strong.** "suffers from", "is missing", "are left out", "more difficult to interpret". No "as with all surveys", no "these results should be interpreted with caution", no sentence re-asserting the contribution after a concession. The only softening word in the whole document is "may" — and it does not occur at all.
- **Each concession names a mechanism.** Not "the sample may not be representative" but "its function as recruitment for an experiment on Claude Code access"; not "the pilot differs" but four named differences and a missing item; not "outcomes are noisy" but "self-reported by respondents", in the caption of the exhibit that plots them.
- **The verdict is stated as both halves.** "the full sample is composed of more pro-AI respondents, **but** the key gradients that we can measure are similar" — the bias is conceded in the same sentence in which the finding is defended, with an explicit fence on what the defence covers. A limitation that ends in a two-clause verdict tells the reader exactly how much to discount, which a list of bullet points does not.
- **What a referee would raise first, and the appendix does not.** (1) No response rate: 44,700 invited, 1,260 analysed, no intermediate counts, no completion rate, no statement of how many of the 1,260 came from each of the five channels — and since one channel is self-selection from mailing lists, the frame cannot be characterised at all. (2) No comparison of respondents to the population of quantitative social scientists on any observable. (3) "There is little difference in overall rates of AI use" is a null with no interval and no minimum detectable effect against a pilot of roughly a hundred. (4) No reliability check on the discipline recode, and no statement of how the model and human coders divided the work. (5) The gender variable is constructed and never used, and the number dropped is not given. (6) "A Poisson model specification gives similar results" is unverifiable as written. (7) No clustering of standard errors is discussed for a design with discipline and career-stage strata. (8) The outcome measures are self-reported counts with no recall window stated anywhere. (9) "Coding agent user" — the treatment variable of the entire document — is never defined; the item that measures it is described only as "our detailed coding agent question".
- **The lesson to take and the lesson to leave.** Take the shape of the "Pilot sample" section: question, unhedged concession, design with its identifying assumption, every imperfection listed before the result, then a verdict in two clauses. Take the naming of the gender variable after its proxy. Leave the missing denominators: in this house the funnel is published at every rung, the null carries its MDE, and "similar results" points at a table.

## Close

**There is no close.** The document's last elements, in order, are the Table A2 caption, the table, and the italic *Notes* line. Verbatim, the last text in the appendix:

> *Notes: The main sample is restricted to include only sociology, political science, and management and exclude full professors, for clearer comparison to the narrow pilot sample. Use case shares are conditional on AI use.*

There is no conclusion, no summary, no restatement of what the appendix established, no pointer back to the parent piece, no references, no data or code availability statement, no acknowledgements, and no contact address. The final third of page 6 is white.

**Annotation.**

- **Ending on a table's notes is the correct ending for the genre**, and it is the same ending as both Economic Index appendices in this corpus — one ends on a footnote about crosswalk vintages, one on a coding scheme. Three appendices, three endings that are pieces of exhibit furniture. An appendix stops when it has handed over the last object it was holding; nothing is concluded because nothing was argued.
- **But the last substantive sentence is a verdict, which the others do not have.** "Overall, the full sample is composed of more pro-AI respondents, but the key gradients that we can measure are similar in the pilot sample drawn without AI-related recruitment materials" is the final sentence of prose, and it settles the question the section opened with. The document therefore *does* close its own argument one paragraph before it stops — it simply does not announce that it is doing so. A section that opens with a question and ends with a two-clause answer needs no concluding paragraph, and this is the cleanest demonstration in the corpus of why: **the close is the answer to the opening question, and if the opening is a question the close writes itself.**
- **Title against ending.** The title is "Appendix: Coding Agents in the Social Sciences" — the parent's subject, with the genre prefixed — and the ending is a note about which disciplines were dropped to make two samples comparable. The title promises the topic and the ending delivers a caveat about the sample, which is the honest relation for this genre: the appendix is where the topic is qualified. The test that does apply is narrower, and this document passes it: every section is the answer to a question a referee would ask of the parent, and they appear in the order a referee would ask them.
- **What this licenses in our own posts, and what it does not.** Licensed: a methodology section may end on a table note; a robustness section may be framed as a question and answered in two clauses; a caption may be the longest paragraph on its page when the exhibit is deliberately superseded. Not licensed: a post may not end this way. A post that stops at its last exhibit has left the reader without the why-it-matters, and this document can omit it only because the parent spent it.
