## Section order

Headings in PDF order. **Nothing is numbered** — no chapters, no sections, no lettered appendix. The three body headings carry the whole structure, and two of the three are questions.

1. **Cover (p. 1).** Title over three lines, then "Published / April 22, 2026" and "Authors / Maxim Massenkoff and Saffron Huang" in two columns. No abstract, no findings, no figure, no affiliation, no acknowledgement.
2. **"Key findings" (p. 2, ~90 words).** Three bullets, one sentence or two each. **No number appears in any of them** except "81,000" as a description of the sample — see `## Opening move`; this is the only summary block in the corpus that is entirely free of findings numbers.
3. **Untitled introduction (p. 2, ~250 words).** Four paragraphs, no heading: the standing instrument and the gap; the new data as the bridge; what the survey asked and what was heard, both signs; the headline pairing with its evidence graded ("initial evidence").
4. **"Who worries about job displacement?" (pp. 3–4, ~450 words).** An epigraph quote with an inferred-occupation attribution and footnote 1; the one unconditional share with four illustrative quotes; **the classifier-method paragraph**; the exposure pairing with its construct re-glossed; the Figure 1 reading, carrying the slope and the quartile ratio; then career stage, with the prior-research pointer, the coverage statement and footnote 2. Figures 1 and 2 (images pp. 4 and 5).
5. **"Who benefits from AI?" (pp. 5–7, ~650 words).** The 1–7 scale defined and anchored with graded responses (footnote 3); the mean, then the selection caveat, then the missingness; the wage-quartile result with its one robustness check and the cross-series echo; the low-paid illustrations; the major-occupational-group ordering (footnote 4); the surplus-destination result and its career-stage split (footnote 5). Figures 3 and 4 (images pp. 6 and 8).
6. **"Scope and speed" (pp. 8–10, ~450 words).** The four productivity types, one quoted response each; the 48/40 split; the speedup construct and its three anchors; the U-shape, the left-hand bar and the creative-work mechanism; the rising arm and its economic reading. Figures 5 and 6 (images pp. 9 and 10).
7. **"Discussion" (p. 11, ~350 words).** Four paragraphs: the perceived/observed synthesis; the benefit side, ending on its own counter-finding; the three caveats; the "Still," paragraph on what the exercise is for.
8. **"Citation" and "Acknowledgements" (p. 12).**
9. **Footnotes 1–5 (p. 13, unheaded).** Continuous numbering, all five on one page.
10. **"Appendix" (p. 14)** — the deferral to the companion study, the occupation-coverage arithmetic, three specimen quotes, and the one robustness check. **"Prompt examples" (p. 15)** — two classifier prompts in full, in mono.

**Where the figures sit, and it is a rule.** Every figure image is on the page *after* the prose that introduces it, with its caption **below** the image, and the next paragraph of body prose below the caption. Figure 1's lead-in is p. 3 and its image is p. 4; Figure 4's lead-in ends p. 7 and its image opens p. 8 above the next section's heading. The consequence for a reader is that the finding and its exhibit are never on the same page, and the March 2026 device of putting a caveat in the paragraph directly under the exhibit is structurally unavailable.

**Where the findings sit: in the bullets, then once each in the body, and not in the close.** No finding is restated in the Discussion with its number; the Discussion contains no digits at all. Each body finding appears exactly once. Compare the March 2026 report, whose close introduced two numbers for the first time.

**Where the methods sit: in the first finding, then in the appendix, then in another document.** There is no methods section. The classifier method is one paragraph inside the first finding (p. 3) — good placement, and better than a methods section for a piece this length, because the reader meets the instrument at the first number it produced. The two published prompts are in the appendix. Everything else — sample, recruitment, consent, languages, the survey instrument, weighting, limitations — is exported in one sentence: "See "What 81,000 people want from AI" and its accompanying appendix for more details on the sample, survey methodology, and limitations. This study uses the same data, but adds additional variables using Claude-powered classifiers." (p. 14). Seven of the eight constructs in the document are new here; the population they are measured on is defined elsewhere. **Our own posts define the sample in the post.**

**What the section order does not contain.** No abstract; no limitations, caveats or threats-to-validity heading; no methods heading; no data-availability statement; no reference list; no statement of which Claude model ran the classifiers or when; and no N for any figure — the word "sample" occurs once in the whole document, in the appendix's deferral sentence.

## Opening move

### "Key findings", verbatim and in full

> **Key findings**
>
> - Our recent survey of 81,000 Claude users shows that people who work in roles that are more exposed to AI have more concerns about AI-driven job displacement. These concerns are also higher among early-career respondents.
>
> - Those in the highest- and lowest-paid occupations report the largest productivity gains, most commonly from increases in scope (doing new tasks).
>
> - Respondents experiencing the largest speedups from AI express higher concern about job displacement.

### The introduction, all four paragraphs, verbatim

> In order to inform the public about the economic changes we're observing with AI, our Economic Index shares what work Claude is being asked to do, and in which jobs Claude is doing the largest share of tasks. To date, however, we've lacked information on how these usage patterns map onto people's thoughts and impressions of AI.

> Our recent survey study with 81,000 Claude users provides a way to connect people's economic concerns with what we've quantified in Claude traffic.

> The survey asked people about their visions and fears around advances in AI. Many of the thoughts that people shared touched on economic topics. We learned that many people fear job displacement—though they also feel more productive and empowered at work. In some cases, AI has enabled them to start businesses, or given them time for more important things; in others, AI feels stifling, or imposed on them by their employers.

> The survey's results provide initial evidence that observed exposure (our measure of AI displacement risk) is correlated with economic concern around AI. People in highly exposed occupations—as defined by the tasks Claude is observed performing—were more nervous about economic displacement. This is consistent with people being broadly aware of AI's diffusion and potential impacts. We expand on our findings below.

(On the web, "Economic Index" links to the March 2026 report, "survey study with 81,000 Claude users" to the companion study, and "observed exposure" to the labor-market-impacts paper.)

### Annotation

- **The opening is a gap sentence, and it arrives in the first paragraph.** "To date, however, we've lacked information on how these usage patterns map onto people's thoughts and impressions of AI." Twenty-one words, and it is the entire why-it-matters. The shape is **instrument → what the instrument cannot see → the new data that can see it → what it showed**. Compare the three openings already in the corpus: February 2025 is *forecast → therefore measure*; January 2026's blog is *three questions → therefore instrument*; March 2026 is *wave → window → what changed*. This one is the only opening in the corpus built on an **admitted blind spot in the team's own existing measurement**, and it is the best available model for a post that layers a second instrument onto the Index. The construction is reusable verbatim: *X shows A and B. To date, however, we've lacked information on how A and B map onto C.*
- **The bridge sentence is one sentence long and does not oversell.** "Our recent survey study with 81,000 Claude users provides a way to connect people's economic concerns with what we've quantified in Claude traffic." "provides a way to connect" — not *establishes*, not *reveals*, not *for the first time*. There is no superlative anywhere in the opening, no "first-of-its-kind", no "clearest picture yet". The novelty claim is carried entirely by the gap sentence that precedes it, which is the cheapest and most honest way to claim novelty: say what was missing, then say what fills it.
- **Both signs are stated before any finding.** "We learned that many people fear job displacement—though they also feel more productive and empowered at work. In some cases, AI has enabled them to start businesses, or given them time for more important things; in others, AI feels stifling, or imposed on them by their employers." Two balanced pairs, the second with a semicolon holding the halves apart. The reader is told the answer is two-sided before being told anything quantitative, so no later caveat has to withdraw an expectation the opening created. **An opening that states the tension cannot be accused of burying it.**
- **The evidence is graded in the sentence that states the finding.** "The survey's results provide **initial evidence** that observed exposure … **is correlated with** economic concern around AI." The strength of the claim ("initial evidence"), the nature of the relationship ("is correlated with") and the construct's status ("our measure of AI displacement risk", in apposition) are all in one sentence, in the opening, before the body. This is the most transferable sentence in the document: the grading is not deferred to a limitations section, because there is no limitations section. Contrast the March 2026 report, whose summary bullets quoted its headline in the unit that read largest.
- **The key findings block contains no findings numbers.** Three bullets, and the only numeral in them is "81,000", which describes the sample. The comparisons are carried entirely in words: "more exposed", "the highest- and lowest-paid occupations", "the largest productivity gains", "the largest speedups", "higher concern". This is unique in the corpus — the March 2026 bullets carried six numbers, the September 2025 report's carried its headline ratios — and it is worth understanding rather than copying wholesale. It works here because every quantity in the document is a share of a differently-defined denominator, so a number in a bullet would need its base attached, and the base would not fit. The lesson for us: **if a headline number cannot carry its denominator in the same breath, state the comparison in words and leave the number to the finding.** The lesson not to draw: our own findings must still put the number in the prose.
- **Where the first number is.** Not in the opening. The first finding number is "One fifth", on p. 3, roughly 600 words in; the first numeral is "1.3 percentage points" two paragraphs later. The reader is asked to accept the frame, the instrument and the two-sidedness of the answer before meeting any quantity.
- **Naming, and why this document's split is the right one.** The title says AI. The bullets say Claude for the sample ("81,000 Claude users") and AI for everything measured ("more exposed to AI", "AI-driven job displacement", "the largest speedups from AI"). That looks like a violation of the house rule until the instrument is read: the classifiers code **what respondents said about AI** — the published prompt asks "does this respondent say AI improves their work?" — while the one observed variable, `observed exposure`, is built on **Claude** usage. So in this document the perceived side genuinely is AI and the observed side genuinely is Claude, and the document holds the split with unusual care: "as defined by the tasks **Claude** is observed performing" sits in the same sentence as "more nervous about economic displacement" from **AI**. **Where a document measures perception of AI against observation of Claude, the naming rule is not a formality: it is the only thing telling the reader that the two axes are not the same object.** The two places the split breaks are both in the close — see `## Close`.
- **First person, and what it is used for.** Institutional and analytical throughout: "we're observing", "we've lacked", "We learned", "we've quantified", "We expand on our findings below". The one construction already available as a substitute is in the same paragraph — "as defined by the tasks Claude is observed performing" — a passive that names the definition rather than the definer. House style forbids the first person; this opening converts cleanly, because the gap sentence is a statement about the instrument — *usage patterns have not been mapped onto thoughts and impressions* — not about the authors.

## Findings and their caveats

### First: how a self-report is marked as a self-report

This is the document's spine, and it is done with verbs rather than hedges. Nearly every finding sentence carries a verb of speech or of reported experience, and the verb is chosen to fit how the claim was obtained:

> One fifth of the respondents in our survey **voiced concern** about economic displacement.

> People in the top 25% of exposure **mentioned the worry** three times as often as those in the bottom 25%.

> early-career respondents were much more likely to **express concern** about job displacement than senior workers

> Overall, people **reported** meaningful productivity gains on average.

> people in high-paying jobs, like software developers, **conveyed** the largest productivity gains from AI

> Some of the lowest-paid workers **describe** high productivity gains as well.

> **Respondents indicated** the recipient of these gains in about a quarter of interviews.

> most of these people **cited** benefits to themselves

> These respondents were more likely to **indicate** that AI posed a significant threat to their livelihoods.

> 40% of users who mentioned productivity **emphasized** speed.

> People are most likely to **talk about** benefits flowing to themselves

**Annotation.** Eleven findings, eleven speech verbs, and no sentence in the document says that a respondent *was* more productive or *was* at risk. The verb does the epistemic work that a hedge would otherwise have to do, which is why the document can report a mean of 5.1 without the word "self-reported" attached to it every time — it attached it once, at the definition ("the extent of people's **self-reported** productivity gains"), and thereafter carries it in the verb. **This is the cheapest and most durable device in the file: the finding sentence names the act of reporting, so the reader never has to be reminded that the measure is a perception.**

Two places the discipline slips, and both are instructive. "For every 10-percentage-point increase in exposure, perceived job threat **increased** by 1.3 percentage points" — the classifier-coded share behaves as a measured quantity here, with the fence carried only by the adjective "perceived". And the third key finding, "**Respondents experiencing** the largest speedups from AI express higher concern", asserts that respondents experienced speedups; the body is careful where the bullet is not ("the speedup **reported** by respondents", "the level of speedup **implied by their answers**"). The bullet is the sentence a critic would quote.

### Second: the provenance fence, and where it is placed

> Throughout this report, we use Claude-powered classifiers to infer people's attributes and sentiments from their responses. For example, many participants mention their line of work in passing or give informative details about their work life, which allows us to infer their occupation. Similarly, we quantify concerns about job loss by prompting Claude to identify and interpret direct quotes in which respondents indicate that their own role is at risk of AI-driven displacement. We give example prompts in the Appendix.

**Annotation.** Four sentences, placed **inside the first finding section, immediately after the first number and before the first comparison** — not in a methods section, not in a footnote, not in the appendix. Its four moves: (i) the scope of the method, first word ("Throughout this report"); (ii) one worked example of the easy case (occupation from passing mention); (iii) one worked example of the hard case, stated in operational detail — "prompting Claude to identify and interpret direct quotes in which respondents indicate that their own role is at risk" — where "identify **and interpret**" concedes that the classifier is doing more than string matching; (iv) a pointer to where the prompts can be read. A reader who stops here knows that every variable in the document is a model's reading of free text.

The fence is then re-stated at every point of use, and never in the same words twice: "coded using Claude" (Figure 1 caption); "Both fields are inferred from free-form responses using Claude-powered classifiers" (Figure 2 caption); "Using Claude to assess the survey responses, we rated…" (p. 5); "Claude gave a 5 to statements like…" (p. 5); "the mean inferred productivity benefit from AI (inferred using a Claude-powered classifier)" (Figure 3 caption, which fences twice in one clause); "by extracting whether their work was now much slower" (p. 9); "the level of inferred speedup" (Figure 6 caption); "we instructed Claude to leave this missing if it was unclear" (p. 14). **Nine fences for seven constructs, and the document never lets a classifier output appear naked.** The verb is always *infer*, *code*, *rate*, *extract* or *assess* — the word **"estimate" does not appear in the document at all**, and neither does "regression". That is a deliberate register: an inference from text is not an estimate of a parameter, and the document declines the vocabulary that would suggest it is.

### Third: how a classifier's scale is made legible

> Using Claude to assess the survey responses, we rated the extent of people's self-reported productivity gains from AI on a 1–7 scale, where 1 is "less productive," 2 is "no change," and each subsequent level denotes a larger gain. Responses that scored 7 included testimonials like, "It used to take months to make the website I [made] in 4-5 days"; Claude gave a 5 to statements like, "What might have taken four hours was accomplished in half the time," and a 2 to ones like, "Personally, I had AI help me fix code on a website. But it took multiple passes to get the result I was after."3

> [3] The scale is not centered because most people say positive things about productivity, yielding almost entirely 6s and 7s on the original Likert scale. The scale we use here ran from 1 = less productive, 2 = no change, 3 = slightly more productive, 4 = moderately more productive, 5 = substantially more productive, 6 = much more productive, to 7 = transformatively more productive—AI has fundamentally changed what or how much they can produce.

**Annotation.** The best passage in the document and the model for reporting any model-assigned score. The scale is anchored not by its endpoints but **by three quoted responses at three named scale points** — 7, 5 and 2 — so the reader can calibrate the instrument on the same material the classifier saw. "Claude gave a 5 to statements like" names the grader in the act of grading. A reader who disagrees with the grade can say so, which is what makes the mean quotable: "The mean productivity rating was 5.1, corresponding to 'substantially more productive.'" **The mean is reported against the scale's own verbal label rather than against another number**, because there is no other number to compare it with; that is the correct treatment of a score with no external benchmark, and it is worth copying exactly.

Footnote 3 then discloses something a referee would otherwise have to find: the published scale is a **re-coding adopted after the original instrument hit its ceiling** ("yielding almost entirely 6s and 7s on the original Likert scale"). A scale chosen in the light of the data, disclosed in a footnote, with the full seven labels supplied so the choice can be inspected. Disclosure without assessment — the document never says what the re-coding does to the mean, or what the mean would have been on the original scale — but the disclosure alone is more than most documents manage. **In our own posts this belongs beside the number, with the direction stated.**

### Finding 1 — the one unconditional share, and how a section opens on a quote

> "Well like anyone who has a white collar job these days I'm 100% concerned, pretty much 24/7 concerned about losing my job eventually to A.I."
> — Software engineer 1

> One fifth of the respondents in our survey voiced concern about economic displacement. Some worried about this in the abstract: one software developer cautioned about "the possibility of AI in its current state being used to replace junior positions." Others lamented that their jobs, or aspects of their jobs, were being automated away. One market researcher said, "In terms of improving my capability, it's no doubt. [B]ut in the future AI may replace my work." In some jobs, people felt it made their work harder. One software developer observed that "when AI arrived, the project managers started giving harder and harder tickets and bugs to solve."

> [1] We inferred people's occupations using the first question in the survey ("What's the last thing you used an AI chatbot for?") or indications given in other responses.

**Annotation.** The section opens on an unattributed-by-name respondent quote, and **the first footnote in the document hangs off the attribution rather than the quote** — footnote 1 tells the reader that "Software engineer" is itself a classifier output, at the first moment an occupation label is used for anything. Fencing the attribution at its first appearance, in a footnote, is an elegant solution to a real problem; the cost is that it is done once, and every later occupation attribution in the document inherits it silently.

The share is **spelled out in words** — "One fifth" — and so are the document's two other approximate denominators, "about half of respondents" (p. 4) and "about a quarter of interviews" (p. 7), while every point estimate is a numeral (1.3, 5.1, 3%, 42%, 48%, 40%, 10%, 60%, 80%) and so are the exact coverage shares in the appendix (61%, 39%, 11%, 28%). That is a consistent and useful convention: **approximate quantities in words, measured ones in numerals**, so the reader can see which is which without being told.

Then four quotes doing four different jobs — the abstract worry, the concrete loss, the intensification of work, and the junior-hiring channel — each attributed to an occupation and none to a person, each under twenty words, and none of them attached to a magnitude. Bracketed corrections are preserved ("[B]ut"), and the editing is minimal and visible. **A quote in this document is never evidence for a share; it is evidence that a category has content.** That division is what lets the document carry twelve respondent quotes in ten pages of body, and four more in the appendix, without reading as anecdote.

What is missing beside the one-fifth share: the denominator is the full sample, but no sentence says so, and no sentence says how many respondents were classifiable either way on job threat. It is the only unconditional share in the document and the only one whose base a reader can guess wrongly.

### Finding 2 — perceived threat against observed exposure: the headline pairing

> Respondents' perceived threat from AI was correlated with our own measure of observed exposure, which reflects the percentage of a job's tasks for which Claude is used. A respondent was more concerned about AI when our observed exposure measure for that respondent was higher. Elementary school teachers were less worried about their own displacement than software engineers, for example, consistent with the fact that Claude usage skews toward coding tasks.

> We show this in Figure 1 below. The y-axis is the percentage of respondents in a given occupation who said that AI is already replacing their role or is likely to do so soon. The x-axis is observed exposure. The plot shows that, on average, people in more exposed occupations tended to express more concern about their jobs being automated away. For every 10-percentage-point increase in exposure, perceived job threat increased by 1.3 percentage points. People in the top 25% of exposure mentioned the worry three times as often as those in the bottom 25%.

**Annotation.** Six moves in two paragraphs, and the order is the thing to copy. (i) The relationship is named with the weakest adequate verb, "was correlated with". (ii) The borrowed construct is **re-glossed in the same sentence** — "which reflects the percentage of a job's tasks for which Claude is used" — so a reader who has not read the source paper can follow. (iii) The axes are *narrated in prose before the figure*: "The y-axis is the percentage of respondents in a given occupation who said that AI is already replacing their role or is likely to do so soon. The x-axis is observed exposure." Two flat sentences, no adjectives, and they restate the coding rule of the outcome rather than its name. Given that the figure sits on the next page, narrating the axes is not redundancy but necessity. (iv) The direction is stated with its qualifier inside the clause: "on average, people in more exposed occupations **tended to** express more concern". (v) The slope is given **in the unit of the comparison, not of the variable** — "For every 10-percentage-point increase in exposure, perceived job threat increased by 1.3 percentage points" — the same device as the March 2026 report's slope per \$10 of hourly wage, and the reason a reader can hold the number. (vi) The extreme contrast is given as a ratio in words: "three times as often as those in the bottom 25%".

The named-occupation pair comes *before* all of it, in the previous paragraph, with its mechanism attached in the same sentence: elementary school teachers against software engineers, "consistent with the fact that Claude usage skews toward coding tasks." **Two recognisable occupations, opposed, with the mechanism in the same breath** — the February 2025 device, and the pair is chosen for recognisability rather than for extremity.

What a referee circles, all of it a matter of how the sentences are built rather than of the analysis: "**A respondent** was more concerned about AI when our observed exposure measure for **that respondent** was higher" asserts an individual-level relationship, while everything that follows — the y-axis, the slope, the quartile ratio — is at the **occupation** level. One sentence at the wrong unit of observation, three sentences before the sentence that gives the right one. No number of occupations, no weighting statement, no interval, and no base rates behind the 3×, so the ratio cannot be converted into shares. And the re-gloss is *simpler than the source definition* — the construct's own paper gates tasks on feasibility and weights automated use above augmentative use (see `wiki/reports/labor-market-impacts-2026-03.md`) — which is the risk of re-glossing a borrowed construct in one clause: **the simpler gloss is the one the reader carries away.**

### Finding 3 — career stage: a finding with no number in it

> Another important worker characteristic is career stage. In previous research, we reported tentative signs of a slowdown in the hiring of recent graduates and early-career workers in the United States. For about half of respondents in this survey, we were able to infer career stage from their answers.2 We found that early-career respondents were much more likely to express concern about job displacement than senior workers.

> [2] This came from various indications in the written responses. For example, several users mentioned using Claude for homework, which put them in the early-career group. And many referred to running their own businesses and being involved in hiring decisions, which put them in the senior group.

**Annotation.** Four sentences, and the third of them is the coverage of the variable — "For about half of respondents in this survey, we were able to infer career stage from their answers" — **stated before the finding it conditions**, which is the right order and is done only here. The finding itself has no number: "much more likely" is the whole magnitude, and the levels exist only inside the Figure 2 image. The document's own hedge vocabulary is used honestly here (the direction is strong, the magnitude is absent), but by the standard this corpus keeps arguing for, **a magnitude word is not a finding**; compare the March 2026 report's "increased slightly" for augmentation, annotated in `wiki/style/economic-index-2026-03-report.md` as the failure mode to avoid.

Footnote 2 is the good part: rather than asserting that the classifier works, it names the **signals** that drove two of the labels — homework for early-career, running a business and hiring for senior. A reader can see immediately that students are being routed into "early-career" in a document about job displacement, which is a real problem the footnote makes visible and the body never discusses. **A footnote that lets the reader find the objection is worth more than one that denies it.**

What the passage never supplies: the mapping from the appendix's six published labels (`student_or_entry`, `junior`, `mid`, `senior_or_lead`, `executive`, `unclear`) onto the two groups the prose and the figure use. The prompt is published in full and the collapse is not, so neither this finding nor the 60/80 split later is reproducible from the document.

### Finding 4 — the mean, with its selection caveat in the next sentence

> Overall, people reported meaningful productivity gains on average. The mean productivity rating was 5.1, corresponding to "substantially more productive." Our respondents were, of course, active Claude users who were willing to take a survey. This could make them more likely to report productivity benefits than the average user. Some 3% reported negative or neutral impacts, and 42% did not give a clear indication on productivity.

**Annotation.** Five sentences in one paragraph, and the sequence is finding → level → **selection → direction of the bias → missingness**. The caveat is two sentences after the number, in the same paragraph, and it is **signed**: "This could make them more likely to report productivity benefits than the average user" says which way the number is wrong. "of course" lowers the temperature — the concession is offered as obvious rather than extracted — and that is a licensed use of the adverb, unlike a hedge that weakens a claim.

Then the missingness, in one clause, as a share: 3% negative or neutral, 42% with no clear indication. **Reporting the non-response of a derived variable in the same paragraph as its mean is the single most useful thing this paragraph does**, and it is the number every later productivity share silently conditions on. What the document does not then do is restate the base at any later point of use: no subsequent sentence says *of the 58% who gave a clear indication*, and Figure 5's caption says "respondents" where the body says "users who explicitly mentioned productivity effects". **A base stated once and never repeated is a base the reader will lose.**

### Finding 5 — the wage-quartile result, its robustness and its echo

> This splits somewhat across income lines. The left panel in Figure 3 shows that people in high-paying jobs, like software developers, conveyed the largest productivity gains from AI. This result is not driven only by coding; it holds when we leave out computer and math occupations. It echoes a previous Economic Index finding that also favored higher-paid workers: in tasks requiring greater levels of education, Claude tended to reduce the time taken to complete a task (relative to doing it without AI) by a higher percentage.

> Some of the lowest-paid workers describe high productivity gains as well. This included a customer service representative using "AI to save me a lot of time with creating a response based on another one." And in some cases, people in low-wage jobs were using AI on technical side projects. One delivery driver, for example, was using Claude to start an e-commerce business, and a landscaper was building a music application.

**Annotation.** "This splits **somewhat** across income lines" — the adverb sizes the claim before the evidence, and it is the right adverb for a pattern the key findings bullet describes as a U ("the highest- and lowest-paid occupations"). Then the exhibit is made the subject of the sentence ("The left panel in Figure 3 shows that…"), which is the first-person-free construction our posts need and which this document uses three times.

The robustness check is in the same sentence as the finding, with a semicolon: "This result is not driven only by coding; it holds when we leave out computer and math occupations." Eleven words for the check, the exclusion named, no numbers. **A composition check stated in the sentence carrying the finding costs one clause**; the cost of doing it without numbers is that the reader cannot tell whether "holds" means unchanged or attenuated. Footnote 4 does the same thing for the other tail — "Removing these 'solopreneurs' still leaves management tied with computer and math occupations" — and is better, because "still leaves … tied" reports the *new* ranking rather than asserting that nothing moved.

The echo is the document's one cross-instrument corroboration, and its construction is careful: "It echoes a previous Economic Index finding that also favored higher-paid workers: **in tasks requiring greater levels of education, Claude tended to reduce the time taken** to complete a task (relative to doing it without AI) by a higher percentage." The earlier finding is restated in full, in its own units, with its counterfactual spelled out in parentheses — so the reader can see that the two results are about different things (a measured time saving by task education level; a self-reported gain by occupational wage quartile) and are only an echo. "Echoes" is the right verb for two different instruments pointing the same way, and it claims nothing more. Compare "is consistent with" and "align with" in the January 2026 blog: **corroboration is always stated as agreement in direction, never as confirmation.**

The low-paid paragraph is where the writing creates a problem it does not name. The two illustrations of high gains among the lowest-paid are a delivery driver starting an e-commerce business and a landscaper building a music application — **new ventures and side projects, not productivity in the current job** — and the document's own `scope` label ("doing things they COULDN'T do before") is exactly what would capture them. The quotes are doing the work of a heterogeneity analysis, and they reveal the ambiguity in the same breath. The same phenomenon appears at the other tail, named and unexamined: "These respondents are mostly entrepreneurs using Claude to build a business."

### Finding 6 — an ordering given as membership, not as values

> We look at this in more detail in the right panel of Figure 3, showing the inferred productivity gain by major occupational group. At the top are management occupations. These respondents are mostly entrepreneurs using Claude to build a business.4 The next highest category is computer and math, which includes software developers. The two groups exhibiting the mildest productivity improvements were workers in scientific and legal professions. Some lawyers worried about AI's ability to follow precise instructions. For example: "I have given very specific rules about what is where, how to read a legal document, what I want it to do… but it diverges every time."

**Annotation.** A ranking reported as **membership and position with no values at all** — top, next highest, and the two mildest — with a gloss on the top group ("mostly entrepreneurs"), a gloss on the second ("which includes software developers"), and a quoted mechanism for the bottom. This is the honest way to read a ranked exhibit whose values are in the image: give the reader the order and the identities, not adjectives about size. It is the same restraint as the March 2026 report's tenure-ranked task lists.

"exhibiting the mildest productivity improvements" is the one place the document reaches for a euphemism; "lowest" would have been shorter and is what the panel shows. And the lawyer's quote is the best-placed quote in the document: it sits immediately after the group with the smallest gain and offers a reason for it that is about the model's behaviour rather than the respondent's disposition.

### Finding 7 — where the surplus goes: one number and an ordinal chain

> A key question as AI diffuses through the economy is where the benefits will accrue—to workers, their managers, consumers, or corporations. Respondents indicated the recipient of these gains in about a quarter of interviews. Overall, most of these people cited benefits to themselves, through faster tasks, expanded scope, and freed-up time.5 But 10% of respondents who named a recipient said that employers or clients were asking for and getting more work. A smaller share mentioned benefits to AI companies, and an even smaller share said that AI would be a net negative. This depended on career stage: only 60% of early-career workers indicated that they personally benefited from AI, compared to 80% of senior professionals.

> [5] A major caveat, however, is that this survey went out to people with personal Claude accounts. A more representative picture would also include enterprise users, who may be more likely to say the value accrues to their employers.

**Annotation.** The finding is introduced by **the open question it answers**, stated as a question about the economy with the four candidate answers enumerated: "where the benefits will accrue—to workers, their managers, consumers, or corporations." The enumeration is the exhibit's category list, given before the exhibit, so the reader knows what the shares will be shares of. Then the base, in its own sentence, before any share: "Respondents indicated the recipient of these gains in about a quarter of interviews."

The distribution is then reported as **one number plus a descending chain**: most to themselves; "But 10% … said that employers or clients were asking for and getting more work"; "A smaller share mentioned benefits to AI companies, and an even smaller share said that AI would be a net negative." Only the 10% is quantified, and it is the one that cuts against the document's own empowerment reading — the number given is the unflattering one, and the flattering majority is left as "most". That ordering is a real editorial choice and the right one. The ordinal chain ("a smaller share … an even smaller share") is how to report a tail whose values live in the exhibit: the reader gets the rank order and nothing they could over-read.

Then the career-stage split, with both arms given ("only 60% … compared to 80%") rather than a difference, and "only" marking which arm is the finding. This is the second appearance of the career-stage cut, and it is the one with numbers — **one cut reported at two outcomes, once qualitatively and once quantitatively**, which is a weaker version of the house rule than testing one claim at two levels, but is what the document has.

Footnote 5 is the best-placed caveat in the document: the exclusion of enterprise users, **attached to the one finding it bites on**, with its direction named ("who may be more likely to say the value accrues to their employers"). It says "A major caveat, however" in its first six words, so a reader skimming footnotes cannot miss which one matters. The same fact appears in the Discussion as a general selection caveat; here it appears as a signed bias on a specific share. **A caveat that names the finding it moves, and the direction it moves it, is worth three general ones.**

### Finding 8 — the type of gain, with a quoted response per category

> Respondents also shared where they experienced gains in productivity. We separate this into scope, speed, quality, and cost. For example, many people using AI for coding tasks said things like, "I'm a non tech guy but now I'm a full stack developer." This is an expansion of scope; AI unlocks new abilities for them. In contrast, some users sped up tasks they were already doing, like the accountant who said, "I built a tool that helps me finish a financing task in 15 minutes that used to take 2 hours." Quality gains often came from more thorough checks of code, contracts, and other paperwork. And a small share of respondents mentioned the low cost of using AI: "[I]f I hire a social media manager it's over my budget."

> We find that the most common productivity enhancement is in scope, which was cited by 48% of users who explicitly mentioned productivity effects. 40% of users who mentioned productivity emphasized speed.

**Annotation.** A four-category taxonomy defined **entirely by example**: the four names in one flat sentence, then one quoted response for each, with the category named in the sentence that follows the quote ("This is an expansion of scope"). No definitions in the body at all — the operational definitions are in the published prompt in the appendix. For a general reader this is better than a definition list, and the quotes are chosen to be unambiguous instances rather than interesting ones. "In contrast" marks the scope/speed boundary, which is the one a reader would blur.

The two shares carry their base in the same clause, twice, in slightly different words: "48% of users who **explicitly mentioned productivity effects**"; "40% of users who **mentioned productivity**". Restating the base rather than writing *and 40% speed* is the discipline; dropping "explicitly" the second time is the kind of drift that makes two shares look like they are on the same footing when the document never says they are. Quality and cost are left unquantified in prose ("often came from", "a small share"), consistent with the ordinal-chain habit.

The reader is not told that the classifier is **forced-choice and single-label** — the appendix prompt says "If they mention several, pick the one they lean on most … Return exactly one label" — so "cited by 48%" reads as a share of mentions when it is a share of dominant-dimension labels. The prompt is published; the consequence for the noun "cited" is not drawn. **When a share comes from a forced-choice label, the sentence has to say so, because "cited" and *was coded as leaning on* are different claims.**

### Finding 9 — a shape as the finding

> People's experience with Claude might also shape their concerns about AI. To assess this, we measured the speedup reported by respondents, by extracting whether their work was now much slower (which we coded as 1), showed no change in speed (4), or had become much faster (7).

> We found that the relationship between speedup and perceived job threat is U-shaped (see Figure 6). The leftmost bar shows respondents who reported that AI slowed them down. These respondents were more likely to indicate that AI posed a significant threat to their livelihoods. For example, some creative workers, like fine artists and writers, found AI too stifling and rigid to help them at their own work. At the same time, they feared the diffusion of AI into creative fields would make it harder for them to find work.

> For the remaining respondents, perceived job threat increases consistently with the level of speedup implied by their answers. This makes some economic sense: if the time required to do one's tasks is shrinking quickly, there may be more uncertainty about the future viability of the role.

**Annotation.** The construct is introduced with its **conjecture first, marked as a conjecture** — "People's experience with Claude **might** also shape their concerns about AI. **To assess this**, we measured…" — which is the closest thing in the document to a stated hypothesis, and it is one sentence long. The coding is then given by its three anchors only (1 much slower, 4 no change, 7 much faster); the intervening levels are not defined and, unlike the productivity scale, no prompt for this classifier is published. A 1–7 scale defined by three anchors is a scale a reader cannot reconstruct.

The finding is a **shape**: "the relationship between speedup and perceived job threat is U-shaped". No bar heights, no Ns, no intervals, no test of non-monotonicity — the claim is entirely qualitative and is stated as such. The document then does the one thing that makes a U-shape reportable: it **splits the two arms and treats them as separate findings with separate mechanisms.** The left-hand bar is isolated ("The leftmost bar shows respondents who reported that AI slowed them down"), given a qualitative mechanism from quotes (creative workers, two channels in two sentences — the tool does not help them, *and* they fear the field), and the rising arm is given a separate economic reading fenced twice ("This **makes some economic sense**: … there **may be** more uncertainty"). Observation and conjecture are in adjacent sentences and are never in the same one.

What is not said, and it is the first thing a referee asks: respondents who reported being slowed down are, on the document's own account of the scale ("most people say positive things about productivity, yielding almost entirely 6s and 7s"), a small group — and the left-hand bar is the half of the U that the key findings bullet does not mention. **A shape claim whose distinguishing arm rests on an unstated and probably small cell needs its N in the sentence.** Note also the only occurrence of the word "significant" in the document is here, and it is not statistical: "AI posed a significant threat to their livelihoods" is a respondent's magnitude, not a test.

### The appendix: coverage, and the one robustness check

> Throughout, we inferred people's jobs from their responses, although we instructed Claude to leave this missing if it was unclear, which was the case for 61% of respondents. The remaining 39% have occupation labels. For about 11% of all respondents, an occupation was explicitly mentioned:

> "At work, I'm a teacher."
>
> "I am an Android software engineer…"
>
> "As a journalist…"

> For the remaining 28%, we inferred occupation from other clues in the responses. For example, one respondent who was labeled a physician reported that they wanted AI to improve "my interactions with patients." Some of these inferences will be wrong. As a robustness check, we confirmed that we got the same qualitative results as in Figures 1 and 3 when we use only the 11% who had explicitly reported their line of work in their response.

**Annotation.** The arithmetic is laid out so it closes: 61% missing **by instruction**, 39% labelled, of which 11% explicit and 28% inferred from clues. "we instructed Claude to leave this missing if it was unclear" makes the missingness a design choice rather than a failure, which is both true and the right way to say it. Then three specimen quotes at the easy end and one worked example at the hard end (a respondent labelled a physician on the strength of "my interactions with patients") — **the hard case is shown, not asserted**, and it is shown at its weakest so the reader can judge it.

"Some of these inferences will be wrong." Five words, flat, future tense, no hedge and no quantification. It is the sibling of the March 2026 report's "We do not observe people who signed up a year ago but are no longer using Claude", and it belongs in the same short list: **a caveat that states a fact about the data rather than a doubt about the result.** Our limitations sections should be built out of sentences of this shape.

The robustness check is then the document's only sensitivity analysis, and it is the right one — the 11% who stated their occupation outright against the full 39% — but it is reported as "the same qualitative results as in Figures 1 and 3" with no coefficient, no share and no sign of how much the slope moved. By the corpus's own standard (September 2025 reran its classifier and reported the 45-versus-49 discrepancy) **a robustness claim with no number is an assertion**, and this one is carrying two of the document's three headline findings.

### The hedge vocabulary, graded

In descending confidence, collected from the passages above: *we find* · *we found* · *we show* · *the plot shows* · *the left panel shows* · *the leftmost bar shows* · *we confirmed* · *increases consistently with* · *was correlated with* · *tended to* · *echoes* · *is consistent with* · *aligns with* · *provide initial evidence* · *there are signs that* · *splits somewhat* · *makes some economic sense* · *could* · *may* · *might* · *will be wrong*.

Two things are notable by absence. **"Suggest" does not appear in the document, and neither does "estimate", "regression" or any form of "significant" in its statistical sense.** The rungs the reports lean on — *these results suggest*, *appear linked to*, *may signal* — are replaced here by speech verbs, which is a different and in some ways stronger instrument: instead of hedging the claim, the document narrows the object of the claim to what was said. And **"provide initial evidence"** is the one place the *strength of the evidence* is graded rather than the claim; it is in the opening, and it governs the whole document.

The one bare causal construction is a conditional, not an assertion: "if the time required to do one's tasks is shrinking quickly, there may be more uncertainty about the future viability of the role." Compare the March 2026 report's "This is caused, mechanically, by…", where an adverb licensed the causal verb. Here the mechanism is offered as reasoning and is marked as reasoning by "makes some economic sense" in the preceding clause.

### Claude versus AI across the whole document

- **Title:** "What 81,000 people told us about the economics of AI" — AI, and "told us", which sets the register for everything: this is a document about what was said.
- **Section headings:** "Who worries about job displacement?" (neither); "Who benefits from AI?" (**AI**); "Scope and speed" (neither); "Discussion".
- **The sample always says Claude:** "81,000 Claude users", "80,508 Claude users", "active Claude users who were willing to take a survey", "users of personal accounts on Claude.ai", "people with personal Claude accounts".
- **The instrument always says Claude:** "Claude-powered classifiers", "prompting Claude", "Using Claude to assess the survey responses", "Claude gave a 5 to", "coded using Claude", "we instructed Claude".
- **The one observed variable always says Claude:** "the percentage of a job's tasks for which Claude is used", "as defined by the tasks Claude is observed performing", "Claude usage skews toward coding tasks", "the jobs where we observe Claude doing the most work".
- **The perceived variables always say AI**, and correctly, because the classifier prompts ask what the respondent says about AI: "perceived threat from AI", "AI-driven job displacement", "said that AI is already replacing their role", "productivity gains from AI", "the largest speedups from AI", "AI posed a significant threat", "said that AI would be a net negative", "Along which dimension does this respondent say AI improves their work?" (prompt, p. 15).
- **Captions:** five of six titles say AI; none says Claude. Claude appears in the glosses only, and only as the instrument. See `## Figure captions`.
- **The two slips are both in the close**, and both run the unusual direction — AI→Claude: "There are also signs that **Claude** empowers its users" and "Most respondents reported that **Claude** enhanced their capabilities in the form of broadening the scope of their work or speeding it up." The measure behind both sentences is defined over **AI** in the published prompt and in Figure 3's caption ("the mean inferred productivity benefit from **AI**"). The close attributes to one product a gain the instrument attributed to the technology.

**The rule this document teaches, which the reports do not.** When a study puts a *perception of AI* against an *observation of Claude*, the two words are carrying the difference between the two axes, and a reader who loses the distinction loses the finding. The title, the bullets and the Figure 1 caption all keep it; the Discussion drops it twice, in the direction that flatters. **That is the mistake to watch for in our own closes.**

## Comparisons

### Perceived against observed — the document's organising comparison, made three ways

> Elementary school teachers were less worried about their own displacement than software engineers, for example, consistent with the fact that Claude usage skews toward coding tasks.

> For every 10-percentage-point increase in exposure, perceived job threat increased by 1.3 percentage points.

> People in the top 25% of exposure mentioned the worry three times as often as those in the bottom 25%.

**Annotation.** One relationship, three depths, coarsening in the reader's favour: a **named pair** they can picture, a **slope in decision units** (per 10 percentage points), and an **extreme-quartile ratio in words** (three times). The pair comes first and carries the mechanism; the slope is the quotable number; the ratio is the memorable one. This is the same three-depth ladder as the March 2026 report's model-class comparison (domain shares → named occupations → slope per \$10) and it is the strongest structural thing in this document.

What holds it back is that the two sides are different objects — a classifier's reading of what respondents said about **AI** on the y-axis, a usage-derived measure of **Claude** on the x-axis — and no sentence says so. The comparison is a validation exercise in disguise (do stated fears track measured usage?) and would be stronger stated that way. And the three depths are not on one base: the slope and the ratio are across occupations, the named pair is two occupations, and "one fifth" earlier is across all respondents.

### The same cut at two outcomes — career stage

> We found that early-career respondents were much more likely to express concern about job displacement than senior workers.

> This depended on career stage: only 60% of early-career workers indicated that they personally benefited from AI, compared to 80% of senior professionals.

**Annotation.** One binary cut, two outcomes, one qualitative and one quantitative, forty pages apart in a fifteen-page document — and the second is introduced as a **dependency of an existing finding** ("This depended on career stage"), which is how to add a heterogeneity result without giving it its own section. Both arms are always given ("60% … compared to 80%"), never a difference; "only" marks which arm is the finding. The cut is never crossed with exposure or occupation, so the document cannot say whether it has a career-stage result or a composition result, and it does not claim to.

### Both tails against the middle — the wage quartiles

> Those in the highest- and lowest-paid occupations report the largest productivity gains, most commonly from increases in scope (doing new tasks).

> This splits somewhat across income lines.

> Some of the lowest-paid workers describe high productivity gains as well.

**Annotation.** A U-shape reported without the word U: the bullet names both tails in one clause, the body gives the high tail its own paragraph with a robustness check and the low tail its own paragraph with two illustrations. The connective "as well" is what makes the second paragraph a second tail rather than a contradiction of the first. **When a relationship is non-monotone, give each arm its own paragraph and let a connective do the joining** — the document does this twice, here and for the speedup U, and it is the more legible of its two structural habits.

### Against its own other instrument — the Economic Index echo

> It echoes a previous Economic Index finding that also favored higher-paid workers: in tasks requiring greater levels of education, Claude tended to reduce the time taken to complete a task (relative to doing it without AI) by a higher percentage.

**Annotation.** The document's only comparison to prior quantitative work, and the only one in the corpus between a **self-reported** gradient and a **measured** one. Its construction is worth copying in full: the earlier finding is restated in its own units and with its own counterfactual in parentheses, the verb claims only agreement in direction ("echoes", "also favored"), and no number from either side is put beside the other. Two instruments, one direction, no false precision. What it does not say — and what a post of ours would have to say — is whether the two orderings agree at the level of occupations, which is the checkable version of the claim.

### Against past research, in three words

> In previous research, we reported tentative signs of a slowdown in the hiring of recent graduates and early-career workers in the United States.

> We also find higher levels of economic anxiety among early-career workers, which aligns with past research.

**Annotation.** The first is a lineage sentence in the January 2026 blog's manner: what the earlier work *reported*, with its own hedge preserved ("tentative signs"), never what it proved. The second, in the close, is the same corroboration compressed to "which aligns with past research" — an unnamed body of work, on the page a hyperlink, in the PDF nothing at all. **In the PDF the phrase "past research" has no referent a reader can follow**, which is the cost of putting the citation in an anchor: the document of record loses it. Our posts name the work in the sentence.

### The exact count against the rounded one

> Our recent survey of 81,000 Claude users

> We thank the 80,508 Claude users who shared their stories.

**Annotation.** The title, the cover and both mentions in the opening round to 81,000; the precise count appears once, in the acknowledgements, as a thank-you. There is no sentence anywhere reconciling them and no analysis N for any figure. Rounding a sample size in the title is normal; **having the exact figure appear only in the acknowledgements means the document's only precise count is in the one section a reader skips.**

### The subset against the whole

> As a robustness check, we confirmed that we got the same qualitative results as in Figures 1 and 3 when we use only the 11% who had explicitly reported their line of work in their response.

**Annotation.** The comparison that matters most for the headline finding — labels the classifier inferred against labels the respondent volunteered — reported in one sentence, qualitatively, with no coefficients. See `## Findings and their caveats`. The construction to keep is the framing ("when we use only the 11% who had explicitly reported…"), which defines the robustness sample by the property that makes it more reliable rather than by its size.

## Figure captions

Six numbered figures, no tables. **PDF form throughout:** 8pt sans semibold "Figure N: sentence-case title" with **no terminal period**, on its own line directly under the image, then the gloss in 8pt sans regular, one to three sentences. **Web form:** the whole caption is italic, the title bold-italic with a terminal period added where it does not already end in a question mark. There are **no in-image chart titles quoted in this file and no numbers read from any chart**, per `room/director-2026-09-16-figure-values-ruling.md`.

> **Figure 1: Perceived job threat from AI and Observed Exposure** Percentage of respondents indicating some job threat from AI vs. the Observed Exposure measure from Massenkoff and McCrory (2026). A respondent was coded as indicating job threat if they said their role was already being replaced or substantially reduced, or that such changes were likely in the near term (coded using Claude). The green line shows a simple linear fit.

**Annotation.** The best caption in the document and the model for any classifier-derived outcome. Three sentences, three jobs: (i) what is plotted, both axes, with the x-variable **attributed to its source as a citation** ("the Observed Exposure measure from Massenkoff and McCrory (2026)") rather than named as a term of art; (ii) **the coding rule of the outcome, in full**, in the caption rather than by cross-reference — "coded as indicating job threat if they said their role was already being replaced or substantially reduced, or that such changes were likely in the near term" — with the provenance in a closing parenthesis, "(coded using Claude)"; (iii) an explicit gloss of the non-data ink, "The green line shows a simple linear fit", which names both the colour key and the estimator in eight words. A caption that carries its own coding rule can be lifted; this one can.

What it lacks, and each absence costs a sentence of the body: no N (the number of occupations plotted), no statement of whether points are weighted by respondent count, no vintage or file for the exposure measure, no unit-of-observation sentence (a point is an occupation, which the body says and the caption does not), and no interval on the fit. The body's whole comparison rests on this exhibit; the caption supports the y-axis and not the x.

> **Figure 2: Concern about economic displacement by career stage** Percentage of respondents indicating some job threat from AI, by career stage. Both fields are inferred from free-form responses using Claude-powered classifiers.

**Annotation.** Two sentences, and the second is the one to copy: "**Both fields** are inferred from free-form responses using Claude-powered classifiers." It fences the **grouping variable as well as the outcome** — the only caption in the corpus that does — which matters because a classifier-assigned group is a different kind of object from an observed one, and a reader comparing two bars needs to know that the bars themselves were inferred. Two words, "Both fields", do it.

The outcome's coding rule is compressed to "indicating some job threat from AI" rather than repeated in full from Figure 1, which is the one place the document cross-references instead of restating. What is absent: the coverage of the variable ("about half of respondents", body p. 4), the mapping from the six published prompt labels to the axis's groups, and any N.

> **Figure 3: Inferred productivity gain by occupation** The left panel shows the mean inferred productivity benefit from AI (inferred using a Claude-powered classifier) by quartile of occupational median wage from the BLS. The right panel shows the same outcome, split by major occupational group. Error bars show 95% confidence intervals.

**Annotation.** Three sentences, and the structure is the corpus's standard for a two-panel exhibit: define the first panel in full, define the second **by difference** ("the same outcome, split by major occupational group"), then gloss the uncertainty. The external variable is named with its source in the same clause ("by quartile of occupational median wage from the BLS"). "Error bars show 95% confidence intervals" is the **only statement of uncertainty anywhere in the document**, in any form.

The construct is fenced twice in one clause — "the mean **inferred** productivity benefit from AI (**inferred** using a Claude-powered classifier)" — which reads clumsily and is the right kind of clumsy: the first "inferred" is part of the variable's name, the second names the instrument. What is missing: the vintage of the BLS wage table (compare the March 2026 report's footnote, which gave "the May 2024 BLS Occupational Employment and Wage Statistics (OEWS) Tables"), whether the quartiles are quartiles of occupations or of respondents, the base (respondents with an occupation label and a productivity rating), and any N per quartile or group.

> **Figure 4: Where does the surplus from AI productivity go?** Among respondents who named a beneficiary of their AI productivity gains, the share identifying each destination.

**Annotation.** The shortest complete caption in the corpus, and the best statement of a conditional base anywhere in it: **the base is a subordinate clause placed before the quantity** — "Among respondents who named a beneficiary of their AI productivity gains, the share identifying each destination." Nineteen words carrying the population, the restriction, the unit and the quantity, in that order. A reader cannot take the shares away from their denominator because the denominator is the sentence's opening.

The title is a question, and it is the document's own framing question from the body ("A key question as AI diffuses through the economy is where the benefits will accrue"), which means the exhibit's title and the paragraph's first sentence are the same question asked twice. That repetition is deliberate and cheap.

> **Figure 5: What kind of productivity gain do users report?** Share of respondents describing each type of productivity benefit.

**Annotation.** The one caption that must not be copied. Its base — "Share of **respondents**" — is not the base the body uses for the same numbers: "48% of users who **explicitly mentioned productivity effects**", "40% of users who mentioned productivity" (p. 8). The document never reconciles the two, and since 42% of respondents "did not give a clear indication on productivity" the difference is large. Set against Figure 4, whose base is exact and conditional, this is the same document getting the same problem right once and wrong once. **The caption's base and the body's base must be the same words.**

The title is again a question, and the verb is right: "do users **report**" keeps the self-report in the exhibit's title.

> **Figure 6: Job threat from AI and speedup** Percentage of respondents who said that displacement at their own job was already happening or likely in the near term by the level of inferred speedup.

**Annotation.** One sentence, and it **restates the outcome's coding rule in full rather than cross-referencing Figure 1** — "who said that displacement at their own job was already happening or likely in the near term" — which is the repeat-verbatim discipline the corpus keeps asking for, and it is the third distinct wording the document gives the same construct (compare Figure 1's caption and the body's y-axis sentence on p. 3). Three wordings of one coding rule is one too many: a reader cannot tell whether "indicating some job threat" (Figure 2) is the same variable as this.

"the level of inferred speedup" is the running variable, and the caption does not say what its levels are. The body gives three anchors on a 1–7 scale; the exhibit is a set of bars; the binning rule is nowhere. No N per bar, no interval, and the body's claim is a shape. (The web page inserts a comma before "by the level".)

### The caption template, distilled

**What this document does that the reports do not, and that we should take:**
1. **The coding rule of a classifier-derived outcome goes in the caption, in the respondent's terms, with the model named in a closing parenthesis** — "(coded using Claude)". Figures 1 and 6.
2. **Fence the grouping variable, not only the outcome** — "Both fields are inferred…". Figure 2.
3. **The conditional base goes first, as a subordinate clause** — "Among respondents who named a beneficiary…". Figure 4.
4. **The speech verb survives into the title** — "do users report", "who said that…". Figures 5 and 6.
5. **AI in the title, Claude in the gloss:** five of six titles name AI, because what is plotted is a perception of AI; Claude appears only as the instrument that did the coding. None of the six titles names Claude. This is the inverse of the March 2026 report, where Claude was the measured object, and it is right in both cases for the same reason — **the title names what is plotted, the gloss names what produced it.**

**What it drops, and each drop costs something:**
1. **No N on any of the six exhibits**, and no sample line of any kind. The document has no N anywhere, in any caption, footnote or sentence.
2. **No uncertainty except on Figure 3.** Five of six exhibits carry a difference, a ranking or a shape with no interval; Figure 1's fitted line has no interval either.
3. **No unit-of-observation sentence** where it is least obvious: Figure 1's points are occupations, and only the body says so. September 2025's caption template calls for "what a point is" and the March 2026 report delivered it in twelve words.
4. **No binning rule** for Figure 6's bars, and no label mapping for Figure 2's groups — in both cases the underlying prompt or scale is published elsewhere in the same PDF, and the collapse is not.
5. **One caption's base contradicts its body's** (Figure 5).
6. **No vintage for either external input** (the exposure measure, the BLS wage table).

For our own posts the rule this document sharpens is: **a caption for a classifier-derived quantity must contain the coding rule, the model that applied it, the base it is conditional on, and the N — and the base must be stated in the same words the prose uses.**

## Limitations

**There is no limitations section**, and no "Caveats", "Threats to validity" or equivalent heading in fifteen pages. This is the series' settled practice and it is not ours. What is distinctive here is the *distribution*: one caveat sits beside its finding, three are pooled in the close, two are in footnotes attached to the findings they bear on, and one is in the appendix.

### 1. Beside the finding

> Our respondents were, of course, active Claude users who were willing to take a survey. This could make them more likely to report productivity benefits than the average user.

> Some 3% reported negative or neutral impacts, and 42% did not give a clear indication on productivity.

> For about half of respondents in this survey, we were able to infer career stage from their answers.

### 2. Pooled in the close, ordinal-marked

> There are key caveats to our analysis, owing to the nature of the data. First, our survey is limited to users of personal accounts on Claude.ai who chose to respond. Among other potential biases, these users could be more likely to perceive the benefits as flowing to themselves. Second, the users weren't asked directly about many of the derived variables here, so our inferences on occupation, career stage, and other variables from contextual clues could be wrong. Relatedly, because the survey is open-ended, our measures are based on what respondents happen to mention; these findings should be confirmed in structured surveys that ask about these topics directly.

### 3. In footnotes, attached to the finding each one bites on

> [3] The scale is not centered because most people say positive things about productivity, yielding almost entirely 6s and 7s on the original Likert scale.

> [4] Removing these "solopreneurs" still leaves management tied with computer and math occupations for showing the highest productivity benefit.

> [5] A major caveat, however, is that this survey went out to people with personal Claude accounts. A more representative picture would also include enterprise users, who may be more likely to say the value accrues to their employers.

### 4. In the appendix

> Some of these inferences will be wrong.

> As a robustness check, we confirmed that we got the same qualitative results as in Figures 1 and 3 when we use only the 11% who had explicitly reported their line of work in their response.

### 5. In captions

Figure 1's coding rule and "(coded using Claude)"; Figure 2's "Both fields are inferred from free-form responses"; Figure 3's "inferred using a Claude-powered classifier" and its 95% confidence intervals; Figure 4's conditional base; Figure 6's "inferred speedup".

### 6. In the opening, as a grade on the evidence

> The survey's results provide initial evidence that observed exposure (our measure of AI displacement risk) is correlated with economic concern around AI.

### Annotation

- **The pooled paragraph is the best-built limitations paragraph in the corpus, and it is three sentences long.** Its shape: an opening sentence that attributes the caveats to the data rather than to the analysis ("owing to the nature of the data"); then three caveats ordinal-marked "First", "Second", "Relatedly", each naming a **mechanism** rather than a doubt — self-selection into a personal-account survey; inference from contextual clues; measurement contingent on what was volunteered; and each of the first two carrying **the direction of its bias** ("could be more likely to perceive the benefits as flowing to themselves", "could be wrong"). It then closes on the design that would settle it: "these findings should be confirmed in structured surveys that ask about these topics directly." **A limitations paragraph that ends on the study that would resolve it is worth three that end on a hedge.** What it does not do is say which findings each caveat moves, or by how much.
- **"Relatedly" is doing real work.** The third caveat is not a third independent threat; it is the reason the second one cannot be fixed by better prompting. Open-endedness means the measure is of *mentions*, so silence is unidentified — and the document has four different silences (61% no occupation, about half no career stage, 42% no productivity indication, about three-quarters no named beneficiary). The word "Relatedly" concedes the dependency and the sentence names the general form of the problem. It is the most important sentence in the document and it contains no number.
- **The signed caveats are the good ones.** Three of the seven say which way the bias runs: the survey-taker selection on productivity, the personal-account selection on the surplus destination, and footnote 5's enterprise exclusion (the same fact, stated twice, once generally and once on the finding it moves). The others are unsigned: classifier error is "could be wrong" with no direction, and the uncentred scale is disclosed with no statement of what it does to the mean.
- **The caveat placement is structurally constrained by the layout.** Because every figure sits on the page after its lead-in with the next paragraph below the caption, the March 2026 report's best device — a caveat paragraph directly under the exhibit it threatens — is not available. The document's answer is the footnote attached to the finding's own sentence (footnotes 4 and 5), which works, and the pooled paragraph in the close, which works less well: a reader meets the exposure slope on p. 3 and the selection caveat on p. 11.
- **The five caveats a referee raises first, in order.**
  1. **No classifier validation of any kind.** Seven constructs are model outputs; there is no gold-standard subsample, no agreement rate, no inter-model check, no prompt-sensitivity test, and no statement of which Claude model ran the classifiers or when. Two of the seven prompts are published; five are described in prose. The document's own sibling publication reports an agreement check for its labels (see `wiki/reports/survey-81k-interviews-2026-03-appendix.md`); this document does not mention that such a check exists, and a reader of this document alone would not know to ask. **The one check offered — the 11% explicit-occupation subset — tests coverage, not accuracy.**
  2. **Four denominators, one restatement.** Each finding conditions on a different classifiable subset, the four coverage figures are stated once each in four different places (p. 4, p. 5, p. 7, p. 14), and no finding is reported both ways. No exhibit states its base except Figure 4.
  3. **The respondent-level sentence on p. 3 against occupation-level evidence** (see `## Findings and their caveats`, Finding 2). One sentence at the wrong unit, in the paragraph that introduces the headline comparison.
  4. **No N and no uncertainty except Figure 3.** The 1.3pp slope, the 3× ratio, the one-fifth share, the 48/40 split, the 60/80 gap and the U-shape are all reported without an interval, a sample size or a test. The U-shape's distinguishing arm is, on the document's own account of its scale, a small cell.
  5. **The education claim in the close has no measure behind it.** "people with low-wage jobs and lower levels of education also reported large productivity gains" (p. 11) — no education variable is described among the classifier outputs, no figure is split by education, and the word "education" occurs exactly twice in the document, the other time as a property of *tasks* in the January 2026 finding. A claim about a respondent characteristic that appears only in the close, with no construct and no number, is the clearest single thing in this document not to imitate: **the close carries no claim the body has not already carried with its evidence.**
- **What the document gets right about the limitations it has.** It grades its own evidence in the opening rather than in a footnote ("initial evidence"). It states the direction of its three selection biases. It names the design that would confirm its findings, in the sentence that raises the objection. It discloses that its main scale was re-coded after the original instrument saturated. And it says "Some of these inferences will be wrong" in the plainest available words, in the appendix, about the variable half its findings are built on.

## Close

### "Discussion", verbatim and in full

> The Economic Index reveals what people do with AI. But another key input for understanding AI's economic impact is to hear directly from people about their experience. The responses explored here show that people's intuitions track the usage data: they worry most about AI's effect in the jobs where we observe Claude doing the most work. We also find higher levels of economic anxiety among early-career workers, which aligns with past research.

> There are also signs that Claude empowers its users. People are most likely to talk about benefits flowing to themselves rather than to employers or AI companies. High-wage workers were the most enthusiastic about the productivity impacts of AI, but people with low-wage jobs and lower levels of education also reported large productivity gains. Most respondents reported that Claude enhanced their capabilities in the form of broadening the scope of their work or speeding it up. But users experiencing the largest speedups were also the most nervous about AI's job impacts.

> There are key caveats to our analysis, owing to the nature of the data. First, our survey is limited to users of personal accounts on Claude.ai who chose to respond. Among other potential biases, these users could be more likely to perceive the benefits as flowing to themselves. Second, the users weren't asked directly about many of the derived variables here, so our inferences on occupation, career stage, and other variables from contextual clues could be wrong. Relatedly, because the survey is open-ended, our measures are based on what respondents happen to mention; these findings should be confirmed in structured surveys that ask about these topics directly.

> Still, the interviews surface real insights about people's feelings around the economics of AI, showing how qualitative data can surface quantitative hypotheses. And the large share of economic-related concerns is a strong signal in itself.

### Annotation

- **What was learned, in one sentence, and it is a comparison.** "The responses explored here show that people's intuitions **track the usage data**: they worry most about AI's effect in the jobs where we observe Claude doing the most work." Five words of thesis — *intuitions track the usage data* — then the same claim restated concretely in the clause after the colon, with the two instruments named on either side of it. This is the sentence the whole document exists to write, it is a comparison rather than a magnitude, and it matches the title. Compare September 2025's "early AI adoption is strikingly uneven" and the absence of any such sentence in the March 2026 close. **A close should contain one sentence that is the document compressed, and the comparison is the natural shape for it.**
- **The close opens by stating what the two instruments are for, in two sentences.** "The Economic Index reveals what people do with AI. But another key input for understanding AI's economic impact is to hear directly from people about their experience." A one-line division of labour between behavioural and self-reported data, with no defence of either and no claim that one validates the other. That framing is what licenses the whole document, and it is placed in the close rather than the opening — the opening said the gap, the close says why two instruments are needed. Either placement works; having both is what makes the loop close.
- **No numbers in the close.** Not one digit in 350 words. Every finding is restated as a direction or a ranking, and none of the six exhibits is named. The same discipline as the January 2026 blog and February 2025, and the opposite of the March 2026 report, which introduced two numbers for the first time in its Discussion. **The close carries no number the body has not already carried** — here, by carrying none.
- **The second paragraph ends on its own counter-finding.** "But users experiencing the largest speedups were also the most nervous about AI's job impacts." The empowerment paragraph is not allowed to end on empowerment; the last sentence is the finding that cuts against it, in the same paragraph, unhedged. That is the March 2026 device (the same population named as both most helped and most exposed) executed in one sentence, and it is why the paragraph cannot be quoted as a promotional claim — except for its first sentence, which can. See below.
- **What comes next: one design, named, inside the caveat.** "these findings should be confirmed in structured surveys that ask about these topics directly." No dataset promise, no "future research" paragraph, no invitation to other researchers, and — unusually for the corpus — **no data-availability statement anywhere in the document**, so the reader is told what study should be run and given nothing to run it with.
- **The methodological claim is the close's real ambition.** "showing how qualitative data can surface quantitative hypotheses." The document claims a genre, not a result: open-ended interviews as a hypothesis generator for later measurement. That is a defensible and useful thing for a close to claim, and it is the honest reading of a document with no intervals and no validation. It also tells a reader how to use the findings — as candidate hypotheses — which is a stronger form of caveat than any of the three in the paragraph above it.
- **No recommendations, to anybody.** Not to policymakers, not to businesses, not to workers, not to researchers beyond the one design sentence. Given a document about who fears displacement and who captures the surplus, that is a narrowing: the surplus-destination finding is the most policy-relevant thing in it and the close mentions it only as evidence of empowerment.
- **The two sentences not to inherit.** First, "**There are also signs that Claude empowers its users**" — the naming slip discussed above (the measure is defined over AI), the vaguest evidence word in the document ("signs"), and the only sentence in the piece that reads as advocacy. Second, the last sentence: "**And the large share of economic-related concerns is a strong signal in itself.**" An unquantified share ("large") asserted as "a strong signal" of nothing specified, when the document's one unconditional share is "One fifth of the respondents in our survey" and the close does not restate it. **A close should not end on a quantity it never measured**, and this is the one place the document ends weaker than it argued.
- **Title against ending.** Title: "What 81,000 people told us about the economics of AI". Ending: "the interviews surface real insights about people's feelings around **the economics of AI**". The title's own phrase returns verbatim in the penultimate sentence, and the title's verb — *told us* — is matched in the close's second sentence, "to hear directly from people about their experience". By the house test the loop closes exactly, and it closes **in the register the title set**: what people said, not what is the case. That is the discipline the whole document depends on, and it is also why the two Claude slips and the education claim stand out — they are the only sentences the title does not license.
- **First person.** Throughout, and for three kinds of act: institutional ("we use Claude-powered classifiers", "we instructed Claude", "We give example prompts"), analytical ("We found", "we find", "We show this in Figure 1", "we measured", "we rated", "we confirmed"), and framing ("We learned", "We expand on our findings below"). House style forbids it. The substitutes are already in the document and are better than the originals: **make the exhibit the subject** ("The plot shows that, on average, people in more exposed occupations tended to express more concern"; "The left panel in Figure 3 shows that…"; "The leftmost bar shows respondents who reported that AI slowed them down"), and **make the classifier act a passive with the rule attached** ("A respondent was coded as indicating job threat if…"; "Both fields are inferred from free-form responses…"). The captions in this document are already first-person-free; they are the model for the prose.
