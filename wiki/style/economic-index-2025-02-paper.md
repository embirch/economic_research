**Register differences from the web reports, in one paragraph.** Where the launch post buys the right to a number with three paragraphs of stakes, the paper buys it with a gap in the literature and then spends the rest of the document defending the instrument. (3) The conclusion restates the headline numbers, which the launch post's close conspicuously does not.

## Opening move

### The Abstract, verbatim and in full (p.1)

> Despite widespread speculation about artificial intelligence's impact on the future of work, we lack systematic empirical evidence about how these systems are actually being used for different tasks. Here, we present a novel framework for measuring AI usage patterns across the economy. We leverage a recent privacy-preserving system [Tamkin et al., 2024] to analyze over four million Claude.ai conversations through the lens of tasks and occupations in the U.S. Department of Labor's O\*NET Database. Our analysis reveals that AI usage primarily concentrates in software development and writing tasks, which together account for nearly half of all total usage. However, usage of AI extends more broadly across the economy, with ∼ 36% of occupations using AI for at least a quarter of their associated tasks. We also analyze how AI is being used for tasks, finding 57% of usage suggests augmentation of human capabilities (e.g., learning or iterating on an output) while 43% suggests automation (e.g., fulfilling a request with minimal human involvement). While our data and methods face important limitations and only paint a picture of AI usage on a single platform, they provide an automated, granular approach for tracking AI's evolving role in the economy and identifying leading indicators of future impact as these technologies continue to advance.

### The first two paragraphs of the Introduction, verbatim (pp.1–2)

> Rapid advances in artificial intelligence suggest profound implications for the evolution of labor markets [Brynjolfsson et al., 2018b, Acemoglu, 2021, Trammell and Korinek, 2023, Hering, 2023, Comunale and Manera, 2024, Maslej et al., 2024]. Despite the importance of anticipating and preparing for these changes, we lack systematic empirical evidence about how AI systems are actually being integrated into the economy. Existing methodologies—whether developing predictive models [Webb, 2019, Eloundou et al., 2023, Kinder et al., 2024], conducting controlled studies of productivity effects [Peng et al., 2023, Noy and Zhang, 2023], or administering periodic surveys of users [Humlum and Vestergaard, 2024, Bick et al., 2024]—cannot track the dynamic relationship between advancing AI capabilities and their direct, real-world use across the economy.

> Here, we present a novel empirical framework for measuring AI usage across different tasks in the economy, drawing on privacy-preserving analysis of millions of real-world conversations on Claude.ai [Tamkin et al., 2024]. By mapping these conversations to occupational categories in the U.S. Department of Labor's O\*NET Database, we can identify not just current usage patterns, but also early indicators of which parts of the economy may be most affected as these technologies continue to advance.

### The five contributions, verbatim (pp.2–3)

> We use this framework to make five key contributions:

> 1. **Provide the first large-scale empirical measurement of which tasks are seeing AI use across the economy (Figure 1, Figure 2, and Figure 3)** Our analysis reveals highest use for tasks in software engineering roles (e.g., software engineers, data scientists, bioinformatics technicians), professions requiring substantial writing capabilities (e.g., technical writers, copywriters, archivists), and analytical roles (e.g., data scientists). Conversely, tasks in occupations involving physical manipulation of the environment (e.g., anesthesiologists, construction workers) currently show minimal use.

> 2. **Quantify the depth of AI use within occupations (Figure 4)** Only ∼ 4% of occupations exhibit AI usage for at least 75% of their tasks, suggesting the potential for deep task-level use in some roles. More broadly, ∼ 36% of occupations show usage in at least 25% of their tasks, indicating that AI has already begun to diffuse into task portfolios across a substantial portion of the workforce.

> 3. **Measure which occupational skills are most represented in human-AI conversations (Figure 5).** Cognitive skills like Reading Comprehension, Writing, and Critical Thinking show high presence, while physical skills (e.g., Installation, Equipment Maintenance) and managerial skills (e.g., Negotiation) show minimal presence—reflecting clear patterns of human complementarity with current AI capabilities.

> 4. **Analyze how wage and barrier to entry correlates with AI usage (Figure 6 and Table 2).** We find that AI use peaks in the upper quartile of wages but drops off at both extremes of the wage spectrum. Most high-usage occupations clustered in the upper quartile correspond predominantly to software industry positions, while both very high-wage occupations (e.g., physicians) and low-wage positions (e.g., restaurant workers) demonstrate relatively low usage. This pattern likely reflects either limitations in current AI capabilities, the inherent physical manipulation requirements of these roles, or both. Similar patterns emerge for barriers to entry, with peak usage in occupations requiring considerable preparation (e.g., bachelor's degree) rather than minimal or extensive training.

> 5. **Assess whether people use Claude to automate or augment tasks (Figure 7)** We find that 57% of interactions show augmentative patterns (e.g., back-and-forth iteration on a task) while 43% demonstrate automation-focused usage (e.g., performing the task directly). While this ratio varies across occupations, most occupations exhibited a mix of automation and augmentation across tasks, suggesting AI serves as both an efficiency tool and collaborative partner.

### The two closing paragraphs of the Introduction, verbatim (p.3)

> Our methods provide an automated, granular, and empirically grounded approach for tracking AI usage patterns as both capabilities and societal usage evolve. This early visibility into emerging trends gives policymakers and civil society crucial lead time to respond to shifts in how AI transforms work. However, we acknowledge multiple key limitations (discussed in Section 4.1); for example, our usage data cannot reveal how Claude's outputs are actually used in practice, and our reliance on O\*NET's static occupational descriptions means we cannot account for entirely new tasks or jobs that AI might create.

> Nevertheless, this framework offers a foundation for understanding AI's evolving impact on the economy. While our methods are imperfect, they provide a systematic way to track usage patterns and identify leading indicators of economic effects across different sectors. As AI capabilities and adoption continue to advance, we believe this kind of empirical measurement will be crucial for understanding and preparing for the technology's broader economic implications.

**Annotation.**

- **What question is posed, and how.** The title asks it — "Which Economic Tasks are Performed with AI?" — and the subtitle answers where the answer comes from — "Evidence from Millions of Claude Conversations". That is the whole framing device: a question in the interrogative, then the evidence type, then nothing else. The abstract does not repeat the question; it states the absence of an answer, twice, in almost the same words as the Introduction's second sentence ("we lack systematic empirical evidence about how these systems are actually being used for different tasks" / "…about how AI systems are actually being integrated into the economy"). The repetition is deliberate and is the paper's opening move: **the gap, not the stake.** Compare the launch post, which opens on a forecast ("AI systems will have a major impact on the ways people work") and converts it into a reason to measure. Here the reason to measure is that nobody has measured. An economist reading the abstract knows within two sentences what would make the paper wrong: someone else having the data.
- **How the gap is made specific.** The Introduction does not assert the gap; it enumerates what exists and says what each existing thing cannot do. Three methodologies, each with its citations, all subordinated inside a single em-dash aside — "whether developing predictive models […], conducting controlled studies of productivity effects […], or administering periodic surveys of users […]" — and then one verb phrase disqualifies all three at once: "cannot track the dynamic relationship between advancing AI capabilities and their direct, real-world use across the economy." One sentence, three literatures, one shared limitation. This is the construction to copy when a post has to say what Anthropic left open: name the existing approaches, then name the one property none of them has.
- **Who is said to be affected.** "labor markets", "the economy", "the future of work" — aggregates, exactly as in the launch post, and no named worker, sector or region. The people named as *acting* on the findings are different: "policymakers and civil society" get "crucial lead time", and §4.2 adds "economists, and other stakeholders". The affected party is the economy; the addressed party is the policy reader. The paper never claims a person will be helped.
- **What the data is said uniquely to show.** Three claims, each pinned to a property of the evidence rather than to a result. "the first large-scale empirical measurement of which tasks are seeing AI use across the economy" (contribution 1); "over four million Claude.ai conversations" (abstract); and the negative differentiator from §2, that this is usage rather than exposure. Note the hedges inside the superlative: *large-scale empirical measurement*, not *first measurement*; and "which tasks are seeing AI use", not *how much AI affects tasks*. The abstract's own summary of the contribution is methodological to the last clause — "they provide an automated, granular approach for tracking AI's evolving role in the economy and identifying leading indicators of future impact". As in the launch post, the instrument is the claim.
- **How soon the first number appears.** In the abstract, the first magnitude is "over four million Claude.ai conversations" at word ~57, and the first percentage is "∼ 36%" at word ~111 — just past halfway. In the body, the first number is the "∼ 4%" of contribution 2, about 260 words into the Introduction; the first two paragraphs contain no quantity at all, not even "millions" in numeral form. So the paper front-loads its numbers by one screen relative to the launch post (which waits ~150 words for "roughly 36%" but has only ~1,700 words to play with) and delays them by four pages relative to §3, where they are earned.
- **The contributions list as a form.** Five items, each with a **bold declarative heading naming the epistemic act** — Provide, Quantify, Measure, Analyze, Assess — followed by an unbolded body giving the result. The verb grades the strength of the claim: *quantify* and *measure* for the two scalar results, *analyze* for the correlational one, *assess* for the classification, *provide* for the descriptive distribution. Nothing is *shown*, *demonstrated* or *established*. Every heading also carries its figure numbers in parentheses, so the list doubles as a map: a reader can go from any claim to its exhibit without the table of contents the paper does not have. This is the closest thing in the corpus to a template for "findings in the order of the pre-registration, each with its figure", and it is worth copying in that exact form — verb, claim, figure reference, then the number in the body.
- **Where the hedge sits in each contribution.** Item 1: "currently show minimal use" (temporal fence on the null). Item 2: "suggesting the potential for deep task-level use" and "indicating that AI has already begun to diffuse" (both convert the scalar into an inference and mark it as such). Item 3: "reflecting clear patterns of human complementarity" (the only unhedged interpretation in the list). Item 4: "This pattern likely reflects either limitations in current AI capabilities, the inherent physical manipulation requirements of these roles, or both" — a three-way disjunction that declines to choose, the same refusal the launch post makes in its third bullet. Item 5: "suggesting AI serves as both an efficiency tool and collaborative partner". The pattern is uniform: number, then a participle ("suggesting", "indicating", "reflecting") that carries the interpretation and marks it as derived rather than observed. The participle is the paper's main device for separating observation from conjecture, and it is cheaper than the launch post's "This likely reflects" because it does not need a new sentence.
- **The limitation inside the opening.** The Introduction's penultimate paragraph concedes limitations *before* the paper has reported anything, names two of them concretely ("cannot reveal how Claude's outputs are actually used in practice"; "cannot account for entirely new tasks or jobs that AI might create"), and forward-references the section where the rest live. Then the final paragraph recovers with "Nevertheless" and the flattest possible concession — "While our methods are imperfect". Two paragraphs, one down, one up, in that order. The launch post never concedes anything above the fold.
- **First person.** Used for every act in the paper, including the findings: "we present", "we leverage", "we lack", "We use this framework", "We find that", "Our analysis reveals", "we believe". The launch post reserves the first person for institutional commitments and leaves findings agentless. The paper does not; "we find" is the standard finding verb throughout. Our house style forbids the construction, which means the paper's most imitable sentence shapes have to be rebuilt agentless: "we find that AI use peaks in the upper quartile of wages" becomes "usage peaks in the upper quartile of wages", and "We find that 57% of interactions show augmentative patterns" becomes "57% of interactions show augmentative patterns". Note that the deletion costs nothing — which is itself the argument for the rule.
- **Claude against AI, in the opening.** The abstract measures "AI usage" and sources it to "Claude.ai conversations": the measured noun is AI, the platform noun is Claude. Contribution 5 is the single place where the opening says Claude of a measurement — "Assess whether people use Claude to automate or augment tasks" — and it is the only contribution whose heading names the product. By our standard (question says AI, every finding says Claude) the paper is less disciplined than its own launch post, where "37.2% of queries sent to Claude" keeps the number on the platform. Read this opening for its structure; read the launch post for its naming.

## Findings and their caveats

### Finding 1 — concentration by occupational category (§3.1, pp.5–6)

> Our analysis reveals that computer-related tasks see the largest amount of AI usage, followed by writing tasks in educational and communication contexts. To understand broader patterns, we group these tasks according to O\*NET's occupational framework—first mapping them to specific occupations (such as Computer Network Architects) and then to broader occupational categories (like Computer and Mathematical Occupations). Figure 2 illustrates this distribution across occupational categories, showing the most common occupations and tasks within each group, while Figure 3 compares these usage patterns to the actual distribution of these occupations in the U.S. workforce.

The five-bullet list that follows Figure 3 (p.6), verbatim:

> Overall, the data reveal several insights:
>
> - In line with our task-level findings, Computer and Mathematical occupations show the highest associated AI usage rate, comprising 37.2% of all queries.
> - Arts, Design, Entertainment, Sports, and Media occupations were second most common (10.3%), likely reflecting the prevalence of marketing, writing, and other kinds of content generation in Claude.ai traffic.
> - Education occupations were also highly represented, including under Educational Instruction and Library Occupations as well as subject-specific occupations such as Life, Physical, and Social Science Occupations.
> - A significant portion of Claude.ai traffic fell under business-relevant occupations: Business and Financial Occupations, Office and Administrative Support Occupations, and Management Occupations.
> - Unsurpringly, occupations requiring physical labor were least present, for example Transportation and Material Moving Occupations; Healthcare Support Occupations; and Farming, Fishing, and Forestry Occupations.

And the caveat paragraph attached to the same finding (p.5), verbatim:

> It is worth noting that the occupational classification of a conversation does not necessarily mean the user was a professional in that field. For instance, while some nutrition-related queries might come from dietitians, others likely come from individuals seeking personal dietary advice. This widespread access to AI assistance for traditionally professional tasks—even if the assistance is not perfect—could have significant implications for these fields, though analyzing such impacts lies outside the scope of this study. We discuss these limitations further in Section 4.1.

**Annotation.** The aggregation ladder is stated before the number: tasks → occupations → categories, each rung named with a worked instance ("such as Computer Network Architects", "like Computer and Mathematical Occupations"), so the reader knows what a category share is a share *of* before being shown one. Only two of the five bullets carry a percentage, and both are ranked rather than absolute ("highest", "second most common"); the other three are ordinal statements with no number at all ("also highly represented", "A significant portion", "least present"). The paper is willing to describe a distribution without quantifying every point of it, which keeps the two numbers it does give load-bearing. Units are named once, in the first bullet — "37.2% of all queries" — and never restated, so bullets two to five inherit the denominator; the launch post repeats "of queries" each time. "likely reflecting" in bullet two marks the mechanism as conjecture in two words; "Unsurpringly" in bullet five does the same work the launch post's "Unsurprisingly" does, pre-empting the objection that the low end is an artefact by conceding it is exactly what anyone would predict (the misspelling is the paper's).

The caveat paragraph is the most instructive thing in §3.1 and has no equivalent in the launch post. It is placed *before* the finding's own bullet list, attached to the construct rather than to the result, and it does three things in four sentences: it withdraws the inference a reader would make ("does not necessarily mean the user was a professional in that field"); it makes the failure concrete with a worked pair, the dietitian and the person seeking dietary advice; and it then turns the defect into a research question ("This widespread access to AI assistance for traditionally professional tasks…could have significant implications for these fields") while explicitly declining to pursue it ("lies outside the scope of this study"). The construction *limitation → the interesting thing it implies → refusal to claim it* is worth copying exactly. "It is worth noting that" is the ritual opener; everything after it is specific.

### Finding 2 — depth of task coverage within occupations (§3.1, p.7)

> **How many tasks per occupation show AI usage?** To assess the depth of AI integration across occupations, we examined what fraction of each occupation's tasks appeared in our Clio run. As shown in Figure 4, we find that AI task use follows a heavily skewed distribution. Only ∼ 4% of occupations show usage for at least 75% of their associated tasks—for example, in Foreign Language and Literature Teachers (75% of tasks), we observe AI usage for tasks related to collaborating with colleagues on teaching issues and planning course content, though not for activities like writing grant proposals or maintaining student records. Just ∼ 11% of occupations show usage for half or more of their tasks, as illustrated by Marketing Managers (50% of tasks) where we see AI usage in tasks involving market research analysis and strategy development, but not in activities like product specification consultation or trade show coordination. The picture broadens at lower thresholds, where ∼ 36% of occupations see at least a quarter of their tasks with some usage of AI—exemplified by Physical Therapists (25% of tasks), where we observe AI interactions in tasks related to research and patient education, but not in hands-on treatment or home care instruction. This distribution suggests that while AI could be touching many occupations today, deep integration across most tasks within any given occupation remains rare for now. Rather than completely automating entire job roles, present-day AI appears to be primarily used for specific tasks within occupations.

The privacy threshold that defines the measure, from the footnote on the same page, verbatim:

> Clio operates with a set of privacy measures that protect the activity of individual users from appearing in Clio outputs. In this work, we only examine tasks that have accumulated at least 15 separate conversations spread across a minimum of five different user accounts—ensuring that a task must appear multiple times across different users to appear in our dataset. For more information on our Clio's privacy controls, please see Tamkin et al. [2024].

**Annotation.** The finding is a curve, and the paper reports it by walking down it: three thresholds (75%, 50%, 25%), three shares (∼4%, ∼11%, ∼36%), and — the move that makes this the best-written finding in the paper — **three worked exemplars, each giving both what is used and what is not**. Foreign Language and Literature Teachers: collaborating with colleagues and planning course content, *not* writing grant proposals or maintaining student records. Marketing Managers: market research and strategy, *not* product specification consultation or trade show coordination. Physical Therapists: research and patient education, *not* hands-on treatment or home care instruction. The paired positive/negative exemplar tells the reader what the threshold *is* in a way the percentage cannot, and the third pair carries the mechanism (hands-on work) without asserting it. The launch post reports the same finding as a bare pair of scalars with no figure; the paper reports it as a distribution with a figure, an intermediate point at 50% that the post drops, and nine named tasks.

The hedging is layered and worth counting. The symbol "∼" fences all three shares. "Only", "Just" and "The picture broadens" grade the three in advance, so the reader is told how to read each number before reading it — the same pre-interpretation the launch post performs with "a slight lean towards augmentation". Then the two closing sentences are built to keep the null from hardening: "This distribution suggests that while AI could be touching many occupations today, deep integration…remains rare for now" carries four hedges in one sentence ("suggests", "could be", "remains rare", "for now"), and "present-day AI appears to be primarily used for specific tasks within occupations" carries two more ("appears", "primarily") plus a temporal fence in the compound adjective. The paper never says no job is automated; it says that at this measurement, deep coverage is rare. The launch post's equivalent — "there wasn't evidence in this dataset of jobs being entirely automated" — fences with the dataset; the paper fences with time.

Note also what the measure actually is: "what fraction of each occupation's tasks appeared in our Clio run", where appearing requires 15 conversations across five accounts. The definition is in the sentence; the threshold is in the footnote; and Figure 4's caption repeats the threshold in words ("five or more unique user accounts and fifteen or more conversations"). The number that decides whether a task counts is stated three times in three registers, which is how a paper makes a construct auditable without interrupting the finding.

### Finding 3 — occupational skills (§3.2, pp.7–8)

> Intuitively, skills requiring physical interaction, including Installation, Equipment Maintenance, and Repairing, showed the lowest prevalence in Claude.ai traffic. By contrast, cognitive skills such as Critical Thinking, Reading Comprehension, Programming, and Writing had the highest prevalence. However, our analysis captures only whether a skill was exhibited in Claude's responses, not whether that skill was central to the user's purpose or was performed at an expert level. For instance, while Active Listening appears as the second most common skill, this likely reflects Claude's default conversational behaviors—such as rephrasing user inputs and asking clarifying questions—rather than users specifically seeking out listening-focused interactions.

**Annotation.** A finding with no numbers at all: two ranked ends of a distribution, named by their members, plus a figure. Then the caveat, in the same paragraph, introduced by "However" — and the caveat is *larger than the finding*. It states precisely what the measurement is ("whether a skill was exhibited in Claude's responses") and precisely what it is not ("whether that skill was central to the user's purpose or was performed at an expert level"), and then spends its longest sentence dismantling one of its own data points: Active Listening ranks second, and that rank is attributed to the instrument rather than to the world ("this likely reflects Claude's default conversational behaviors—such as rephrasing user inputs and asking clarifying questions"). This is the single most copyable paragraph in the paper. A finding that names its own most misleading component, gives the mechanism of the artefact, and does so before any reader can raise it, is worth more than a limitations section. "Intuitively" opens the paragraph the way "Unsurpringly" opens bullet five — conceding that the expected part is expected, so the attention goes to the unexpected part. The measured subject is Claude throughout ("exhibited in Claude's responses", "Claude's default conversational behaviors"), which is the naming discipline our house rule asks for, and it appears here because the caveat is about the model.

### Finding 4 — wage and barrier to entry (§3.3, pp.8–9)

> **Wage** Figure 6 shows how usage of AI varies by the median wage of that occupation. We find that usage peaks in the upper quartile of wages with computational occupations such as Computer Programmers and Web Developers. Occupations at both extremes of the wage scale show lower usage. For example, waiters and anesthesiologists (low- and high-wage occupations, respectively) are among the least represented in the data, which is in line with our findings that skills requiring physical interaction are least common in our data.

> **Barrier to entry** Occupations in the O\*NET database are placed in Job Zones ranging from one to five, categories defined by the amount of preparation needed for a human to perform the duties of a given occupation. Occupations requiring higher levels of education, experience, and training are placed in higher Job Zones. As Job Zone increases from one to four, so does the representation of that zone in our data, peaking at Zone 4: Considerable Preparation Needed, which comprises occupations that typically require a four-year bachelor's degree. However, representation falls for Job Zone 5: Extensive Preparation Needed, where most occupations require advanced degrees. These results align with our wage analysis which showed high usage in the upper quartile of jobs (e.g., software developers) but low usage at the highest end of the spectrum (e.g., anesthesiologists and gynecologists), which tend to require advanced training. These results make clear that human barriers to entry may be significantly different than barriers to language models. See Appendix D.2 and Table 2 for the complete results.

And the numbers themselves, from Appendix D.2 (p.24), verbatim:

> The analysis reveals peak AI usage in Job Zone 4 (requiring bachelor's degree-level preparation), with a representation ratio of 1.50, indicating 50% higher usage than expected given its baseline distribution. Both minimal preparation (Job Zone 1) and extensive preparation roles (Job Zone 5) show lower usage rates, with representation ratios of 0.40 and 0.58 respectively. Job Zones 2 and 3 demonstrate usage rates more closely aligned with their baseline distribution (ratios of 0.78 and 0.94). This pattern suggests AI tools are most readily used in occupations requiring substantial but not elite-level specialization, likely due to these roles involving structured, analytical tasks well-suited to current AI capabilities. The lower usage in both minimal preparation roles and highly specialized positions may occur due to many of those jobs requiring physical manipulation of the environment. For the most specialized tasks, regulations and access to sensitive information (e.g. in healthcare roles) may also play a role in limited AI usage.

**Annotation.** Two run-in paragraphs, both describing a non-monotonic relationship in words with no coefficient, no correlation and no functional form — exactly as the launch post does. The shape is carried by named occupations at each region: Computer Programmers and Web Developers at the peak; waiters and anesthesiologists at the two tails, in a parenthetical that spells out which is which ("low- and high-wage occupations, respectively") so the reader cannot mis-assign them. The wage paragraph then links itself back to Finding 3 — "which is in line with our findings that skills requiring physical interaction are least common in our data" — making the wage result corroborative rather than free-standing, and noting twice in one clause that the claim is about the data ("in our data").

The Job Zone paragraph is the one place in the body where the paper carries a *ratio-style* finding, and it does so without the ratio: "peaking at Zone 4… However, representation falls for Job Zone 5" and then a pointer, "See Appendix D.2 and Table 2 for the complete results." The representation ratios (1.50, 0.40, 0.58, 0.78, 0.94) exist only in the appendix and in Table 2. This is the same delegation the launch post performs by leaving representation ratios in image alt text, and it has the same cost: the body sentence a reader remembers has no magnitude in it. Note what the paper puts in the body instead — the interpretive sentence "These results make clear that human barriers to entry may be significantly different than barriers to language models", whose hedge placement is worth studying: *make clear that* (strong) governs *may be significantly different* (weak), so the sentence asserts confidently that something is uncertain. The appendix paragraph then stacks three conjectural mechanisms in three sentences, each separately marked — "likely due to", "may occur due to", "may also play a role" — and offers them as alternatives rather than a ranking, which is the same refusal to adjudicate as contribution 4.

### Finding 5 — automation against augmentation (§3.4, pp.9–10)

> While the previous analyses reveal which tasks are seeing the most AI usage, they do not tell us how AI is being used for these tasks. A key distinction in the economics literature is between automation—where technology substitutes for human labor—and augmentation—where technology complements and enhances human capabilities [Autor, 2015]. To understand which pattern is more prevalent, we used Clio to classify conversations into one of five different collaboration patterns grouped into automative vs. augmentative behaviors, listed in Table 1.

> Both augmentative and automative collaboration behaviors are present in users' with Claude.ai interactions, with slightly more conversations being labeled as augmentative (57%) than automative (43%). That said, we note an important caveat that users might edit and adjust the response they receive from Claude outside the chat window, suggesting that the true proportion of augmentative conversations may be even higher. In addition, even automation of simple tasks can serve to enhance human capabilities when embedded within broader human-directed workflows.

> **Automative behaviors** A majority of the Directive conversations consisted of writing and other content generation tasks. There was also a high percentage of Directive conversations in the business-relevant tasks such as "Draft and optimize professional business email communications" and schoolwork-related clusters such as "Solve diverse geometry problems with calculations and proofs." The vast majority of the Feedback Loop conversations were related to coding and debugging, where the user repeatedly relayed the error they received back to the model.

> **Augmentative behaviors** Task Iteration conversations often involved front-end development (tasks such as "Assist with web development tasks and UI improvements" and "Create and modify landing pages and key website components") as well as professional communication tasks (for example, "Optimize resumes, cover letters, and job applications" and "Assist with professional and academic writing and communication"). The highest percentage of Learning conversations occurs in general education tasks such as "Explain and analyze martial law implementation and impacts," "Offer gastroenterology and digestive health advice," and "Assist with microcontroller programming and embedded systems projects". Validation was the smallest category of conversations, and was nearly all concentrated to tasks discussing language translations.

**Annotation.** The definitional sentence is the model for introducing a two-pole construct: both poles defined by em-dash apposition inside one sentence, both sourced to the same citation ("[Autor, 2015]"), and the definitions given in the vocabulary of the discipline ("substitutes for human labor" / "complements and enhances human capabilities") rather than in product terms. The construct is named *collaboration patterns* — five of them — and the two poles are groupings of those five, which is the terminology our house standard treats as authoritative.

The finding itself is a single clause: "slightly more conversations being labeled as augmentative (57%) than automative (43%)". Three fences in eleven words. "slightly more" pre-interprets the magnitude so 57% cannot read as a landslide. "being labeled as" attributes the category to the classifier, not to the world — the paper says these conversations were *labelled* augmentative, not that they *were* augmentative, and that one participial phrase is the difference between a measurement and a claim. And both shares are given rather than one plus a complement, so the reader performs the subtraction and sees how narrow it is.

Then the caveat, in the same paragraph and immediately after: users "might edit and adjust the response they receive from Claude outside the chat window, suggesting that the true proportion of augmentative conversations may be even higher", plus a second, conceptual objection that automation and augmentation are not exclusive ("even automation of simple tasks can serve to enhance human capabilities when embedded within broader human-directed workflows"). **The direction of the bias is named.** That is what makes the caveat actionable, and it is the same construction the launch post uses with the edited memo — but here it sits one sentence from the number rather than 400 words away, which is the improvement. The paper also declines to say what the bias would do to the headline figure; it says only that the true proportion "may be even higher", with no bound. A referee would ask for the bound.

The two run-in paragraphs that follow give composition without percentages — "A majority of", "a high percentage of", "The vast majority of", "The highest percentage of", "the smallest category", "nearly all concentrated" — six ordinal quantifiers and not one number, each anchored to task strings quoted verbatim from the classification. When a post cannot put a defensible percentage on a subcomponent, this is how to describe it: rank it, then name the thing.

### The hedge vocabulary, graded

Read as a set, the paper's hedges fall into six devices, roughly from weakest commitment to strongest.
1. **The symbol.** "∼" before every share derived from the depth curve (∼4%, ∼11%, ∼36%, ∼57%, ∼20K, ∼1,500). Used where the threshold, not the arithmetic, is the arbitrary part. Exact percentages (37.2%, 10.3%, 57%, 43%, 1.50) take no tilde.
2. **Attribution to the classifier.** "being labeled as augmentative", "captures only whether a skill was exhibited in Claude's responses", "our analysis captures only", "appeared in our Clio run".
3. **Attribution to the sample.** "in our data" (used four times in §3.3 alone), "in Claude.ai traffic", "in the data".
4. **The participle carrying the inference.** "suggesting", "indicating", "reflecting", "highlighting" — always after the number, never before.
5. **Modals for mechanism.** "likely reflects", "may occur due to", "may also play a role", "could have significant implications", "might come from".
6. **Temporal fences.** "currently show minimal use", "present-day AI", "remains rare for now", "has not yet materialized".

### Claude against AI, across the whole paper

The measured noun in the body is almost always **AI**: "AI usage", "AI task use", "AI use peaks", "AI usage rate", "the largest amount of AI usage". **Claude** names the platform the data came from ("Claude.ai conversations", "Claude.ai traffic", "in our Clio run"), the classifier ("we use Claude to classify"), and the object of a caveat ("Claude's default conversational behaviors", "Claude's outputs", "the response they receive from Claude"). The substitution is therefore *AI for the quantity, Claude for the source and for the doubt* — the reverse of the discipline our house rule imposes, where the question says AI and every finding says Claude. The paper's own launch post is the better example on this point: "37.2% of queries sent to Claude". One passage in the paper does it our way, and it is the skills caveat in §3.2; one contribution heading does, and it is number 5. When drafting, take this paper's structure and the launch post's nouns.

## Comparisons

Every comparison that carries a finding, quoted and annotated.

### To the three existing literatures, as a disqualification

> Existing methodologies—whether developing predictive models [Webb, 2019, Eloundou et al., 2023, Kinder et al., 2024], conducting controlled studies of productivity effects [Peng et al., 2023, Noy and Zhang, 2023], or administering periodic surveys of users [Humlum and Vestergaard, 2024, Bick et al., 2024]—cannot track the dynamic relationship between advancing AI capabilities and their direct, real-world use across the economy.

**Annotation.** The paper's opening comparison and its licence to exist. Three alternatives, each with citations, each named by what it *does* (predict, run controlled studies, survey) rather than by what it concludes, and all three disqualified by one shared incapacity. The comparison is *of method to method*, and the differentiator is the evidence type — the same move the launch post makes in three short sentences ("We don't survey people on their AI use, or attempt to forecast the future; instead, we have direct data"). The paper's version is one sentence, denser, and sourced; the post's is punchier. Both put the negation before the positive claim.

### To the task-based framework, as inheritance

> Most notably, Autor et al. [2003], Autor [2013] argue for modeling labor markets through the lens of discrete tasks which can be performed by either human workers or machines—for example, debugging code or cutting hair. Building on this framework, Autor [2015] shows that while technologies automate some tasks, they often augment human capabilities in others due to complementarity between humans and machines, leading to higher demand for labor. In addition Acemoglu and Restrepo [2018] use this framework to explore a model where automation technologies can create entirely new tasks in addition to displacing old tasks.

**Annotation.** Three sentences, three papers, one commitment. The abstraction is made concrete by a two-item example with maximal spread — "debugging code or cutting hair" — which is the same device as the launch post's four occupations sharing visual pattern recognition, and cheaper. The second sentence is doing structural work: it imports the automation/augmentation distinction from Autor [2015] *here*, in the literature review, so that §3.4 can use it later without redefining it. Note that the theory is stated as what the cited authors argue and show, never as fact, and that the third sentence flags the mechanism the paper's own O\*NET-based method cannot see (newly created tasks) — the limitation in §4.1 is therefore pre-installed in §2.

### To the exposure forecasts, against the paper's own numbers

> Webb [2019] analyzes the overlap between patent documents and job task descriptions to predict the "exposure" of tasks to AI, finding highest exposure in high-education, high-wage occupations—a pattern partially reflected in our empirical usage data, though we find peak usage in mid-to-high wage occupations rather than at the highest wage levels.

> Eloundou et al. [2023] also consider exposure of tasks to language models, using language models themselves to obtain more granular estimates of exposure at the level of individual tasks—an approach we follow in our work. When considering the impacts of language model-powered software, they conclude that around half of all tasks in the economy could one day be automated by language models. While our empirical usage data shows lower current adoption (∼ 36% of occupations using AI for at least a quarter of their tasks), the patterns of usage across tasks largely align with their predictions, particularly in showing high usage for software development and content creation tasks.

**Annotation.** The paper's signature comparison form, used twice in §2 and twice more in §4.2: *prior prediction, then our measurement, then a verdict on the difference, with the direction of the difference named*. Both are split verdicts — "partially reflected… though"; "shows lower current adoption… the patterns…largely align" — and both are careful that the two quantities are not the same quantity. "exposure" appears in quotation marks the first time it is used, which marks it as the other literature's construct; the paper's own construct is "usage", never in quotation marks. The distinction between exposure and usage is what the whole comparison rests on, and it is established by punctuation.

### The same comparison again, in the Discussion, with a number attached

> Comparison to predictive studies Our empirical findings both validate and challenge previous predictions about AI's impact on work. Webb [2019] predicted highest AI exposure in occupations around the 90th wage percentile, while we find peak usage in mid-to-high wage occupations, with notably lower usage at both extremes of the wage distribution. This pattern suggests that factors beyond technical feasibility—such as implementation costs, regulatory barriers, and organizational readiness—may be tempering adoption in the highest-wage sectors. Eloundou et al. [2023] predicted that 80% of U.S. workers could have at least 10% of their work tasks affected by language models; by contrast, our empirical data shows current adoption at ∼ 57% of occupations using AI for at least 10% of their tasks—lower than predicted but potentially trending toward their forecast as capabilities improve and adoption barriers decrease. However, their prediction of higher usage in industries like healthcare has not yet materialized in our data, and we observe noticeably higher usage in scientific applications than they expected, highlighting the impact of both sector-specific barriers to diffusion as well as unexpected developments in model capabilities. These discrepancies between predictions and actual usage underscore the importance of empirical measurement in understanding AI's evolving economic impact and suggest that technical feasibility alone may not be sufficient to predict where and how AI will be adopted across the economy.

**Annotation.** The best-constructed passage in the paper, and the one with no counterpart in the launch post. Its architecture: a thesis in seven words that refuses to pick a side ("both validate and challenge"); then four specific prediction-against-measurement pairs, each stated as *number predicted / number observed / direction of the gap*; then a mechanism for the gap, marked as conjecture and offered as a list of three candidates with no ranking ("implementation costs, regulatory barriers, and organizational readiness"); then a conclusion about *method* rather than about the economy ("underscore the importance of empirical measurement"). Two details worth copying. First, the ∼57% at 10% of tasks is a number that appears *nowhere else in the paper* — it is generated specifically to make the comparison commensurable with Eloundou et al.'s threshold, and the paper says so by stating both thresholds in the same sentence. If a post has to compare against a published number, this is how: re-cut your own data to the other paper's threshold, and put both thresholds in the sentence. Second, "80% … ∼57% … lower than predicted but potentially trending toward their forecast" states the gap and then offers a reading under which the gap closes, hedged twice ("potentially", "as capabilities improve") and conditioned on two named things. The comparison carries the finding, and the finding is that feasibility does not predict adoption.

### Between two measurement choices, as a robustness check

> To assess the robustness of our methodology for measuring AI usage across occupations, we examined if our focus on total conversation counts could systematically bias results toward occupations that tend to generate many short interactions (e.g. programmers debugging code through multiple separate conversations). To address this, we compared two different approaches to analyze our task-level classifications: measuring the total number of conversations per task (as discussed in the main text) vs. measuring the unique user accounts per task.

> As shown in Figure 10, the patterns of AI usage across occupations remain remarkably stable regardless of which approach we use. This suggests our findings are not affected by variation in interaction patterns across occupations.

**Annotation.** A comparison used to *defend* a finding rather than to make one, and the form is exemplary: name the specific bias you are worried about, name the population it would favour (programmers, with the mechanism — many short debugging conversations), name the alternative measure, report the comparison, state the conclusion in one sentence. Everything is in the subjunctive until the result arrives: "could systematically bias", "if our focus". The report of the result is qualitative — "remain remarkably stable" — with the evidence delegated to the figure and no correlation given, which is the one thing here not to copy; our standards would ask for the statistic.

### Between the conversation-level and cluster-level pipelines, graded by aggregation level

> We find strong correlation between the exposure of occupational categories determined via direct assignment and via clusters at various aggregation levels. When comparing direct assignment to cluster-based approaches, we observe minimum correlation values of 0.95 (Pearson), 0.95 (Spearman), and 0.82 (Kendall's Tau-b).

> We find reasonable correlation between occupational exposure patterns whether measured through direct conversation assignment or through cluster-based assignment at different aggregation levels. […] Specifically, when comparing direct assignment to cluster-based approaches, we observe minimum correlation values of 0.70 (Pearson), 0.58 (Spearman), and 0.46 (Kendall's Tau-b). These correlations are calculated only for occupations that have associated tasks identified by at least one method.

> We find relatively weak correlation between task exposure patterns when comparing direct assignment to cluster-based approaches. When comparing these methods, we observe minimum correlation values of 0.47 (Pearson), 0.22 (Spearman), and 0.18 (Kendall's Tau-b). These correlations are calculated only for tasks identified by at least one method.

> The relatively weak correlation at the task level can be attributed to two key factors. First, there is intrinsic noise in the O\*NET task dataset itself, with many tasks being highly similar or overlapping. For example, tasks like "Diagnose, troubleshoot, and resolve hardware, software, or other network system problems, and replace defective components when necessary" and "Perform initial debugging procedures by reviewing configuration files, logs, or code pieces to determine breakdown source" are functionally very similar but listed as distinct tasks. Second, there exists a many-to-many relationship between conversations and tasks (a single conversation might involve multiple tasks) and between tasks and occupations (a single task might map to multiple occupations). This means that uncertainty expands at the task level but naturally narrows as we aggregate upward to occupational categories; therefore, we see stronger correlations at higher levels of aggregation. This pattern suggests that while cluster-based analysis may not perfectly reconstruct task-level assignments, it remains reliable for analyzing broader occupational trends.

**Annotation.** The same comparison run at three grains — category, occupation, task — with the verdict adjective graded to match the numbers and stated *before* them: "strong" (0.95/0.95/0.82), "reasonable" (0.70/0.58/0.46), "relatively weak" (0.47/0.22/0.18). Three correlation families are reported each time, and the *minimum* across aggregation levels is reported rather than the best, which is the conservative choice and is said to be the choice ("minimum correlation values"). The denominators are stated ("calculated only for occupations that have associated tasks identified by at least one method"). Then the weak result is explained rather than buried, with two mechanisms, one of them demonstrated by quoting two near-duplicate O\*NET task strings in full so the reader can see the problem — and the closing sentence draws the boundary of validity rather than defending the method everywhere: "may not perfectly reconstruct task-level assignments, it remains reliable for analyzing broader occupational trends". **One claim tested at more than one level, with the level at which it fails named.** This is the passage to re-read before writing a robustness section.

### Between two models of the same family

> Our analysis reveals clear specialization in how these models are used (Figure 8). Relative to Sonnet, Opus sees higher usage for creative and educational work (e.g., "Produce and perform in film, TV, theater, and music," "Manage book and document publishing process," "Design and develop comprehensive educational curricula and materials," "Conduct academic research and disseminate findings"). These patterns align with widespread user observations about Opus' relatively unique character and writing style. By contrast, Claude 3.5 Sonnet (new) is preferred for coding and software development tasks (e.g., "Develop and maintain software applications and websites" and "Program and debug computer systems and machinery"), consistent with external evaluations highlighting its relatively strong coding abilities.

**Annotation.** A within-platform comparison stated entirely in relative terms — "Relative to Sonnet", "By contrast", "is preferred for" — with no share for either model and six task strings quoted as the evidence. Both halves are then corroborated against something outside the data: the Opus pattern against "widespread user observations" (footnoted to Anthropic's own claude-character post), the Sonnet pattern against "external evaluations". The corroboration is the finding's only validation, and the paper says as much by using "align with" and "consistent with" rather than any causal verb. The sample composition that makes the comparison legible — "approximately 54% Claude 3.5 Sonnet (new) conversations and 46% Claude 3 Opus conversations" — is in Appendix B.6, not here.

### Against official statistics, delegated to a figure

> Figure 2 illustrates this distribution across occupational categories, showing the most common occupations and tasks within each group, while Figure 3 compares these usage patterns to the actual distribution of these occupations in the U.S. workforce.

**Annotation.** As in the launch post: the comparison that a reader will remember — usage share against workforce share — is set up in half a sentence and then handed to the exhibit. The paper does slightly better than the post by naming the benchmark's source inside the caption with a citation ("according to the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics [U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, 2024]") rather than only in alt text, but it still states no representation ratio in prose anywhere for occupational categories. The only representation ratios the paper prints are the Job Zone ones in Table 2. Worth noting as a limit of both forms: two of the three most quotable comparisons in this line of work exist only inside figures.

### Small comparisons that do work in one clause

> for example, debugging code or cutting hair

> waiters and anesthesiologists (low- and high-wage occupations, respectively)

> providing instructions for a basic omelette does not indicate culinary expertise

> human barriers to entry may be significantly different than barriers to language models

**Annotation.** Four one-clause comparisons, each carrying an argument that would otherwise need a paragraph. The first makes the task framework concrete. The second establishes non-monotonicity by pairing two occupations at opposite ends of the wage scale with the same low usage. The third — the omelette — makes the complexity limitation memorable in nine words and is the paper's best single sentence of self-criticism. The fourth reframes the Job Zone result as a statement about two different difficulty orderings, human and model, which is a genuinely new idea delivered in fourteen words with one hedge. Collect these; they are the sentences that survive summarisation.

## Figure captions

Twenty-seven numbered figures and two numbered tables. The caption pattern is constant: the label "Figure N:" or "Table N:", then a **bold lead phrase or sentence** that names what the exhibit is, then unbolded sentences giving the method, the units, the benchmark's citation or the reading. Captions sit below figures and above tables. There are no separate source lines, no panel letters, and no sample sizes in any caption — those are in Appendix E. Appendix captions (Figures 11–13, 21, and the uncaptioned Appendix E table) drop the explanatory sentences and sometimes the bold.

### Body figures and tables

> **Figure 1: Measuring AI use across the economy.** We introduce a framework to measure the amount of AI usage for tasks across the economy . We map conversations from Claude.ai to occupational categories in the U.S. Department of Labor's O\*NET Database to surface current usage patterns. Our approach provides an automated, granular, and empirically grounded methodology for tracking AI's evolving role in the economy. (Note: figure contains illustrative conversation examples only.)

**Annotation.** A schematic, so the caption narrates the pipeline instead of plotting anything: what we introduce, what we map to what, what the approach provides. Three sentences that restate the abstract's method claim almost word for word. The load-bearing part is the parenthetical at the end — "(Note: figure contains illustrative conversation examples only.)" — which forbids a reader from treating any conversation shown in the figure as real data. That disclaimer is the caption's whole ethical content and it is placed last, in parentheses, in a separate sentence; the launch post puts its equivalent privacy note in mid-clause. Copy the placement when a figure contains illustrative material. (The stray space before the first period, "across the economy .", is the paper's.)

> **Figure 2: Hierarchical breakdown of top six occupational categories by the amount of AI usage in their associated tasks.** Each occupational category contains the individual O\*NET occupations and tasks with the highest levels of appearance in Claude.ai interactions.

**Annotation.** Bold sentence names the cut ("top six occupational categories") and the ranking variable ("by the amount of AI usage in their associated tasks"). The second sentence explains the nesting, which is what a reader needs to know to read a three-level exhibit, and names the unit — "highest levels of appearance in Claude.ai interactions". No percentages in the caption although the figure is full of them.

> **Figure 3: Comparison of occupational representation in Claude.ai usage data and the U.S. economy.** Results show most usage in tasks associated with software development, technical writing, and analytical, with notably lower usage in tasks associated with occupations requiring physical manipulation or extensive specialized training. U.S. representation is computed by the fraction of workers in each high-level category according to the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics [U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, 2024].

**Annotation.** Three parts: the comparison named in the bold ("X and Y"); the reading given in prose, so the caption tells the reader what to see; the benchmark defined and cited in the last sentence, which is the part the launch post leaves to a parenthesis. This is the closest the paper comes to the house caption template — *what is plotted, in what units, for what sample, with the benchmark's provenance* — and the one thing it lacks is the series key, because the figure's own legend carries the colours. ("and analytical" is the paper's; the noun is missing.)

> **Figure 4: Depth of AI usage across occupations.** Cumulative distribution showing what fraction of occupations (y-axis) have at least a given fraction of their tasks with AI usage (x-axis). Task usage is defined as occurrence across five or more unique user accounts and fifteen or more conversations. Key points on the curve highlight that while many occupations see some AI usage (∼ 36% have at least 25% of tasks), few occupations exhibit widespread usage of AI across their tasks (only ∼ 4% have 75% or more tasks), suggesting AI integration remains selective rather than comprehensive within most occupations.

**Annotation.** The best caption in the paper and the one to imitate. Four moves: the topic in a bold noun phrase; the geometry with both axes named by role ("what fraction of occupations (y-axis) have at least a given fraction of their tasks with AI usage (x-axis)"); **the operational definition of the plotted quantity, in words, in the caption** ("Task usage is defined as occurrence across five or more unique user accounts and fifteen or more conversations"); and the two annotated points restated with their hedges intact, followed by the reading marked as an inference ("suggesting AI integration remains selective rather than comprehensive"). The figure is fully self-contained and the caption carries the privacy threshold that the body relegates to a footnote. When a figure's definition is arbitrary, put the definition in the caption.

> **Figure 5: Distribution of occupational skills exhibited by Claude in conversations.** Skills like critical thinking, writing, and programming have high presence in AI conversations, while manual skills like equipment maintenance and installation are uncommon.

**Annotation.** Two sentences. The bold names the measured object with the naming discipline our house rule wants — "exhibited by Claude", not by AI — and the reading sentence is a single high/low contrast with three examples each side, lower-cased where the body upper-cases the O\*NET skill names. The caption does not repeat §3.2's caveat about Active Listening, so the figure lifted on its own loses the paper's most important qualification. Worth noting: the caption's second sentence switches back to "AI conversations", so the naming is inconsistent within two sentences.

> **Figure 6: Occupational usage of Claude.ai by annual wage.** The analysis reveals notable outliers among mid-to-high wage professions, particularly Computer Programmers and Software Developers. Both the lowest and highest wage percentiles show substantially lower usage rates. Overall, usage peaks in occupations within the upper wage quartile, as measured against U.S. median wages [US Census Bureau, 2022].

**Annotation.** Pattern: *y by x* in the bold, then three sentences giving the outliers, the tails and the peak — the entire non-monotonic shape, in order, without a coefficient. The wage benchmark is cited at the end ("as measured against U.S. median wages [US Census Bureau, 2022]"), which is a different source from the O\*NET median wages the body uses; the caption is where the reader finds that out. No axis units are given, and none of the named occupations in the caption match the two the body names for the tails (waiters, anesthesiologists) — the caption and the body pick different exemplars for the same figure.

> **Table 1: Taxonomy of Human-AI Collaboration Patterns.** We classify conversations into five distinct patterns across two broad categories based on how people integrate AI into their workflow.

**Annotation.** A caption for a definition table, and it does only two things: name the taxonomy and state its shape (five patterns, two categories) and its basis ("based on how people integrate AI into their workflow"). All the content — the two category glosses, the five pattern names with their one-line definitions, and an **Illustrative Example** quoted for each — is inside the table, in the two-column layout reproduced on p.10. The table is where "Directive", "Feedback Loop", "Task Iteration", "Learning" and "Validation" get their definitions; the launch post moves those same five definitions into a figure caption. Both make the exhibit self-contained; the paper does it with a table, the post with a caption, and the post's version is the one that travels.

## Limitations

### §4.1 Limitations, verbatim and in full (p.12)

> Data sample We use snapshots of Claude.ai Free and Pro conversations over 7-day periods. It is possible that this sample is not representative of usage on Claude.ai across longer time windows, and quite likely that our sample differs in important ways from API data or data from other AI model providers due to differing model capabilities, product features, and user bases. Additionally, Claude.ai only outputs text as opposed to other modalities. This removes key potential users who may rely on image or video outputs (e.g. fashion designers). For these reasons our findings should be interpreted as an imperfect snapshot of AI usage across the labor market, while noting that a broader understanding of model interaction patterns will emerge as more researchers and organizations are able to share usage data from diverse deployment contexts.

> Reliability of model-driven classification Our use of Claude to classify user conversations may also introduce potential inconsistencies if the model's understanding of tasks differs from the intended reading in the O\*NET database. While we conduct human validation of our pipeline (Appendix C), rely upon past validations of Clio [Tamkin et al., 2024], and corroborate our results with cluster-level analyses (Appendix G), it is important to note that these classifications likely contain some inherent noise.

> Varying complexity of users' queries While we make efforts to exclude conversations without relevance to any occupational tasks (Appendix B), our method does not account for the complexity of user queries—for example, providing instructions for a basic omelette does not indicate culinary expertise. Consequently, we may overestimate usage rates for certain tasks by classifying conversations from novice users.

> Limitations of the O\*NET database While the O\*NET database offers valuable insights into current economic sectors, its static nature presents key limitations for analyzing AI's impact on the labor market. The database cannot capture emerging tasks and occupations that AI systems such as Claude may create or transform. Additionally, while O\*NET captures a very large number of tasks, it cannot contain all tasks in the economy. Furthermore, these tasks are often written in general terms, leading to inherent ambiguity when classifying conversations—many tasks are similar across multiple different occupations. Finally, as a U.S.-centric classification system, O\*NET may overlook significant occupational categories and tasks from other regions, potentially skewing our distributional analysis of global Claude.ai usage. This limits our analysis as AI usage patterns can vary across international contexts Gmyrek et al. [2023].

> Lack of full context into user workflows Although our work analyzes conversation data on Claude.ai, our methods are not able to capture how users are using the outputs of Claude.ai conversations. For example, we cannot observe whether users are copying code snippets into development environments, incorporating writing suggestions into documents, fact-checking responses against other sources, or using outputs as inspiration rather than verbatim content. Thus, it remains out of reach to make definitive judgments as to how much Claude's outputs are actually incorporated by users in their tasks. We aim to provide the preliminary framework and findings for this further study to take place.

The footnote attached to the first run-in, and the appendix table it points to (p.24), verbatim:

> See Appendix E for exact date ranges.

> Below we provide the metadata for each analysis provided in this paper. We include the number of Claude.ai conversations used and the time span from which these conversations were sampled.

The validation numbers §4.1 defers to, from Appendix C (p.21), verbatim:

> - At the top level, 95.3% of conversations are judged as assigned to an acceptable task.
> - At the middle level, 91.3% of conversations are judged as assigned to an acceptable task.
> - At the base (O\*NET) level, 86% of conversations are judged as assigned to an acceptable task.

> As tasks become more specific, classification becomes more difficult. Thus, accuracy of our method predictably decreases as we traverse the hierarchy. Furthermore, there is likely some irreducible noise in the assignment process, given that the O\*NET database, while extensive, does not encompass all possible tasks in the economy. As model capabilities increase, we expect classification performance to improve; we treat this as the baseline performance for future efforts to track AI usage using our framework. Although our methods are imperfect, our high validation scores provide confidence in our overall methodology and results.

> We hand-validate 150 examples for our automation vs. augmentation classifications (Section 3.4). We assess whether the label assigned by our framework (Directive, Feedback Loop, Validation, Task Iteration, or Learning) represents the best categorization for each conversation. We find that 90.7% of conversations are assigned to their optimal label as assessed by human raters.

And the dataset-composition check the "Varying complexity" run-in relies on, from Appendix B.7 (p.21), verbatim:

> After applying our occupational task screening, we found that non-work conversations only comprise 23% of the dataset, and usage relating to coursework comprises only 5-10% of conversations, depending on the chosen level of confidence. Importantly, we found that the majority of these "non-work" interactions still mapped meaningfully to occupational tasks. For example, personal nutrition planning relates to dietitian tasks, automated trading strategy development connects to financial analyst tasks, and travel itinerary planning maps to travel agent tasks. These results give us increased confidence in the utility of our dataset for understanding patterns of AI usage across economic tasks, regardless of whether those tasks happen within a formal work context.

**Annotation.**

- **Placement.** Its own numbered subsection, §4.1, first thing in the Discussion, before the implications — so the reader meets the limitations *between* the findings and the claims made on them, and §4.2 opens by acknowledging them ("While acknowledging these limitations"). The forward reference from the Introduction means a reader who reads only the first page has been told the section exists and given two of its contents. The launch post puts its caveats inside Results, under a conversational heading, after the findings and before the conclusion. The paper's placement is later in the document but earlier in the argument; nothing that follows §4.1 is read without it.
- **Form.** Five bold run-in headings, each naming a *source* of error rather than the error itself: the sample, the classifier, the queries, the taxonomy, the workflow. Not bullets, not ranked, not numbered — paragraphs, ordered roughly from the data inward to the interpretation. Each is a self-contained paragraph of two to six sentences with the same internal shape: what we did, what it cannot do, a concrete instance, the consequence for the reading.
- **How specific.** Highly, and the specificity is always a worked instance rather than a category. "snapshots … over 7-day periods" with the exact dates delegated to Appendix E. Fashion designers, as the users a text-only product excludes. The omelette, for query complexity. Four named workflow behaviours — "copying code snippets into development environments, incorporating writing suggestions into documents, fact-checking responses against other sources, or using outputs as inspiration rather than verbatim content" — where the launch post names two. Four distinct O\*NET defects in one paragraph, each with its own connective ("Additionally", "Furthermore", "Finally"), so a referee can attack them separately.
- **Direction of bias, where it is given.** Twice, and only twice. "we may overestimate usage rates for certain tasks by classifying conversations from novice users" — signed, with the mechanism. And "potentially skewing our distributional analysis of global Claude.ai usage" — direction unspecified. §3.4's augmentation caveat gives a third ("the true proportion of augmentative conversations may be even higher"). A signed limitation is worth three unsigned ones; three out of roughly nine is the paper's rate.
- **Graded epistemic language within a single sentence.** The strongest sentence-level device in the passage: "It is possible that this sample is not representative of usage on Claude.ai across longer time windows, and quite likely that our sample differs in important ways from API data or data from other AI model providers…". Two clauses, two different confidence levels — *possible* for the within-platform problem, *quite likely* for the across-platform problem — with the reasons for the second given immediately ("due to differing model capabilities, product features, and user bases"). Grading two limitations against each other inside one sentence tells the reader which one to worry about, and it costs three words. Copy this.
- **Where a claim is withdrawn.** "our findings should be interpreted as an imperfect snapshot of AI usage across the labor market" (sample); "it remains out of reach to make definitive judgments as to how much Claude's outputs are actually incorporated by users in their tasks" (workflow). Both are instructions about how to read the paper, not descriptions of noise, which is what makes them the two load-bearing limitations. The launch post's equivalent — "we don't argue that the uses in our dataset are a representative sample of AI use in general" — is blunter and, for a post, better.
- **How each limitation is paid for.** Every run-in that can be answered points at where the answer lives: Appendix C and Appendix G for the classifier, Appendix B for the screening, Appendix E for the dates. The numbers are real when the reader gets there — 95.3% / 91.3% / 86% down the hierarchy, 90.7% for collaboration patterns, 150 hand-validated examples each, 23% non-work, 5–10% coursework. The Appendix C passage also does something §4.1 does not: it explains *why* accuracy declines ("As tasks become more specific, classification becomes more difficult"), concedes irreducible noise, and then reframes the whole number as a floor for later work ("we treat this as the baseline performance for future efforts to track AI usage using our framework"). Turning a validation score into a benchmark for successors is a generous move and a citable one.
- **What a referee would raise first.** No sampling uncertainty anywhere: not one interval, standard error or minimum detectable effect in 38 pages, for percentages computed on samples of 500K–1M conversations. The 15-conversation/5-account threshold is stated but its effect on the depth curve is not bounded, so the ∼4%/∼36% pair has no sensitivity analysis. The Appendix C validation is run on conversations "for which users submitted feedback", and the paper says the distribution "likely differs from regular Claude.ai usage" and then uses it anyway on the grounds that it is "more reliable than alternative open-source datasets" — an unquantified comparison. Appendix G's own Figure 25 reports that under 20% of O\*NET tasks are recovered, a coverage fact that never reaches §4.1. And the five run-ins are unranked, so the reader is not told that the workflow limitation is the one that constrains every automation claim.
- **The closing sentence of the section.** "We aim to provide the preliminary framework and findings for this further study to take place." The limitations section ends by redefining the paper as preliminary. Both this paper and its launch post end their limitations by lowering what the work claims to be, and in both cases it is the last sentence that does it.

## Close

### §5 Conclusion, verbatim and in full (p.14)

> Understanding how AI affects the economy requires grounding our analysis in real-world data. Our analysis of millions of conversations on Claude.ai reveals clear patterns: AI usage peaks in software development and technical writing, with ∼ 4% of occupations showing usage across three-quarters of their tasks and ∼ 36% showing usage in at least a quarter of their tasks. Usage splits nearly evenly between automation (43%) and augmentation (57%) of human capabilities. While these patterns are informative, they capture just the beginning of AI's integration into work. As AI systems expand beyond text to handle video, speech, and physical actions through robotics, and as AI agents become more capable of carrying out extended tasks autonomously, the nature of human-AI collaboration is poised to transform dramatically. New tasks and even entirely new occupations may emerge around these capabilities. Empirical frameworks that track these changes dynamically will be crucial for anticipating and preparing for the evolving landscape of work. The challenge ahead lies not just in measuring these changes, but in using our understanding of them to help shape a better future.

### The end of §4.2, which carries the recommendations, verbatim (p.13)

> Task-level measurement Our findings highlight the importance of analyzing AI use at the task level rather than at the job level. Currently, we observe usage concentrated in specific tasks (e.g., software engineering, content creation) rather than wholesale automation of occupations. If this pattern persists—with AI affecting only a subset of tasks within jobs—it suggests occupations will evolve rather than disappear. However, if the breadth of task usage grows without signs of saturation, that may suggest the possibility of a more comprehensive workplace transition.

> Augmentation vs automation Within affected tasks, the way AI systems are used can differ significantly. Our analysis reveals an important distinction: while some users employ AI systems to completely automate tasks, others use them as collaborative tools that enhance their capabilities. This difference matters for both workers and productivity. When AI serves as an augmentative partner rather than a replacement, studies have shown improved productivity while maintaining individuals' meaningful engagement in their work [Noy and Zhang, 2023, Peng et al., 2023, Cui et al., 2024]. These patterns can inform policy priorities—supporting the development of collaborative AI interfaces where they show clear benefits, while ensuring appropriate preparation for areas where automation becomes more prevalent.

> From usage patterns to broader impacts Understanding how current AI usage patterns may translate into broader economic changes remains a key challenge. While our data reveals where AI is being used today, inferring long-term consequences from these early usage trends poses significant empirical challenges [Acemoglu et al., 2022]. For instance, high usage in certain occupations could signal future productivity gains or displacement effects, while the uneven distribution of AI use across wage levels may offer early indicators of how AI could reshape economic opportunities and inequalities. While our present results cannot definitively map these relationships, longitudinal analysis tracking both usage patterns and outcomes could help reveal the mechanisms by which AI usage drives changes in the workplace.

> Overall, our findings demonstrate that AI has already begun to see use across a significant fraction of economic tasks. We offer this initial framework for tracking AI's evolving impact on work as a starting point, and hope to partner with policymakers, economists, and other stakeholders to develop policy proposals that will spread the benefits of AI across the economy.

**Annotation.**

- **What was learned.** Restated with its numbers, in one sentence and a half, in the order of the paper: concentration, then depth at two thresholds, then the split. "AI usage peaks in software development and technical writing, with ∼ 4% … and ∼ 36% …. Usage splits nearly evenly between automation (43%) and augmentation (57%) of human capabilities." Every hedge survives the restatement — both tildes, and "nearly evenly", which is the conclusion's version of §3.4's "slightly more". This is the sharpest divergence from the launch post, which puts no number in its close at all and treats the findings as a baseline rather than a result. The paper's close is a summary; the post's is a horizon. **For our own posts the post's version is the model**, since a close that repeats the numbers is a summary block by another name, and the standard forbids one.
- **Why it matters.** Not asserted in the conclusion; the conclusion says only that the patterns "capture just the beginning of AI's integration into work". The stake is built earlier, in §4.2's **Task-level measurement** paragraph, and it is built as a two-branch conditional: "If this pattern persists—with AI affecting only a subset of tasks within jobs—it suggests occupations will evolve rather than disappear. However, if the breadth of task usage grows without signs of saturation, that may suggest the possibility of a more comprehensive workplace transition." The launch post has the first branch and not the second. Two branches is better: it states what would have to change for the reading to flip, which is the same discipline as naming what would falsify a hypothesis, and it keeps the modal verb in both branches ("suggests", "may suggest the possibility of"). This is the paragraph to re-read before writing a why-it-matters.
- **What comes next.** Three commitments, all methodological and none directional. In the conclusion, a claim about what the world will do ("As AI systems expand beyond text to handle video, speech, and physical actions through robotics, and as AI agents become more capable of carrying out extended tasks autonomously") followed by what that implies for measurement ("Empirical frameworks that track these changes dynamically will be crucial"). In §4.2, longitudinal analysis "tracking both usage patterns and outcomes". In **Dynamic tracking of AI usage**, three named functions of the instrument. Note the asymmetry the launch post also keeps: the *method* is promised, the *result* is not. Note also what is promised here that the post does not promise — the capability shift is asserted as coming, which is the one forecast the paper allows itself, and it is placed in the conclusion where no number depends on it.
- **Recommendations.** Three, all conditional and all addressed to policy rather than to Anthropic. "policymakers can develop targeted interventions—whether supporting sectors showing promising (or lagging) productivity gains or addressing potential displacement effects in areas of rapid automation." "These patterns can inform policy priorities—supporting the development of collaborative AI interfaces where they show clear benefits, while ensuring appropriate preparation for areas where automation becomes more prevalent." And the offer to collaborate: "hope to partner with policymakers, economists, and other stakeholders to develop policy proposals". Every one uses *can* rather than *should*; every one names both directions of the intervention rather than one; and the augmentation recommendation is the only one grounded in cited evidence outside this paper ("studies have shown improved productivity while maintaining individuals' meaningful engagement in their work [Noy and Zhang, 2023, Peng et al., 2023, Cui et al., 2024]"). The launch post's equivalent is a flat refusal to prescribe ("it doesn't provide policy prescriptions"); the paper prescribes, but only in the subjunctive and only where a citation supports it. Both are defensible; the paper's is the form to use when there is a recommendation to make.
- **How it avoids a template.** Four devices. (1) The first sentence is a methodological thesis, not a summary: "Understanding how AI affects the economy requires grounding our analysis in real-world data" — the same claim the abstract opens with, so the paper closes the loop it opened rather than listing what it did. (2) The numbers are compressed into one sentence and one clause, so the restatement occupies two lines of a twelve-line paragraph and the other ten are about what comes after. (3) The middle of the paragraph is about capabilities the paper did not study (video, speech, robotics, agents), which moves the reader forward rather than back. (4) The last sentence leaves the discipline entirely: "The challenge ahead lies not just in measuring these changes, but in using our understanding of them to help shape a better future." A paper that has spent 38 pages insisting on measurement ends by saying measurement is not the point. That inversion is what stops the close reading as a template — and it is also the paper's only unhedged sentence.
- **Title against ending.** Title: "Which Economic Tasks are Performed with AI? Evidence from Millions of Claude Conversations" — a question plus its evidence type. Ending: the tasks are named ("software development and technical writing"), the answer is quantified, and then the answer is declared provisional because the tasks will change. The question in the title is answered and then destabilised, which is a legitimate match for a first-wave paper. Contrast the launch post, whose title names an instrument and whose close promises to keep running it. The lesson holds in both directions: if the title asks a question, the close must answer it and say what would change the answer; if the title names an instrument, the close must be about the instrument.
- **First person.** Throughout, including in the summary of findings ("Our analysis of millions of conversations on Claude.ai reveals clear patterns"), the offer ("We offer this initial framework"), and the hope ("hope to partner with"). Our house style forbids it. The rhetorical work of "We offer… as a starting point" is a public downgrade of the paper's own claim, and it has to be rebuilt without an agent — the substitute is to make the framework the subject ("this framework is offered as a starting point") or to state the limit of the claim directly, which is stronger.
