# claude-code-expertise-2026-06-appendix — style annotation

## Source

- **Title on the cover:** "Appendix to “Agentic Coding and Persistent Returns to Expertise”" — set over three lines in 36 pt serif display under the ANTHROP\C wordmark, with a rule beneath and the date under the rule. The parent's title sits inside typographic quotation marks; the appendix has no title of its own. This is the Economic Index appendix convention (`economic-index-2026-03-appendix`, `economic-index-2026-06-appendix`) and not the convention of `coding-agents-social-sciences-2026-05-appendix`, which prefixes "Appendix:" to the parent's subject. The difference matters to a detached reader: this cover names the document it belongs to, that one names only a topic.
- **Date on the cover:** "June 2026". No day. The PDF's own creation date is 2026-06-22.
- **URL fetched:** https://cdn.sanity.io/files/4zrzovbb/website/a94728142a45694292336165947f8d6e3e1a357e.pdf — 27 pages. It is the only published location recorded for this file in `wiki/INDEX.md`, which lists the same URL in both the URL and methodology-appendix columns; no second build was looked for.
- **Parent piece:** "Agentic coding and persistent returns to expertise", slug `claude-code-expertise-2026-06` (https://www.anthropic.com/research/claude-code-expertise). Not fetched for this file; its style entry is a separate slug. The appendix prints no URL for it and names it only through the cover title and the running footer. Prose links to it exactly twice, by bare cross-reference: "the central claim of Section 4" and "It is the construction behind Figure 6." `wiki/INDEX.md` records the parent at 18 pages; **the appendix is half again as long as the piece it appends to**, which is the first structural fact about it.
- **Document type:** classifier-and-regression methods appendix for a log-analysis paper. It carries five things the report could not: the sample definition and the classifier run configuration; seven classifier prompts verbatim and the first of the task-value estimator's two prompts; the calibration and holdout validation of that estimator; the definitions of every derived measure; and three validation exercises plus the regression tables behind the headline claim.
- **Byline, abstract, contents, references, footnotes:** none, none, none, none, none. No named authors — the text refers to "one of the authors of this report" without naming them. No acknowledgements, no "how to cite", no version number, no data or code availability statement.
- **Production:** Adobe InDesign 21.4 (Macintosh), Adobe PDF Library 18.0. A typeset object, not a printed document, and it has the furniture to match: every page after the cover carries the full parent title in grey 8 pt sans at the foot left and the page number at the foot right; the cover carries "anthropic.com" in that slot. House fonts throughout — AnthropicSerifDisplay-Light 36 pt (cover), AnthropicSansDisplay-Semibold 24 pt (chapter heads), AnthropicSansText-Semibold 15 pt (sub-heads), AnthropicSerifText-Regular 11 pt (body), AnthropicMono-Regular 9 pt (prompt boxes), 8 pt sans (captions and footer).
- **Length, and where it goes.** 7,678 extractable words. Of these **5,079 (66%) are monospace prompt text** and only 1,986 (26%) are body prose; captions account for about 280 words and headings for 86. Pages 3–18 are almost entirely grey prompt boxes. The proportion is the document's editorial thesis: the instrument is the contribution, and prose exists to hand it over.
- **Audience:** a replicator first, a referee second. There is no why-it-matters sentence anywhere, no stakes, no restatement of the report's argument, and no interpretation of any result except where a result bears on whether a measure can be used.
- **The word "appendix" never appears in the body.** It occurs only in the cover title and the running footer. The document calls its parent "this report" throughout — "every number in this report", "the report's classifiers", "the report uses the estimates comparatively" — and so speaks from inside the report rather than about it. There is no "this appendix accompanies" sentence and no sentence describing the document's own contents.

## Section order

Only two heading levels exist: four 24 pt chapter heads and seventeen 15 pt sub-heads. There is no third level, and that absence does visible damage (below).

1. **Cover (p. 1)** — wordmark, title, rule, "June 2026". Nothing else: no summary, no contents, no authors.
2. **"Sample" (p. 2)** — five paragraphs, ~330 words, the whole of the data construction: coverage and surfaces; the counts; the privacy regime; the classifier call configuration; the telemetry inventory. No exhibit.
3. **"Classifier Prompts" (pp. 3–20)** — eight sub-sections across eighteen pages, in this order: *Work Mode* · *User expertise* · *Occupation (user profile)* · *Occupation (work performed)* · *Session outcome (judged success)* · *Success signal* · *Failure signal* · *Estimated task value (freelance-equivalent price)*. Each is one or two sentences of gloss followed by the prompt in a grey monospace box. The last sub-section breaks the pattern: one prompt box (the job-posting summariser; the pricing call is described in prose and never shown), a paragraph on the calibration corpus, and then three further sub-heads that are about the estimator rather than about a prompt — *Validation on a stratified holdout* (pp. 18–19), *Choice of calibration examples* (pp. 19–20), *Choosing the pricing model* (p. 20); the first and the last carry a rasterised table, the middle one carries none.
4. **"Derived measures and definitions" (pp. 20–21)** — five definitions, no exhibit, each a bold run-in term and one or two sentences.
5. **"Classifier validation and cross-check" (pp. 21–24)** — three sub-sections: *Classifiers versus telemetry* (Figures A1 and A2), *Internal outcome validation* (one paragraph, no exhibit), *Classifier validation via strong-model agreement* (three paragraphs, one rasterised table, and the document's only hyperlink).
6. **"Robustness of the expertise-success gradient" (pp. 24–27)** — *Verified success and expertise under cumulative controls* (Table A1) and *Verified success by expertise level (step function)* (Table A2). Page 26 is blank but for the running footer; Table A2 and its caption occupy the top third of p. 27 and nothing follows them.

**What the order tells you.** The document runs *inputs → instruments → derived quantities → validation of the instruments → robustness of the headline claim*. Every section is a precondition for the next, and the last two sections are the only ones that could be called evidence. Compare `economic-index-2026-06-appendix`, whose chapters are numbered to match the report's chapters so that the numbering *is* the cross-reference: this appendix has no numbering, and pays for it with the two bare pointers ("Section 4", "Figure 6") that are its only links into the parent's argument. The order within "Classifier Prompts" is the order of the constructs the report builds on, from the session outward: what the session was, who the user was, what the work was, how it ended, what evidence there is that it ended that way, what it was worth.

**A real structural flaw: the missing level.** "Robustness of the expertise-success gradient" is set at 15 pt — the same size as "Verified success and expertise under cumulative controls", which is its own subsection, and smaller than "Derived measures and definitions", which is a shorter and less consequential chapter. The section that carries the regressions behind the report's central claim is typographically a peer of the sub-head beneath it. Where a document has three logical levels it needs three typographic ones; conflating two of them buries the section a referee opens first.

**Where the prose sits relative to the exhibits.** Prose always before, exhibit after, caption after that — with two exceptions. The first rasterised table (p. 19) appears at the top of a page, between the paragraph that introduces the holdout on p. 18 and the two "Note that…" paragraphs that read it, so it is discussed both before and after and named by neither. The second (p. 20) is introduced by a colon at the end of a sentence — "on the stratified holdout:" — which is the cleanest hand-over in the document and the one to copy.

**What is shown, and what is not.** Shown in full: date range, surfaces included and excluded, the literal command form of the excluded case, the sample and user counts, the repeat-user distribution, the truncation limits in characters, the model and temperature, eight prompt boxes (the seven classifiers and the job-posting summariser), both SOC option lists in full (twice), the calibration filters, the holdout construction, three metric definitions, the bootstrap count, every derived measure, and the fixed-effect ladder. Not shown anywhere: **the pricing prompt** — the second call of the task-value estimator, described in prose and never printed, in the one measure whose own section says accuracy is "specific to the exact model and prompt the pipeline runs" — and the prompts for at least three classifiers the document itself uses — decision attribution (Figure A2, and the report's attribution result), `codebase_context` (referenced inside the Work Mode prompt), and whatever produces the "same type of task" and "same estimated value" conditioning that the Table A1 caption claims (its row labels, read from the render, name a task-occupation and a task-value-band fixed effect) — the date the classifiers ran, any seed, any cost, the aggregation threshold ("a minimum number of distinct users" is never given a number), and the report's own figures.

## Opening move

There is no opening move in the sense the brief asks for: no introduction, no preamble, no first paragraph about the document. The first prose is a section heading and a sample definition.

> ## Sample
>
> Our sample covers interactive Claude Code sessions between October 2025 and April 2026 (inclusive). This includes sessions conducted through the Claude Code command-line interface, the Claude Code desktop app, and claude.ai. This excludes Claude Code usage via third party integrated developer environments as well as via software development kits. Sessions with zero human turns are excluded—these excluded sessions are non-interactive calls in “headless” mode i.e., via claude -p “<prompt>”.

**Annotation.**

- **The first sentence is the scope statement and nothing else.** Population, instrument, date range, in fourteen words, with the qualifier that does the most work — "interactive" — sitting in front of the noun where it cannot be read past. Compare the June 2026 Economic Index appendix, which opens by stating a delta ("we made several changes…"); this one opens by stating a frame. Both are the genre's move: **an appendix opens with the fact that bounds the evidence, not with a claim.**
- **Includes, excludes, excludes — in that order, one sentence each.** Three surfaces in, two classes of traffic out, then the third exclusion with its definition attached. Giving each boundary its own sentence with the same verb at the front ("This includes… This excludes… are excluded") makes the frame auditable at a glance, and the reader can see that the sample is a product of three decisions rather than one.
- **The last exclusion is defined by the command that produces it.** "non-interactive calls in “headless” mode i.e., via claude -p “<prompt>”" — the class is named, then glossed, then pinned to the literal invocation a replicator would grep for. A boundary that can be expressed as a command should be.
- **Where the numbers sit.** Not here. The counts are held back to the second paragraph, so the first paragraph is purely definitional. That separation — *what the sample is* before *how big it is* — is worth copying, because it stops the size from doing the work of the definition.
- **Register.** First person plural, and only for methodological acts: "Our sample covers", "We exclude", "we use telemetry", "we gathered", "We tested", "We ran", "we took the variant". Never for a finding: the findings are written with the instrument as subject ("the task value estimator reaches", "the pricer anchors", "the share of sessions judged Succeeded rises"). House style forbids the first person outright, and this document shows the substitute it already uses half the time — **put the measure in subject position and the researcher disappears without a passive.**
- **Tense.** Present for what the sample and the pipeline are ("Our sample covers", "the model is shown", "we use telemetry"); past for what was done once to produce this release ("All classifications ran", "we gathered 250 examples", "We ran a grid", "we experimented"). The two are never mixed inside a sentence.

## Findings and their caveats

A methods appendix reports no finding about the economy. What it reports is construction, and three validation results that exist to say how far the construction can be trusted. The phrasing is annotated in document order.

### How the sample is counted

> The analysis covers 398,198 sessions uniformly randomly sampled from Claude Code traffic. These sessions come from 234,751 users. 73,066 users appear more than once in the sample, 6,382 users appear more than 5 times and 1,413 users appear more than 10 times. We exclude internal Anthropic usage.

**Annotation.** Four sentences, four different facts, and the paragraph's shape is *unit, then the unit above it, then the dependence between them, then one more exclusion*. The third sentence is the one to copy: it gives the repeat-user distribution at three thresholds instead of asserting that clustering is or is not a problem, which lets a reader judge the clustering for themselves — and the regression captions later confirm that standard errors are clustered by user, so the disclosure and the estimator agree. "uniformly randomly sampled" states the design in two words. Two defects: the exclusion of internal usage is appended to a paragraph about counts rather than to the paragraph about inclusion rules, where it belongs, and its size is never given; and no arithmetic anywhere reconciles 398,198 with the 375,687 that the Table A1 caption reports twenty-three pages later.

> All classifications ran through our privacy-preserving analysis infrastructure. Researchers never read individual sessions or transcripts, and every number in this report is an aggregate over a cell of sessions that is suppressed unless it contains a minimum number of distinct users.

**Annotation.** The privacy regime is stated as three commitments — where the classification ran, what the researchers did not see, and what suppression rule the outputs pass through — and each is a checkable claim rather than an assurance. The flaw is the last clause: "a minimum number of distinct users" is a threshold written without its value, and the same threshold is invoked again in the Table A1 caption ("cells that fell under the aggregation limit") to explain why three columns have fewer observations. **A suppression rule that changes a sample size must be published with its number.**

> For each classifier call, the model is shown the session transcript (human, assistant, and system turns) with each turn middle-truncated at 5,000 characters and the transcript as a whole truncated at 25,000 characters. Almost every classifier below is a single call to Claude Sonnet 4.6 at temperature 0.2 that must answer with exactly one of the listed options—the exception is the estimated task value classifier which uses Claude Haiku 4.5 for an initial summarizer prompt and Opus 4.7 for an estimation prompt.

**Annotation.** The best-specified paragraph in the corpus for a model-based measure: what the model sees, how it is cut and where the cut falls ("middle-truncated", so head and tail survive — the detail that makes the truncation note inside the outcome prompt intelligible), the two limits in characters, the model version, the temperature, the output contract, and the single exception named with both of its models. Every one of these is a parameter a replicator must set and most write-ups omit at least three. What is still missing is the run date and the prompt version, which matters here more than usual because the very next chapter shows that accuracy is model-dependent.

> Alongside the classifiers, we use telemetry recorded automatically for every session. The telemetry we use here includes the number of human prompts (“turns”), model calls (API requests), successful tool calls, output tokens, lines of code added and removed and the age of the user’s account (days since signup with Anthropic, not with Claude Code specifically).

**Annotation.** A second data source is introduced in one sentence and then enumerated exhaustively, with the house vocabulary pinned to the telemetry field in parentheses at first use ("human prompts (“turns”)", "model calls (API requests)"). The last parenthesis is the model of an inline caveat: "(days since signup with Anthropic, not with Claude Code specifically)" concedes, at the point of definition, that the tenure variable does not measure tenure with the product being studied. Ten words, no hedge, no apology, and it travels with the variable everywhere it is used. **When a proxy is off-target, say what it is not, inside the definition.**

### How each classifier is introduced

Each prompt is preceded by one or two sentences in the same shape: *this classifier* + a verb + what it returns + how many options + what it does when it cannot tell. The verbs are not interchangeable, and the choice of verb is the whole disclosure:

> This classifier categorizes each session into one of nine work modes that best describes what the session is trying to accomplish.

> This classifier measures the strength of the user’s expertise exhibited in a session, on a five-point scale.

> This classifier guesses the user’s likely occupation given the available signals in the session, such as hints from claude.md files and project memory, specific technical or industry jargon, and referenced artifacts. It chooses one of 23 major groups of the BLS Standard Occupational Classification, or, if there are not enough signals to make a solid guess, the classifier returns “Unclear.”

> This classifier judges whether the user, overall, accomplished what they set out to do. It first identifies the user’s primary objective in the session and then judges the outcome.

**Annotation.** "categorizes" for a partition, "measures" for a scale, "judges" for an outcome — and **"guesses" for the occupation classifier**, twice in one gloss ("guesses the user's likely occupation", "not enough signals to make a solid guess"). The weakest construct in the paper is labelled as a guess in the sentence that introduces it, in the author's own voice, before the prompt is shown. That is the single most transferable move in this document: **grade the verb to the evidence, and let the reader meet the weakness before the instrument.** Note also "expertise exhibited in a session" rather than "the user's expertise" — the construct is named after what is observable, the same discipline that `coding-agents-social-sciences-2026-05-appendix` applies when it calls a gender proxy "typically male name".

**Where the gloss contradicts the instrument.** The success-signal gloss reads "If there are no observable **failure** signals, it returns “None.”" and the failure-signal gloss reads "If there are no observable **success** signals, it returns “None.”" Both prompts, printed immediately below their glosses, say the opposite: NONE means no *success*-shaped signal for the success classifier and no *failure*-shaped signal for the failure classifier. The two residual conditions have been transposed. It is a copy-editing error rather than a methods error, and it is exactly the class of defect a build that verifies prose against the instrument would catch: **the gloss and the thing it glosses are two renderings of one fact, and they disagree.**

### How prompts are presented

Verbatim, in grey monospace boxes, full width, no line numbers, no commentary inside the box, nothing elided and nothing marked as elided inside a box that is shown — the prompts are printed as strings, down to the unrendered Markdown emphasis:

> **Pick by DOMINANT activity, measured by volume of meaningful work, NOT by the user’s stated intent in turn 1.** If a session opens with planning but then spends most turns writing code, that’s Build, not Plan. The triggering intent doesn’t decide the label — the bulk of the work does.

**Annotation.** The asterisks are left in. A reader sees the bytes the model saw, not a typeset version of them, which is the correct fidelity for an instrument and a deliberate ugliness. The only slot syntax in the document is `{TRANSCRIPT}`, inside `<transcript>` tags in the summariser prompt; unlike the June 2026 Economic Index appendix there are no `{descriptive}` slots and no bracketed elisions, because nothing has been cut.

Four conventions inside the prompts are worth copying into our own pre-registrations, because each is a coding rule that a human coder would otherwise have to invent:

- **Forced choice, stated as a prohibition.** "There is NO ‘Unclear’ option — pick the BEST fit from the nine, even when evidence is thin." Abstention is removed explicitly rather than by omission, so the reader knows the distribution has no missing category — and knows, therefore, that thin sessions are pushed into substantive labels. Where abstention *is* allowed, the prompt says what it is reserved for: "Reserve for sessions where the user’s contribution is so thin that NO direction-of-expertise signal exists".
- **Boundaries written as ordered pairs.** "Key distinctions:" followed by eight "X vs Y" lines, then a "NOT this:" clause inside most option definitions. The taxonomy is defined twice — once by what each label is, once by where it stops — and the second definition is the one that makes the first reproducible.
- **Named anti-signals.** "Naming files or paths that are visible on screen is NOT domain knowledge — anyone using Claude Code does that." "Generic asks (‘please double-check’, ‘are you sure?’, ‘verify your work’) are EPISTEMIC HUMILITY, not expertise — a careful novice does this." "Do NOT classify on the task being performed in this session. If the only signal is ‘they are writing Python’, that is not user-profile evidence, that is task evidence." Each names a plausible cue and forbids it, with the reason. **The confound is disclosed in the instrument rather than in the limitations.**
- **Traps enumerated with their resolutions.** "Two traps to avoid: (1) a single intermediate tool error that the agent recovers from is at most a 1 — the FINAL state is what counts. (2) Polite user disengagement at the end of a clean session is NOT a failure signal — silence after success-shaped work is just silence." And in the outcome prompt: "DO NOT confuse ‘the agent worked competently’ with ‘the user’s goal was achieved’. The agent can write clean code that does the wrong thing."

**The one place the same list is printed twice.** The 23 SOC major groups appear in full inside the user-profile prompt (pp. 9–10) and again in full inside the work-performed prompt (pp. 10–11), and the two printings differ in exactly two entries: the user-profile version adds "Only pick this when there is an affirmative signal that software/data is the user’s PROFESSION, not just what they are doing in this session" to *Computer and Mathematical*, and the work-performed version adds "— actual legal analysis and drafting, not just touching a legal topic" to *Legal Occupations*. Two pages are spent on a repetition so that the difference between the two questions is visible in the option text rather than asserted in prose. That is the right trade for an instrument, and it is the opposite of the June 2026 Economic Index appendix's twenty-one cluster names set as running prose: **print the instrument twice if the two runs differ; never compress a taxonomy the reader is expected to check.**

**Naming discipline.** Body prose says "Claude Code sessions", "Claude Code traffic", "Claude Sonnet 4.6", "the model", "the classifier", "the pricer" — and never says "AI". The string "AI" occurs five times in the document and every occurrence is inside a prompt: four are label definitions for the Orchestrate work mode ("meta-AI work", "the AI system itself"), and the fifth is the instruction that governs the task-value measure: "Do not mention Claude, AI, or that this came from a Claude Code session." The one place the appendix reaches for the generic term is the place where the model is being told to *suppress* provenance, so that a freelance price is formed on the work rather than on who did it. Our own rule (the question says AI, every finding says Claude) is the same rule read from the other end, and this is the cleanest demonstration of why it holds: **the product name is used where a product did the work, and stripped where the comparison requires that it not have.**

### How the estimator's calibration is written

The task-value measure is the only one built from two calls, and the only one whose instrument is half withheld: the summariser prompt is printed in full, and the prompt that does the pricing is described in prose and never shown.

> The second call (Opus 4.7) prices that posting against a calibration corpus of 200 real freelance postings with their actual prices—a tier-balanced sample drawn from a public job-postings datasets from 2024-2026. Postings are eligible for the calibration sample if they are fixed-price, priced between $10 and $50,000, and have a description of at least 50 characters. About 23,000 postings survive these filters.

**Annotation.** Three sentences: the design, the eligibility rule, the surviving count. Eligibility is given as three conditions in one sentence, each checkable, and the funnel's last rung is published ("About 23,000 postings survive these filters") — the rung that `coding-agents-social-sciences-2026-05-appendix` omits. What is not published is the name of the corpus: "a public job-postings datasets from 2024-2026" is indefinite, ungrammatical and unnamed, and the SWE-chat dataset five pages later shows the house can do better with a link. **A named external corpus is a citation; an unnamed one is an assertion.** Nor is the pricing prompt shown, in a sub-section that goes on to say accuracy is "specific to the exact model and prompt the pipeline runs" — a document that prints eight prompts to make itself reproducible withholds the ninth at the point where it matters most.

> Below, we discuss the development and validation of this classifier in more detail.

**Annotation.** The document's only forward pointer, one line, placed where a reader might otherwise stop. An appendix that departs from its own pattern should say so where it departs.

> In a holdout study with 999 job postings, the task value estimator reaches log R² = 0.38. To construct the holdout, we gathered 250 examples from four price tiers ($10–200, $200–1,000, $1,000–5,000, and $5,000–50,000), drawn from the same filtered corpus described above.
>
> We report three metrics: log R² (Pearson R² between log actual and log predicted price), level R² (the same in raw dollars), and multiplicative bias (the geometric mean of predicted/actual). Confidence intervals are 1,000-draw bootstraps.

**Annotation.** Headline accuracy in the first clause, construction in the second sentence, and then every metric defined before any is used — including the estimator behind the interval. Defining "multiplicative bias" as "the geometric mean of predicted/actual" in five words is what makes the caveat two paragraphs later ("over-priced about 2.4x") readable without a footnote. **Define the statistic in the sentence before the one that quotes it.** One arithmetic slip sits in plain text: 250 examples from each of four tiers is 1,000, and the holdout is stated as 999; the reconciliation exists only in the unnumbered table's own count column and never in prose.

> Note that the overall 0.38 largely reflects cross-tier ordering—because the holdout weights all four tiers equally, the pricer earns credit for placing $50 jobs below $8,000 jobs even where it cannot rank jobs within a tier. Within-tier discrimination is concentrated at the low end and is essentially zero above $5,000.
>
> Note also that bias reverses across the range. Small jobs are over-priced about 2.4x, the largest under-priced about 7x. So, we generally use these task value estimates ordinally rather than using them to get aggregate task values.

**Annotation.** These two paragraphs are the best writing in the document and the reason this file is in the corpus. Read what they do, in order: the headline number is quoted back and then **deflated by its own authors** — the accuracy is attributed to a design feature of the holdout rather than to the instrument's skill; the residual capability is stated where it exists and where it does not, on a stated boundary ("essentially zero above $5,000"); the bias is then shown to be non-monotone, with both ends quantified as multiples; and the paragraph ends by **restricting the licence of the measure**: ordinal use only, no aggregates. A caveat that ends in a rule about what the number may be used for is worth ten that end in "should be interpreted with caution". The corresponding lesson for our own posts is exact: **state the scale on which a measure is valid, and then do not leave the reader to infer the prohibition.** Note also "So, we generally use" — "generally" is the one loose word in the passage, and it leaves open whether any quoted figure in the report is an aggregate after all.

> We tested multiple options for the calibration set. With a uniformly weighted draw, the pricer anchors to the small-job range and compresses everything above it: on the validation holdout it under-priced $5,000+ jobs by roughly 12x (median prediction $800).

**Annotation.** A design variant that was tried and rejected, reported with the size of its failure. Publishing the losing configuration, with the mechanism named ("anchors to the small-job range and compresses everything above it") and the damage quantified, is what turns a design choice into evidence. Compare the same corpus's weakest robustness sentence, "A Poisson model specification gives similar results": one asserts, this one shows its work.

> Balancing the calibration set to 50 examples per tier raised the prediction level for larger jobs (median prediction for $5,000+ jobs rose to $2,000) and gave the best overall accuracy. On small jobs, ranking accuracy was statistically unchanged, at the cost of somewhat more over-pricing. Since the report uses the estimates comparatively rather than as dollar levels, we took the variant with the best overall ranking and the least price-dependent distortion.

**Annotation.** The selection rule is stated last and stated as a consequence of the use the measure is put to — not "we chose the most accurate", but *this is how the report uses the number, therefore this is the loss function*. That sentence is the model for any tuning decision in our own work. The concession is inside the same paragraph as the choice ("at the cost of somewhat more over-pricing"), and "statistically unchanged" is the one place a test is invoked without a statistic.

### How the derived measures are defined

> **Verified success.** A session reaches verified success when the outcome classifier judges it Succeeded AND the success-signal classifier scores 4 or 5—i.e., the transcript carries at least one hard verifiable signal (a matching commit, a passing test suite, a command completing with output matching the objective, or explicit user confirmation).

> **Abandoned.** A troubled session is abandoned when the outcome classifier judges it Failed AND telemetry records zero lines of code added.

> **Software/math vs. other professions.** A user is counted in software-related occupations when the user-profile occupation classifier returns Computer and Mathematical; “other identified professions” are all other SOC major groups. Sessions whose user profile is Unclear are excluded from occupation analyses (about 30% of sessions).

**Annotation.** One paragraph per measure; the term in bold as a run-in head; the sentence then begins with the definiendum and states the rule as a condition, not as a description. Three properties to copy. First, **the conjunctions are capitalised and the operands are named instruments** — "the outcome classifier judges it Succeeded AND the success-signal classifier scores 4 or 5" — so a reader can rebuild the variable from the printed prompts without guessing. Second, two of the measures **cross the two data sources on purpose**: "Abandoned" requires a classifier judgment *and* a telemetry fact, which is what makes it more than a model's opinion. Third, the definition that excludes cases **states the exclusion's size in the same sentence** — "(about 30% of sessions)" — which is the number `coding-agents-social-sciences-2026-05-appendix` omits when it drops androgynous names. The only weakness is the "i.e." gloss in *Verified success*: it re-describes score 4 or 5 as "at least one hard verifiable signal", which is true of level 4 and understated for level 5, where the prompt requires multiple.

### How the validation results are phrased

> **Outcome-classifier.** The success-signal and failure-signal classifiers track the independent outcome judgment in the expected directions: the share of sessions judged Succeeded rises from 13% at success-signal 1 to 90% at success-signal 5, and falls from 85% at failure-signal 1 to 3% at failure-signal 5 (Figure A1).

**Annotation.** A convergent and a discriminant check in one sentence, both given as ranges with their endpoints rather than as correlations, both signed by the direction word before the numbers ("rises", "falls"), and both tied to the exhibit by a terminal reference. "in the expected directions" states the prediction before the result, which is what makes this a test rather than a description. What it does not do is say what would have counted as failure: no threshold, no interval, no statement of how far from monotone the relation could have been before the classifier was rejected.

> We ran the same classifier prompts on internal Anthropic Claude Code sessions, where session IDs can be joined to observable engineering outcomes (whether the session’s commits landed on the main branch). The expertise–success gradient replicates on this sample, including within-engineer (comparing sessions of the same person at different rated expertise levels): judged success rises by roughly 3–5 percentage points per expertise level with author fixed effects, and sessions at higher rated expertise are more likely to produce code that lands.

**Annotation.** The strongest paragraph of evidence in the document, and it is eighty words. Three moves: the external criterion is named and its availability explained in a parenthesis ("whether the session’s commits landed on the main branch"); the claim is then tested at a *second level* — not just across users but within the same engineer, with the comparison spelled out in a parenthesis for a reader who does not read "author fixed effects"; and the magnitude is given as a range with "roughly", at a precision the design can bear. **One claim tested at two levels, with an outcome the classifier cannot see, is worth more than six claims at one level** — this is the paragraph our own posts should be measured against. What a referee would still ask: the internal sample has no n, no date range, no statement of how many engineers, and no standard error; "more likely to produce code that lands" is a direction with no magnitude at all; and internal Anthropic usage is excluded from the main sample precisely because it is unrepresentative, which is a validity cost the paragraph never names.

> It is challenging to validate classifiers on Claude Code sessions—a single session can span hundreds of turns, files, and tool calls, and hours (or even days) of work. The norm for classifier validation (pursued in previous reports) has been to benchmark the classifier’s performance against human performance for a subset of session transcripts that are accessible from a privacy perspective. But the length and complexity of Claude Code sessions call into question whether such an approach is feasible and reliable. It is not clear that humans, with their limited working memory and limited understanding of unfamiliar codebases and agentic tool traces, can label sessions more consistently than a powerful model.

**Annotation.** A methodological argument, not a result, and it is placed *before* the method it justifies rather than after. The shape is: the difficulty, with its cause stated concretely; the standing norm, credited and located ("pursued in previous reports"); why the norm does not transfer here; and the claim that follows, hedged in the only way that claim can honestly be hedged ("It is not clear that humans… can label sessions more consistently"). Departing from a standard is announced, motivated and bounded before anything is shown. **A method that breaks with the house norm earns a paragraph of its own, ahead of the results it produces.**

> The report’s classifier and the reference agree on 78-98% of sessions on the categorical classifiers. On the classifiers with ordinal scales, the report’s classifier and the reference agree on 78-99% counting adjacent scale points as agreement, and 53-68% counting only exact matches as agreement. The judge rated a vast majority of the disagreements as genuinely ambiguous—concerning disagreements are 0-3% of sessions per classifier (at most 5 of 198). In the final step, a human reviewer audited the 15 disagreements the judge had flagged as concerning and concurred with the judge in 11 of 15 sessions.

**Annotation.** Agreement is reported **as a range across classifiers rather than as an average**, so the worst instrument is visible in every sentence; and the ordinal agreement is given twice, under both the lenient and the strict rule, with the rule named each time ("counting adjacent scale points as agreement", "counting only exact matches"). A reader can therefore take the number they think is right instead of the number the authors preferred. The last sentence reports the human check as a fraction with both terms — "concurred with the judge in 11 of 15 sessions" — which lets the reader see that the human disagreed with the adjudicator four times, a fact no percentage would have preserved. **Small denominators are published as fractions, not shares.**

> Taken together, the results of our validation are encouraging. They do caution against over-reading any particular label, but most of the report’s conclusions rest on relative comparisons, i.e., differences between groups and changes over time, and on those, the classifier is more reliable. As long as the classifier’s mistakes are similar across groups (which we did not extensively verify here), those mistakes cancel each other out when making comparisons.

**Annotation.** The verdict paragraph, and the structure is *claim, restriction, mechanism, and the assumption the mechanism needs* — with the assumption conceded in a parenthesis in the same sentence that relies on it: "(which we did not extensively verify here)". Six words that hand a referee the exact hole and decline to fill it. The move is the same one `coding-agents-social-sciences-2026-05-appendix` makes with "the key gradients **that we can measure** are similar": a fence inside the sentence being defended. The difference is that this one fences an *identifying assumption* rather than a measurement, which is the more consequential kind. Note the register: "encouraging" is the strongest word in the document, and it is immediately qualified.

## Comparisons

This is the most comparison-dense appendix in the corpus, and the recurring move is that a comparison is introduced by the alternative it exists to rule out. Six sections make seven comparisons.

### The specification ladder

> Each column in the table below adds a control, testing a different hypothesis. First, that expert-rated users pick different kinds of work (column 2), work in different months (3), work on different subjects (4), hold different occupations

> (5), or run longer sessions on stronger models (6). The coefficient is the change in the probability of verified success per step on the five-point expertise scale.

*(One sentence, broken across the page 24 / page 25 boundary by Table A1 and its caption, and quoted here in the two parts the layout produces.)*

**Annotation.** The sentence to copy in this whole file. Each column of a regression table is named with **the alternative explanation it is there to kill**, in the reader's language rather than in the language of fixed effects: not "adds month fixed effects" but "work in different months". Five rival hypotheses are enumerated, numbered to the columns, in one sentence and forty-six words; then the estimand is defined in plain units in the next. A cumulative-controls table written this way *is* an argument, and a reader who never looks at the numbers still knows what has been ruled out. Compare the report's own habit of naming the preferred column in the caption rather than in the prose — both are needed, and both are present here.

### Linear against step function

> Table A1 assumes that expertise is a linear scale, i.e., that each step of the expertise scale is worth the same. Table A2 instead uses an indicator for each level of the expertise scale, under the Table A1 column-(5) fixed effects, with Novice as the omitted category. It is the construction behind Figure 6.

**Annotation.** The functional-form assumption is named, then relaxed, and the relaxation reuses the preferred specification so that exactly one thing changes between the two tables. Stating what the first table *assumes* before showing the table that drops the assumption is the honest order; and the closing sentence binds the appendix exhibit to the parent's figure, which is the only place in the document where a table is given a purpose outside itself.

### Unadjusted against adjusted, inside one table

The Table A2 caption sets the two side by side and defines both:

> The unadjusted rate is the raw share of sessions at each expertise level ending in verified success, with no controls. The adjusted rate holds the fixed effects constant.

**Annotation.** Both quantities live in the same exhibit with one column each, so the reader sees the composition effect directly instead of being told about it. Where `coding-agents-social-sciences-2026-05-appendix` puts the unadjusted contrast in the appendix and the adjusted one in the parent, this table puts them in adjacent columns — the better arrangement, because the comparison becomes a subtraction the reader can perform rather than a claim they must accept.

### The pricing-model grid

> Holdout accuracy for this task is model-dependent, so the cited validation numbers are specific to the exact model and prompt the pipeline runs. We ran a grid of two models (Sonnet 4.6 and Opus 4.7) and two calibration variants (uniform draw from freelance corpus and tier-balanced) on the stratified holdout:

**Annotation.** A 2×2 of design choices, with the reason for running it stated first as a general property of the task ("Holdout accuracy for this task is model-dependent"), and the consequence for the reader stated in the same sentence ("the cited validation numbers are specific to the exact model and prompt the pipeline runs"). The colon hands straight to the table. This is a robustness sweep in three lines of prose; the defect is that the table it hands to carries no number, no caption and no reference (see below), so nothing in this paragraph can be cited.

### Classifier against telemetry, and classifier against a reference model

The first compares a transcript judgment with a machine-recorded fact — "convergence between a transcript-based judgment and harness-recorded telemetry" — and names the variable that is *expected not to move*: "Execution share is near-orthogonal to these (Figure A2)." A validation that predicts a null for one dimension and a gradient for another, in the same sentence, is stronger than one that only predicts a gradient. The second replaces the human benchmark with a stronger model and adjudicates the disagreements blind. Neither is called a robustness check, and neither has a threshold stated in advance.

### Internal against external sample

The internal-commit validation is the document's only comparison of *the same claim on a different population with a different outcome measure*, and it is also the comparison whose population the main sample deliberately excludes. The appendix runs it and does not remark on the tension.

**What is absent.** No comparison of the new classifiers against the ones used in earlier reports; no human-labelled subsample at all; no sensitivity of any result to the truncation limits, to temperature, or to a second run of the same prompt (nothing in the document addresses run-to-run variability of a temperature-0.2 classifier); and no leave-one-out on the fixed-effect ladder beyond the cumulative order in which it is presented.

Two numbered figures, two numbered tables, **two unnumbered and entirely uncaptioned tables**. Every caption sits **below** its exhibit, left-aligned to the text block, in 8 pt sans — the same size and family as the running footer, and a different family from the serif body, so captions read as furniture rather than as prose. Each has a semibold label-and-title line and a regular-weight gloss.

### Figure A1

> **Figure A1. Success outcome versus success and failure signals**
> The left panel shows the probability that the success outcome classifier records “succeeded” for sessions of each success signal strength. The right panel shows the probability that the success outcome classifier records “succeeded” for sessions of each failure signal strength.

**Annotation.** The title names the comparison and both of its sides. The gloss is two sentences in identical grammar, one per panel, differing in one word — which is the cheapest way to make a two-panel figure unambiguous and the reason no reader can mistake which axis is which. The estimand is named exactly ("the probability that the success outcome classifier records “succeeded”"), with the *instrument* in the sentence rather than the concept: not the probability of success, but the probability that a named classifier records it. **When the outcome is a model's label, the caption says so.** What the caption omits: the n (it is printed inside the chart instead), the source of the error bars that the figure draws, and any statement of what the comparison is for — a reader who has not read the paragraph above does not learn from the caption that this is a validation exercise. **Annotation.** Three defects in one caption, all instructive. ### Table A1

> **Table A1. Verified success and expertise under controls**
> OLS with fixed effects. 375,687 sessions, columns (4)-(6) have fewer due to cells that fell under the aggregation limit. Standard errors in parentheses, clustered by user. Column (5) is the preferred specification. It compares sessions doing the same kind of work, at the same estimated value, in the same month, on the same type of task, from users in the same occupational group. **Annotation.** Eighty-one words, and the most complete caption in the corpus. Read it as seven clauses, each answering a different question, in an order worth copying exactly: **estimator** ("OLS with fixed effects") · **n, with the reason it varies** · **uncertainty and its clustering level** · **which column to read** · **what that column holds constant, in English rather than in variable names** ("sessions doing the same kind of work, at the same estimated value, in the same month, on the same type of task, from users in the same occupational group") · **what the last column adds, and that it is a different kind of control** ("choices made during the session") · **the exclusion**. The fifth clause is the one most captions lack and the one that makes a fixed-effects table legible to an economist who is not going to read the row labels. The sixth does quiet work too: separating pre-session characteristics from within-session choices is a statement that column (6) conditions on a mediator, which is why it is not the preferred column.

### The two uncaptioned tables

The validation-metrics table (p. 19) and the pricing-model grid (p. 20) have **no number, no caption, no notes and no reference in prose**. Then a regular-weight gloss carrying, in this order, the estimator, the sample and why it varies, the uncertainty with its clustering, which column or panel to read, what is held constant in plain English, and the exclusions. Captions below the exhibit, always. No caption in the document states a date, a source, or a licence, and none says what the exhibit is *for*.

## Limitations

**There is no limitations section, no caveats heading, and no paragraph that collects the document's qualifications in one place.** Every limitation sits in the sentence that creates it. Collected, verbatim, in order of appearance:

> Sessions with zero human turns are excluded—these excluded sessions are non-interactive calls in “headless” mode i.e., via claude -p “<prompt>”.

> the age of the user’s account (days since signup with Anthropic, not with Claude Code specifically)

> This classifier guesses the user’s likely occupation given the available signals in the session

> Within-tier discrimination is concentrated at the low end and is essentially zero above $5,000.

> Note also that bias reverses across the range. Small jobs are over-priced about 2.4x, the largest under-priced about 7x. So, we generally use these task value estimates ordinally rather than using them to get aggregate task values.

> Sessions whose user profile is Unclear are excluded from occupation analyses (about 30% of sessions).

> It is challenging to validate classifiers on Claude Code sessions—a single session can span hundreds of turns, files, and tool calls, and hours (or even days) of work.

> They do caution against over-reading any particular label

> As long as the classifier’s mistakes are similar across groups (which we did not extensively verify here), those mistakes cancel each other out when making comparisons.

> This validation approach is far from perfect, but we do think it is promising to use a strong reference model alongside blind adjudication of disagreements

**Annotation.**

- **Where they sit, and what that costs.** Inline, each beside the decision that causes it, with nothing deferred and nothing repeated. The strength is that no caveat can be skipped by skipping a section; the weakness is that no reader ever sees them together, and the document never totals what it does not know. The June 2026 Economic Index appendix has the same arrangement and the same weakness. The one departure here is that the task-value caveats are given two dedicated paragraphs in a row, both opening "Note that" / "Note also that" — a two-word signal that the paragraph is about to subtract from what was just claimed. That signal is cheap and worth stealing.
- **The caveat that ends in a licence.** "we generally use these task value estimates ordinally rather than using them to get aggregate task values" is the only sentence in the corpus that converts a measurement limitation into an operating rule for the paper that follows. It is the best limitation sentence in the corpus for that reason.
- **Each concession names a mechanism.** Not "the occupation measure is noisy" but a verb — "guesses"; not "the estimator is imprecise" but "bias reverses across the range" with both multiples; not "validation is hard" but sessions that "span hundreds of turns, files, and tool calls". There is no "as with all studies" sentence anywhere, no "results should be interpreted with caution", and no sentence that re-asserts the contribution after conceding something.
- **The hedge inventory is small and consistent.** "about", "roughly", "generally", "largely", "essentially zero", "far from perfect", "encouraging", "it is not clear that", "(which we did not extensively verify here)". Every one is attached to the claim it qualifies, in the same sentence; none is a free-standing disclaimer. "may" does not occur in body prose at all.
- **What a referee would raise first, and the appendix does not.** (1) **Four instruments are used and never printed** — the pricing prompt of the task-value estimator, the decision-attribution classifier (the whole of Figure A2 and the report's attribution result), `codebase_context`, and whatever produces the "same type of task" and "same estimated value" conditioning the Table A1 caption claims — in a document whose organising principle is that instruments are published. (2) **No human validation of any classifier**, by design and with an argument, but the argument is asserted rather than tested: no experiment compares human and reference-model consistency on the same sessions, so the premise that licences the whole approach is untested. (3) **The reference model is not identified.** "Mythos Preview" appears three times, is never expanded, never versioned and never dated, and is the sole standard against which every classifier is measured. (4) **Reference model and blind judge are the same model**, which the text states plainly ("again Mythos Preview") and never treats as a threat: a judge that adjudicates its own disagreements is not independent, and the 15-session human check is the only evidence bearing on it. (5) **No run-to-run variability** for classifiers run at temperature 0.2, and no seed. (6) **The aggregation threshold is never given a number**, although it changes the sample in three columns of Table A1. (7) **Three different session counts** — the Sample section's, the Table A1 caption's, and the table's own — with no reconciliation in prose. (8) **The internal validation sample** has no n, no engineer count, no date range and no standard error, and is drawn from the one population the main sample excludes. (9) **Agreement ranges are reported without saying which classifier sits at which end**, so the 53% exact-match floor cannot be attributed to a measure. (10) **The calibration corpus is unnamed**, and the pricing prompt's own instruction to strip provenance is never tested for whether it succeeds.
- **The lesson to take and the lesson to leave.** Take the two "Note that" paragraphs, the ordinal-use licence, the "(which we did not extensively verify here)" fence, the exclusion sizes stated inside definitions, and the within-engineer replication against an outcome the classifier cannot see. Leave the silences: in this house every instrument the post uses is printed, every reference model is named and versioned, every threshold that moves an n is published, and three renderings of one count do not survive the build.

## Close

**There is no close.** The last elements of the document, in order, are: the paragraph on p. 25 that introduces Table A2, then an empty page 26 carrying only the running footer, then Table A2 itself and its caption at the top of p. 27, then two thirds of a page of white. Nothing follows — no conclusion, no summary, no pointer back to the report, no references, no data or code statement, no contact. Verbatim, the last text in the appendix:

> Excludes sessions with no clear goal.

**Annotation.**

- **Ending on a table's notes is the correct ending for the genre**, and it is now the third appendix in this corpus to do it: one ends on a coding scheme, one on a note about which disciplines were dropped, this one on an exclusion rule. An appendix stops when it has handed over the last object it was holding. Nothing is concluded because nothing was argued.
- **But the genre's closing move happens here on page 24, in the middle of the document.** The strong-model-validation section ends with something no other appendix in the corpus contains — a statement of what comes next and an invitation:

> We are actively developing methods of validation that take seriously that human labels may no longer be the gold standard for complex sessions and also that some human review may still be valuable for interpretation. We encourage others in the research community to take up this methodological challenge alongside us.

  This is a *what-comes-next* and a call to the field, in an appendix, three pages before the end, attached to the section that departs from house method rather than to the document. The placement is right: the invitation belongs to the problem it arose from, not to the file. **A methodological open question is closed where it is opened, not in a concluding paragraph.** Our own posts spend their close on what was learned and what comes next; this shows the same material working at section scale.
- **Title against ending.** The title names the parent and nothing else; the ending is an exclusion rule in a table note. The "title matches the ending" test does not apply to this genre in the form it applies to a post. The equivalent test — *does the document end on the object its last section promised* — is passed: the section is called "Robustness of the expertise-success gradient", it promises "We show regressions underlying the claims", and it ends with the second of those regressions.
- **The blank page.** Page 26 carries nothing but the running footer, between the prose that introduces Table A2 and Table A2 itself. A reader paging through meets an empty page where a table was promised, and a reader who stops there has seen the promise without the evidence. It is a layout artefact, not a decision, and it is the one place where the document's production actively obstructs its argument — the same class of defect as the June 2026 Economic Index appendix's footnote deferred fourteen pages past its marker.
- **What this licenses in our own posts, and what it does not.** Licensed: a methodology section may end on a table note; a validation section may end on an open problem and an invitation; a caption may be the longest paragraph on its page when the exhibit carries the central claim; an instrument may be printed twice when two runs of it differ. Not licensed: a post may not end this way. A post that stops at its last exhibit has left the reader without the why-it-matters, and this document can omit it only because the parent spent it.
