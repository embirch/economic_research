# economic-index-2025-03-report — style annotation

## Source

- **Title on page:** "Anthropic Economic Index: Insights from Claude 3.7 Sonnet" (H1). Browser/OG title: the same string, plus "\ Anthropic". Meta description, and the page's own self-description: "The second update from the Anthropic Economic Index".
- **Date on page:** "Mar 27, 2025".
- **Primary URL fetched:** https://www.anthropic.com/research/anthropic-economic-index-insights-from-claude-sonnet-3-7 (the page's own `canonical`).
- **Other URLs it points at (not fetched for this file):** the predecessor report at https://www.anthropic.com/news/the-anthropic-economic-index (own slug `economic-index-2025-02-report`), the Index hub at https://www.anthropic.com/economic-index, the Claude 3.7 Sonnet launch post at https://www.anthropic.com/news/claude-3-7-sonnet, the Clio post at https://www.anthropic.com/research/clio, the dataset at https://huggingface.co/datasets/Anthropic/EconomicIndex/, a Google Forms "researcher input form", and https://www.anthropic.com/jobs.
- **PDF or appendix:** no PDF, and no separate appendix document. The `wiki/INDEX.md` row carries `—` in the PDF column. The appendix is two H2 sections of the same page ("Appendix", "Additional methodological details"), which is the structural novelty of this piece: unlike the first report it does not delegate depth to a paper, it carries its own appendix inline.
- **Document type:** wave report. Not a launch post and not a paper summary — an update pegged to a model release, whose job is to re-run a published analysis on a later window and to ship two new datasets. Byline area is categorical, not personal — "Societal Impacts" / "Economic Research"; no named authors anywhere on the page, and no Acknowledgements section.
- **Approximate length:** roughly 1,400 words of body prose, plus roughly 500 words carried in figure captions — captions are close to a quarter of the running text, a far higher share than in the first report, and several of them are the only place a number appears. Twelve images (one uncaptioned hero, five captioned exhibits in the body over six image files, three captioned exhibits in the appendix over five image files). Seven H2 sections of substance plus "Work with us", three H3s under Appendix, no footnotes.
- **Audience:** the same economists-and-researchers audience as the first report, but addressed as *users of the release* rather than as readers of the argument. Two sections exist mainly to hand work over ("To enable further research in this space, we're releasing a new dataset…"; "While we leave detailed analysis of this dataset to future work…"). A reader who wants the finding gets four sentences; a reader who wants the data gets a folder.

## Section order

Headings in order, with what each does and how long it runs.

1. **Topic tags + H1 + date + hero image.** No "Read the paper" link, because there is no paper. Where the first report put a pointer to the record above the prose, this one puts nothing: the post *is* the record.
2. **Untitled opening (no heading)** — two paragraphs, then "Briefly, our latest results are the following:" and three bullets, then a paragraph on the bottom-up taxonomy, then the dataset link, then the one-line pointer "Read on for more details on our findings." ~250 words. Same shape as the first report's opening (stakes → bullets → invitation → pointer), and the pointer sentence is a near-verbatim series convention: "See below for further details on our initial findings." became "Read on for more details on our findings."
3. **H2 "What's changed since the launch of Claude 3.7 Sonnet?"** — ~280 words, three paragraphs, then the wave-comparison figure and its caption. Paragraph one is the sample, paragraph two is the pipeline reminder, paragraph three is the result and its three candidate explanations.
4. **H2 "How are people using extended thinking mode?"** — ~180 words, three paragraphs, then the leaderboard figure and its caption. Definition, result, handover.
5. **H2 "How does augmentation vs. automation vary by task and occupation?"** — ~330 words, the longest section, four prose blocks interleaved with three exhibits: the V1/V2 mode figure, the by-category figure, and the two-panel "Top occupations by interaction type" pair.
6. **H2 "A bottom-up taxonomy of usage on Claude.ai"** — ~180 words, two paragraphs and six bullets. The motivation is a limitation of the previous method; the contribution is a dataset; the analysis is explicitly deferred.
7. **H2 "Conclusion"** — two paragraphs, ~120 words. The shortest close in the series so far.
8. **H2 "Work with us"** — recruiting. Boilerplate; not part of the argument.
9. **H2 "Appendix"** — one framing sentence ("We share a few additional results and technical details in this appendix.") and three H3s: **"Task Curve"** (~90 words plus figure), **"Full change across occupational categories"** (figure and caption only, no prose at all), **"Results for other interaction modes"** (three images and one caption, no prose at all).
10. **H2 "Additional methodological details"** — one framing sentence and two bullets. The last section of the document.

**Where the findings sit.** Three of the five body headings are questions with question marks — "What's changed…?", "How are people using…?", "How does augmentation vs. automation vary…?" — where the first report used declarative headings ("Mapping AI usage across the labor market", "Results"). The consequence is that this piece has no "Results" section: each finding is the answer to the heading immediately above it, and there is no place where the findings sit together. The opening bullets are the only synoptic view, and they carry no numbers at all.

**Where the qualifications sit.** There is no "Caveats" heading and no "Limitations" heading. The first report gathered six caveats into an H3 inside Results; this one distributes them to four places: inside captions (the 0.5% floors, the share-versus-level note), inside one body sentence ("Note that the O\*NET descriptions may not be optimally representative…"), inside the section that motivates the new dataset (the O\*NET coverage gap), and in the final H2, where the two changes to the pipeline are filed under "for transparency" rather than under limitations. A reader who stops at the Conclusion — which is where a reader stops — meets none of the last group. That relocation is the single most consequential structural difference from the first report, and it is the one thing in this piece a referee would fix first.

## Opening move

Verbatim, the opening in full (hyperlink URLs stripped; anchor text marked below):

> Last month, we launched the Anthropic Economic Index—a new initiative where we're regularly releasing data and research aimed at understanding AI's effects on labor markets and the economy over time.

> Today, we're releasing our second research report from the Index, covering usage data on Claude.ai following the launch of Claude 3.7 Sonnet—our newest and most capable model with strengths in agentic coding and a new "extended thinking" mode.

> Briefly, our latest results are the following:
>
> - Since the launch of Claude 3.7 Sonnet, we've observed a rise in the share of usage for coding, as well as educational, science, and healthcare applications;
> - People use Claude 3.7 Sonnet's new "extended thinking" mode predominantly for technical tasks, including those associated with occupations like computer science researchers, software developers, multimedia animators, and video game designers;
> - We're releasing data on augmentation / automation breakdowns on a task- and occupation-level. For example, tasks associated with copywriters and editors show the highest amount of *task iteration*, where the human and model co-write something together. By contrast, tasks associated with translators and interpreters show among the highest amounts of *directive* behavior—where the model completes the task with minimal human involvement.

> In addition, we're releasing a first-of-its-kind bottom-up taxonomy of usage on Claude.ai. This new dataset covers 630 granular categories ranging from "Help resolve household plumbing, water, and maintenance issues" to "Provide guidance on battery technologies and charging systems." We hope this bottom-up taxonomy will be useful for researchers, and reveal use-cases that might be missed by top-down approaches which map usage onto a list of predefined tasks.

> The datasets for these analyses are freely available to download.

> Read on for more details on our findings.

Anchor text carrying links, in order: "Anthropic Economic Index" → the Index hub; "Claude 3.7 Sonnet" → the model launch post; "freely available to download" → Hugging Face.

**Annotation.**

- **What question is posed.** None in the prose. The opening is occasion-driven, not question-driven: "Last month, we launched…" / "Today, we're releasing…". Two sentences of institutional chronology establish that this is the second observation of a repeated measurement, which is the only warrant the piece needs. The questions are then delegated to the section headings, where they are posed literally, with question marks. Compare the first report, whose opening asserted a forecast and derived an instrument from it; the sequence here is *predecessor → occasion → what is new*, and the occasion is a product launch. This is the move available to a wave report and to nothing else: the reason to look again is that the thing being measured changed.
- **Who is said to be affected.** Not the labour market, and not "people work" — occupations, by name, inside the findings: computer science researchers, software developers, multimedia animators, video game designers, copywriters, editors, translators, interpreters. Eight named occupations in the opening bullets against zero in the first report's. The aggregate ("labor markets and the economy") appears once, in the boilerplate clause that defines the Index. The affected party has descended two levels of aggregation between reports, and the naming does the work an adjective would otherwise do: the reader knows the result is concentrated because the list is short and specific.
- **What the data is said uniquely to show.** One superlative, and it is attached to a dataset rather than to a finding: "a first-of-its-kind bottom-up taxonomy of usage on Claude.ai". Same discipline as the first report — the vantage point is unprecedented, the findings are not said to be. The uniqueness claim is then immediately qualified into a hope rather than a result: "We hope this bottom-up taxonomy will be useful for researchers, and reveal use-cases that might be missed by top-down approaches". "We hope", "will be useful", "might be missed" — three hedges in one sentence, on the contribution the post is proudest of.
- **How soon the first number appears.** Very late, and this is the most imitable thing about the opening. The three headline bullets contain **no numbers at all** — where the first report's three bullets carried 36%, 4%, 57% and 43%. The first quantity in the piece is "630 granular categories", a count of dataset rows, roughly 230 words in. The first *measured* quantity is "1 million anonymized Claude.ai Free and Pro conversations", in the next section. The first percentage anywhere in the body prose is "almost 10%", roughly 600 words in, and the caption of the headline figure carries no number either. So a wave report's opening can state its three findings in the direction-and-rank vocabulary alone — "a rise in the share", "predominantly", "the highest amount", "among the highest amounts" — and defer every percentage to the section that earns it.
- **How the bullets are built.** Each bullet is a different grammatical type, deliberately. Bullet one is a change ("we've observed a rise in the share of usage for…"), fenced twice: "Since the launch of Claude 3.7 Sonnet" dates it, and "the share of usage" states that the quantity is compositional, not a level. Bullet two is a cross-section with a hedge in the adverb ("predominantly") and a list of exemplars introduced by "including those associated with occupations like", which concedes the list is illustrative. Bullet three is not a finding at all: its first sentence is a data release ("We're releasing data on…"), and the finding arrives as an example of what the data supports ("For example, …"). Two of the three bullets end in semicolons and the third in a full stop — the same punctuation tell as the first report's caveats list.
- **Register details worth copying.** Findings have no agent: "usage" rises, tasks "show", the balance is "unchanged". First person plural is reserved for institutional acts — "we launched", "we're releasing", "we've observed", "We hope" — with one exception worth noticing, "we've observed a rise", where the verb of observation is first-person and so the fence and the agent arrive together. Terms are defined in the clause that introduces them, by em-dash or relative clause, never in a glossary: "*task iteration*, where the human and model co-write something together"; "*directive* behavior—where the model completes the task with minimal human involvement". The same two terms are re-defined differently in the body section, in the vocabulary of the specific occupation being discussed — the definitions are local, and the piece does not treat that as an inconsistency to resolve.
- **Claude versus AI in the opening.** "AI" appears twice and both times in the definitional boilerplate about the Index ("AI's effects on labor markets"). Every sentence attached to something measured says Claude, Claude.ai or "the model". The first report's discipline holds here — but see the note at the end of `## Figure captions`, where it does not.

## Findings and their caveats

### Finding 1 — the wave-to-wave shift in the occupational mix

> When looking at the breakdown of these 1 million conversations, we see that the proportion of usage in several occupational categories has increased modestly, including coding, education and the sciences. While this increase in coding usage was expected due to Claude 3.7 Sonnet's improved scores on coding benchmarks, the increase in these other categories could reflect either ongoing diffusion of AI throughout the economy, novel applications of coding to those domains, or unexpected capability improvements in the model.

And the sample sentence that precedes it:

> We reran our previous analysis on data from the 11 days following the launch, covering 1 million anonymized Claude.ai Free and Pro conversations. The vast majority of the data we analyzed was from Claude 3.7 Sonnet, as it is set as the default on Claude.ai and our mobile app.

**Annotation.** The finding is stated without a number in the body and without a number in its caption; the only figure attached to it anywhere on the page is "+3%", which sits in an appendix caption two screens below. That is a real choice, not an oversight — the claim being made is directional, and "increased modestly" is the whole of the magnitude the piece is willing to assert. The hedging is layered and each layer does a different job: "the proportion of usage" (compositional, not a level), "several occupational categories" (unenumerated), "modestly" (magnitude), "including" (non-exhaustive). Note the sequencing of the mechanism sentence, which is the strongest construction in the report: the *expected* part of the result is conceded first and sourced ("this increase in coding usage was expected due to Claude 3.7 Sonnet's improved scores on coding benchmarks"), and only the residual is treated as needing explanation. Then three candidate explanations are named and none is chosen — "could reflect either ongoing diffusion of AI throughout the economy, novel applications of coding to those domains, or unexpected capability improvements in the model". Three alternatives, one modal, no adjudication, and no claim that the list is complete. This is the same refusal the first report made with two candidates for the wage gradient, scaled up. The caveat that is *not* here: the sample is the 11 days after a launch, and the sentence that says so ("data from the 11 days following the launch") is written as a description of the sample, never as a threat to the inference. A reader is given the fact and left to draw the exposure.

**Healthcare.** The opening bullet says "coding, as well as educational, science, and healthcare applications"; the body sentence says "coding, education and the sciences". Healthcare is dropped between the bullet and the body, carries no number anywhere, and does not reappear in the conclusion. Worth recording as a hazard of writing the bullets before the body: the synopsis lists one more item than the evidence section supports.

### Finding 2 — where extended thinking is used

> Our analysis reveals that Claude 3.7 Sonnet's extended thinking mode is predominantly used in technical and creative problem-solving contexts. Tasks associated with computer and information research scientists lead with almost 10% using extended thinking, followed by software developers at around 8%. Tasks associated with digital creative roles like multimedia artists (~7%) and video game designers (~6%) also show substantial usage.

> While these early usage patterns reveal insights about when people choose to use extended thinking mode, many important questions remain about this new model capability. To enable further research in this space, we're releasing a new dataset that maps each O\*NET task to its associated thinking mode fraction.

And the definition, one paragraph earlier:

> Claude 3.7 Sonnet features a new "extended thinking" mode which, when activated by the user, enables the model to think for longer when answering more complex questions.

**Annotation.** Interpretation first, then the ranking that supports it: "predominantly used in technical and creative problem-solving contexts" is asserted before any percentage, and the four numbers that follow are there to show the shape of the top of the distribution, not to be read individually. Every one of them is fenced by an approximator — "almost 10%", "around 8%", "(~7%)", "(~6%)" — and the fences escalate from words to tildes as the numbers get less important. The rhetorical unit is *lead / followed by / also show*, which orders the list without quantifying the gaps; the reader learns the ranking and is given no dispersion. Note what the numbers are *of*: "Tasks associated with computer and information research scientists lead with almost 10% using extended thinking" — the subject is tasks, the attribution to the occupation runs through "associated with", and the piece never says that computer scientists use extended thinking 10% of the time. That prepositional distance is carried consistently ("Tasks associated with", "tasks associated with copywriters and editors", "Tasks associated with Translators and Interpreters") and it is the main device by which the whole report avoids claiming it observed workers. Copy it. The caveat is a stated ignorance rather than a threat to validity: "many important questions remain about this new model capability", placed in the same sentence as the contribution ("While these early usage patterns reveal insights…"). It is followed by the handover, and the handover is the substantive response to the caveat — the dataset is released precisely because the report cannot answer the questions it has raised. The two things not said: no sample-wide extended-thinking rate is published, so the reader cannot tell whether 10% is high; and the mode is a user-set toggle, which the definition states plainly ("when activated by the user") without ever noting what that does to the interpretation of an occupational ranking.

### Finding 3 — augmentation and automation, unchanged in balance and changed in composition

> In our last report, we analyzed how AI usage varied between *augmentative* uses, like learning or iterating on an output, and *automative* uses, like asking the model to directly complete a task or debug errors. Our analysis shows the balance of augmentation and automation is essentially unchanged in our new data, with augmentation still comprising 57% of usage. However, we did see some change in *types* of automation and augmentation uses—for example, we saw *learning* interactions, where the user asks Claude for information or explanation about different topics, rise from ~23% to ~28%.

**Annotation.** A null and a positive in one paragraph, in that order, joined by "However" — the structure that makes a no-change result publishable. The null is stated twice over, in prose ("essentially unchanged") and in the word doing the comparison work ("augmentation **still** comprising 57%"): "still" is how the piece carries the previous wave's value without restating it, which is economical and also means the reader never sees the two numbers side by side. "Essentially unchanged" is the only characterisation of the null offered; no interval, no test, no statement of how large a difference would have counted, which for a claim of stability is the gap a referee reaches for first — in our own posts this sentence has to carry the minimum detectable effect. The positive finding is fenced at both ends ("~23% to ~28%") so the reader sees that the five-point move is approximate at both endpoints, and it is characterised only as "some change", with the emphasis placed on the *kind* of quantity that moved ("change in *types* of") rather than its size. Definitions again arrive inside the sentence that needs them, with examples rather than criteria: "*augmentative* uses, like learning or iterating on an output", "*automative* uses, like asking the model to directly complete a task or debug errors", "*learning* interactions, where the user asks Claude for information or explanation". Note "In our last report, we analyzed how **AI** usage varied" — the recap sentence says AI where the measurement said Claude, which is the loosest naming in the body.

### Finding 4 — how the mode split varies across occupational categories

> When splitting the data by high-level occupational categories, we find some categories are highly augmentative; for example, Community and Social Service tasks, which includes education and guidance counseling, approach 75% augmentation. At the other end of the extreme, tasks associated with production or computer and mathematical occupations, we see the balance skew closer to 50-50%. We don't see any occupational categories where automation dominates.

**Annotation.** A spread described by its two ends and then closed with a negative claim, which is the paragraph's real point: "We don't see any occupational categories where automation dominates." Seven words of subject-verb-object, no hedge, no scope condition — and the scope condition exists and is stated elsewhere, in the caption ("Occupational categories shown are limited to those with at least 0.5% representation in the data"). The lesson is a construction to *avoid*: the threshold that bounds the claim is in the caption and the claim is in the prose, so the sentence travels without its fence. Compare the first report's practice of putting "in our dataset" between the superlative and the subject. The numbers themselves are rendered as approach-values rather than point estimates — "approach 75% augmentation", "skew closer to 50-50%" — and the second is a range written as a ratio, which tells the reader that the interesting fact is the evenness, not the value. "At the other end of the extreme" is a slip for "at the other extreme"; it is in the source and is quoted as written.

### Finding 5 — occupations at the top of each interaction mode

> Getting more granular, we can also look at specific occupations within these occupational categories, as well as tasks associated with that occupation. For example, tasks associated with copywriters and editors show the highest amount of *task iteration*, where the user iterates on various writing and editing tasks with the model. By contrast, tasks associated with Translators and Interpreters show among the highest amounts of *directive* behavior—where the model is used for translating documents with minimal human involvement. Note that the O\*NET descriptions may not be optimally representative of what Claude is being used for—for example, while we see usage in the occupation "fine artists, including painters, sculptors, and illustrators," Claude is probably used far more for creating digital art than for painting or sculpture.

**Annotation.** The finding is a matched pair — "show the highest amount of *task iteration*" against "show among the highest amounts of *directive* behavior" — and the asymmetry of the two hedges is deliberate and worth copying: "the highest" for the claim the data supports outright, "among the highest" for the one it does not. "By contrast" is the whole of the argument; the two occupations are chosen because they sit at opposite poles of the same measure, so the comparison carries the finding and neither number needs to be stated in the prose (both are in the caption). Each mode is re-defined in the terms of the occupation at hand ("where the user iterates on various writing and editing tasks with the model"; "where the model is used for translating documents with minimal human involvement"), which is more concrete than the opening's generic glosses and inconsistent with them. The caveat is the third sentence of the same paragraph, introduced by the flattest possible signal ("Note that"), and it is the best-written limitation in the piece: it names the construct at fault (the O\*NET description), hedges the indictment ("may not be optimally representative"), then supplies a worked counter-example with the direction of the error stated ("Claude is probably used far more for creating digital art than for painting or sculpture"). A limitation that names which way the bias runs is actionable; this one is, and it sits adjacent to the claim it qualifies rather than 400 words away, which is the improvement this report makes on the first. It is then repeated, reworded, inside the figure caption — so it survives the figure being lifted out of the page.

**Naming discipline across the findings.** The rule the first report set holds in the body of this one, with the prepositional device doing extra work: "AI" is the subject when the sentence is about the phenomenon or the recap ("how AI usage varied", "ongoing diffusion of AI throughout the economy"), and Claude, Claude.ai, "the model" or "Claude 3.7 Sonnet" is the subject or object wherever a number is attached. Measured quantities are attributed to *tasks associated with* an occupation, never to the workers in it. The discipline breaks in one place only, an appendix caption, recorded below.

## Comparisons

### Wave to wave — V1 against V2, the comparison the report exists to make

The comparison is made five times, in five different registers, and no two are phrased alike.

**In the opening bullet, as a direction with no magnitude:**

> Since the launch of Claude 3.7 Sonnet, we've observed a rise in the share of usage for coding, as well as educational, science, and healthcare applications;

**In the body, as a magnitude given in an adverb:**

> we see that the proportion of usage in several occupational categories has increased modestly, including coding, education and the sciences

**In the headline caption, as an encoding — the comparison delegated entirely to colour:**

> **In the two months since our original data sample, we've seen an increase in the share of usage for coding, education, and the sciences. Graph shows share of Claude.ai Free and Pro traffic across top-level occupational categories in O\*NET. Grey shows the distribution from our first report covering data from Dec '25 - Jan '25. Colored bars show an increase (green) and decrease (blue) in the share of usage for our new data from Feb '25 - March '25. Note that the graph shows the share of usage rather than absolute usage. See Appendix for chart showing change across the full list of occupational categories.**

**In one adverb, for the null:**

> Our analysis shows the balance of augmentation and automation is essentially unchanged in our new data, with augmentation still comprising 57% of usage.

**In the appendix, as an area under a curve, with a conjecture attached:**

> We also recompute the "depth of task usage" plot from our original paper. We find generally a very similar curve to our first analysis. If anything we see slightly less area under the curve for the newer model—perhaps owing to an increase in concentration in our sample of conversations towards coding. That said, while we haven't seen a dramatic change in this curve over the last two months, we will continue to monitor as model capabilities and product surfaces continue to advance.

**Annotation.** Five observations, all of them transferable to any second-wave post.

1. **The baseline is named as a document, not as a number.** "our original data sample", "our first report", "our original paper", "our first analysis", "our last report" — the comparison is anchored to the previous publication rather than to a value, and "still" in the 57% sentence is the only place the previous value is carried at all. The effect is that V1 and V2 are never printed side by side in prose. Where a number *is* wanted, it is confined to the range form: "rise from ~23% to ~28%".
2. **The comparison label is defined in a caption, once.** "the two months between our data samples (V1 and V2)" is the only definition of V1 and V2 on the page, and it appears inside the augmentation caption. Every other use assumes it. If a post is going to abbreviate its waves, the definition belongs in the body.
3. **Direction is encoded, magnitude is not.** The headline exhibit is built as grey-baseline-plus-signed-colour ("Grey shows the distribution from our first report… Colored bars show an increase (green) and decrease (blue)"). That is the wave-comparison chart grammar: one series is the previous wave in neutral grey, the change is a signed overlay. Note that the colour key changes between the body figure (green/blue) and the appendix figure for the same encoding (yellow/blue) — the same variable is given two palettes in one document.
4. **The share/level fence is in the caption and stated as an instruction.** "Note that the graph shows the share of usage rather than absolute usage." One sentence, in the imperative register a reader obeys, at the point of the chart. It is the most important caveat in the report — a compositional rise is consistent with the numerator being flat — and it is never restated in prose.
5. **Nothing in the comparison is quantified as a change.** No difference, no ratio, no interval, no test; the only percentage-point figure on the page is in an appendix caption ("+3%"). What *is* supplied instead is a conjecture, marked as one: "perhaps owing to an increase in concentration in our sample of conversations towards coding", and then withdrawn to a promise to keep watching ("we will continue to monitor"). The pattern for a wave post is worth naming: **direction plus adverb in the body, one signed number in the appendix, mechanism offered as a conjecture, the rest deferred to the next wave.** Its cost is also worth naming: two things changed between the waves besides the calendar (the relevance filter and the classifier model, both in the last section of the page), and the prose never brings them into the same paragraph as the change they could explain.

### Method against method — top-down against bottom-up

> Our research so far has relied on the O\*NET dataset of tasks and occupations, which was created and maintained by the US Department of Labor. While O\*NET covers a very large number of tasks, O\*NET may not be the best taxonomy to describe the capabilities of general purpose models which can be used for tasks which are not present in O\*NET—and thus might be missed by our analysis.

> To address this gap, we're releasing a new bottom-up dataset of user activity patterns on Claude.ai. This dataset was also created with Clio, and uses the same dataset of anonymized conversations used for the above analysis, meaning that it enables comparisons between top-down and bottom-up approaches.

And from the opening:

> We hope this bottom-up taxonomy will be useful for researchers, and reveal use-cases that might be missed by top-down approaches which map usage onto a list of predefined tasks.

**Annotation.** A comparison of instruments, written as *the limit of the old instrument → the gap → the new instrument*. The indictment of the incumbent is hedged three times in one sentence ("may not be the best taxonomy", "can be used for", "might be missed") and it is an indictment of the report's own published method, which is what gives the passage its authority. The "same conversations" clause is the load-bearing one — "uses the same dataset of anonymized conversations used for the above analysis, meaning that it enables comparisons between top-down and bottom-up approaches" — because holding the sample fixed is what makes the two taxonomies comparable at all, and the sentence states the design property and the inference it licenses in one breath. The comparison is then not performed: "While we leave detailed analysis of this dataset to future work, we highlight a few particularly interesting clusters". A promise stated this precisely is a promise a reader can hold the series to, and the honest form is the one used here — the enabling condition claimed, the comparison explicitly deferred, no implication that it was done.

### Within the data — pole against pole

> we find some categories are highly augmentative; for example, Community and Social Service tasks, which includes education and guidance counseling, approach 75% augmentation. At the other end of the extreme, tasks associated with production or computer and mathematical occupations, we see the balance skew closer to 50-50%. We don't see any occupational categories where automation dominates.

> For example, tasks associated with copywriters and editors show the highest amount of *task iteration* … By contrast, tasks associated with Translators and Interpreters show among the highest amounts of *directive* behavior

**Annotation.** Two ends of one measure, each anchored to a named unit, with the middle left out — the same device the first report used for the wage gradient (shampooers and obstetricians) applied to a bounded proportion rather than a non-monotonic curve. Each pole gets a gloss that explains why it is where it is, inside the same clause ("which includes education and guidance counseling"; "where the user iterates on various writing and editing tasks with the model"), so the comparison is self-explaining. "By contrast" and "At the other end of" are the only connectives used; no dispersion statistic appears anywhere.

### Against expectation

> While this increase in coding usage was expected due to Claude 3.7 Sonnet's improved scores on coding benchmarks, the increase in these other categories could reflect either ongoing diffusion of AI throughout the economy, novel applications of coding to those domains, or unexpected capability improvements in the model.

**Annotation.** The expectation is stated, sourced to a specific external measure ("improved scores on coding benchmarks"), and used to *partition* the result: the expected part is conceded and set aside in a subordinate clause, and the analytical attention goes to the unexpected remainder. The word "unexpected" then reappears inside one of the three candidate explanations, which is a small and good piece of writing — the surprise is held open as a possible cause of itself.

### Against the source taxonomy

> Note that the O\*NET descriptions may not be optimally representative of what Claude is being used for—for example, while we see usage in the occupation "fine artists, including painters, sculptors, and illustrators," Claude is probably used far more for creating digital art than for painting or sculpture.

**Annotation.** A comparison between the label and the thing labelled, used to withdraw the literal reading of a row in a table. Structure: hedged general claim, em-dash, one concrete instance, direction of the discrepancy. Nineteen words of example do more than a paragraph of methodological discussion would.

### Exhibit 1 — V1 against V2 across top-level occupational categories

Pattern: *finding* → *what is plotted, in what unit, for what sample* → *series one with its colour and its provenance* → *series two with its colour and its dates* → *unit caveat* → *pointer to the fuller version*. The first sentence is a conclusion, not a description, which is a departure from the first report's captions — every one of those opened on what was plotted. Both series are dated inside the caption, which is how a wave chart should be labelled; the first date is written "Dec '25 - Jan '25" and is an error in the source for December 2024 (see `## Verification`) — quoted here as printed, never to be propagated. The caption carries no number at all, and it does two things no first-report caption did: it states a caveat as an instruction ("Note that the graph shows the share of usage rather than absolute usage"), and it tells the reader the exhibit is a selection and where the complete one is ("See Appendix for chart showing change across the full list of occupational categories"). Both are worth copying; a caption that admits its own selection is cheap insurance.

### Exhibit 2 — extended thinking by occupation

> **What tasks see the highest associated usage of extended thinking mode? Graph shows the O\*NET occupations with highest usage of thinking mode in their associated tasks. Occupations shown are limited to those with at least 0.5% representation in the data.**

**Annotation.** Pattern: *the question the exhibit answers, as a question* → *what is plotted* → *the inclusion threshold*. Opening a caption with a question mark mirrors the section headings and is the tightest device in the piece: the reader learns what to look for before being told what is drawn. Note the careful mismatch between sentence one ("what tasks") and sentence two ("the O\*NET occupations … in their associated tasks") — the chart is occupations, ranked by a task-level quantity, and the caption says so rather than smoothing it. The threshold sentence is a template: *unit shown are limited to those with at least X% representation in the data*. It appears in three captions on this page, in three slightly different wordings.

### Exhibit 3 — the mode split, V1 against V2

It is the finding restated with both of its hedges ("relatively constant", "appreciably") and the concessive that joins them ("though"), and it is also the only place on the page where V1 and V2 are defined. As a model it is the weakest caption here and the most instructive as a contrast — a caption built this way cannot be lifted, because a reader who sees only the image and this sentence does not know what is being divided by what. Our own captions state the unit even when the sentence states the finding.

### Exhibit 5 — top occupations by interaction type (two panels, one caption)

> **Top occupations by interaction type. **Annotation.** The longest caption on the page and the closest thing here to a house template, because it is the one caption that is fully self-contained. Its parts, in order: *title phrase* → *the construction rule, with the five categories enumerated* → *two worked examples with numbers* → *how the annotation inside each panel was chosen, with the selection rule stated* → *pointer to the companion figure* → *the taxonomy caveat, restated in the caption's own words* → *the inclusion threshold*. Four things to copy. (1) The five modes are enumerated in the caption, as the first report enumerated its five subtypes, so the figure introduces its own taxonomy. (2) The two example numbers ("librarians … at ~56%", "copy writers lead in Task Iteration at ~58%") appear nowhere in the body — the caption is the only source for them, and the tildes fence both. (3) The rule for the in-panel annotation is given explicitly, including its two inputs ("based on both how frequently the task occurs and how often that interaction mode is used within the task"), so a reader knows the highlighted task was selected by a stated criterion and not chosen for interest. (4) The O\*NET caveat is reworded rather than copied from the body — "usage on Claude.ai probably tilts more towards digital art than sculpture" against the body's "Claude is probably used far more for creating digital art than for painting or sculpture" — which is how a caveat survives being read in either place without reading as boilerplate. Two flaws in the source, recorded as written: the bold run breaks mid-caption, so the last two sentences render outside the bold on the live page; and "Figures for the other learning modes" says *learning* where the five categories are interaction modes.

### Exhibit 6 — the task-depth curve (Appendix)

> **The depth of task usage across occupations. For example, the graph shows that about 40% of occupations see AI usage in at least 20% of their tasks (where x=0.2, y≈0.4). There is little change in the curves between our first and second reports.**

**Annotation.** Pattern: *what is plotted* → *how to read one point on it, twice over* → *the wave comparison*. The middle sentence is the device worth stealing: a cumulative curve is hard to read, so the caption walks the reader through a single coordinate in words and then repeats it in notation ("about 40% of occupations see AI usage in at least 20% of their tasks (where x=0.2, y≈0.4)"). Stating the same fact in prose and in coordinates costs nine characters and removes all ambiguity about which axis is which. The number is hedged twice ("about 40%", "y≈0.4"). **This is also the one caption on the page where the naming discipline fails:** "40% of occupations see *AI* usage" attaches a measured percentage to AI, where every comparable sentence in the report attaches it to Claude or Claude.ai. The first report's captions said Claude without exception. In our own posts the finding says Claude even when the section it sits under says AI, and captions are included in that rule.

### Exhibit 7 — the full change across occupational categories (Appendix)

## Limitations

There is no section called Limitations or Caveats. The whole of the material that does that work, in the order a reader meets it:

**Inside captions** (quoted in full above): "Note that the graph shows the share of usage rather than absolute usage."; "See Appendix for chart showing change across the full list of occupational categories."; "Occupations shown are limited to those with at least 0.5% representation in the data."; "Occupational categories shown are limited to those with at least 0.5% representation in the data."; "Only occupations with at least 0.5% representation in the overall dataset are shown."

**Inside a finding**, as the third sentence of the paragraph it qualifies:

> Note that the O\*NET descriptions may not be optimally representative of what Claude is being used for—for example, while we see usage in the occupation "fine artists, including painters, sculptors, and illustrators," Claude is probably used far more for creating digital art than for painting or sculpture.

**As a stated ignorance, next to the contribution it motivates:**

> While these early usage patterns reveal insights about when people choose to use extended thinking mode, many important questions remain about this new model capability.

**As the motivation for the new dataset:**

> While O\*NET covers a very large number of tasks, O\*NET may not be the best taxonomy to describe the capabilities of general purpose models which can be used for tasks which are not present in O\*NET—and thus might be missed by our analysis.

**As a deferral:**

> While we leave detailed analysis of this dataset to future work, we highlight a few particularly interesting clusters

**And in the final H2, in full:**

> ## Additional methodological details

> While we mainly follow the methodology of our original report, we make a few changes which we document here for transparency:

> - In contrast to our last report, we do not filter based on whether conversations are relevant to an occupational category. Instead, we simply filter out conversations that flagged our safety classifiers. We find these approaches lead to similar results as our original analysis, while preserving more data that we can release via our bottom-up taxonomy of usage.
> - We use Claude 3.7 Sonnet in place of all cases where we previously used Claude 3.5 Sonnet. We found that using our newer model increased the accuracy of classifications according to the internal benchmarks we use to assess Clio's accuracy.

**Annotation.**

- **Where it sits.** Nowhere and everywhere. The first report gathered its caveats under an H3 inside Results, before the conclusion, so the close was read through them. This report distributes them, and the two that bear on the headline comparison are in the last section of the page, after the Appendix, under a heading that promises method rather than limits. A reader who stops at the Conclusion — which is where a reader stops — never learns that the relevance filter was dropped or that the classifier was swapped. The distribution has one real advantage, visible in Finding 5: a caveat placed in the paragraph it qualifies is read with the claim, which is what the first report failed to do when it put the augmentation misclassification 400 words from the 57/43 split. The right lesson is to do both — adjacent caveats *and* a gathered section — not to choose.
- **The framing sentence.** "While we mainly follow the methodology of our original report, we make a few changes which we document here for transparency". "For transparency" recasts a threat to comparability as an act of disclosure; "a few changes" and "mainly" are the only quantification of how far the two waves diverge. There is no equivalent of the first report's "as with all studies it has important limitations" — the ritual sentence is absent, and so is the ritual heading.
- **How specific.** The threshold caveats are exact and repeated at every figure they apply to: "at least 0.5% representation in the data" is stated three times rather than once, so no exhibit can be read without it. The O\*NET caveat is the most specific in the piece and the only one with a worked counter-example and a stated direction of error. The two methodological bullets are specific about *what* changed and silent about *how much* it mattered: "We find these approaches lead to similar results as our original analysis" carries no number, no table and no figure, and it is the only statement anywhere of how comparable V1 and V2 are. The accuracy claim rests on "the internal benchmarks we use to assess Clio's accuracy", named but not described, not published, not versioned, and with no magnitude attached.
- **Which are weakest.** The unquantified "similar results", which is load-bearing for every wave-to-wave sentence in the report; the absence of any statement of how large a difference would have had to be for "essentially unchanged" to fail; and the 0.5% floors, which are stated in captions and then not carried into the prose claim they bound ("We don't see any occupational categories where automation dominates"). No standard error, interval, test or minimum detectable effect appears anywhere on the page.
- **What is not withdrawn.** The first report's strongest caveat converted a limitation into a withdrawn claim ("we don't argue that the uses in our dataset are a representative sample of AI use in general"). This report withdraws nothing. There is no statement that the 11 days after a launch might be unrepresentative, no statement that the Free-and-Pro sample is not the market, and no restatement of the first report's representativeness disclaimer. The sample boundary is stated once, as a neutral fact, inside the sentence that describes the data: "1 million anonymized Claude.ai Free and Pro conversations."

## Close

Verbatim, the Conclusion in full:

> ## Conclusion

> As models continue to advance, so too must our measurement of their economic impacts. In our second report, covering data since the launch of Claude 3.7 Sonnet, we find relatively modest increases in coding, education, and scientific use cases, and no change in the balance of augmentation and automation. We find that Claude's new extended thinking mode is used with the highest frequency in technical domains and tasks, and identify patterns in automation / augmentation patterns across tasks and occupations. We release datasets for both of these analyses.

> In the coming months, we aim to continue tracking these metrics and developing new ones as capabilities improve and models continue to be applied across the economy.

**Annotation.**

- **What was learned.** Restated in order, with every hedge carried over intact and not one number: "relatively modest increases", "no change in the balance", "used with the highest frequency in technical domains and tasks", "identify patterns". The close is a summary, which is the opposite of the first report's choice — that close mentioned none of its four findings and treated them as a baseline for future monitoring. The discipline that saves this one is hedge-matching: "relatively modest" in the close against "increased modestly" in the body, "no change" against "essentially unchanged", "highest frequency" against "predominantly". Nothing is promoted on the way out. That is the test to apply to any summarising close — read the close's adjectives against the body's and check that none has been upgraded. Note also that the fourth finding (the category spread, and "we don't see any occupational categories where automation dominates") is compressed to "identify patterns" rather than restated; the close declines to carry forward the claim whose scope condition lives in a caption.
- **Why it matters.** One sentence, first, and it is about measurement rather than about the economy: "As models continue to advance, so too must our measurement of their economic impacts." The stake claimed is the obsolescence of the instrument, not a consequence for workers. This is the wave-report form of why-it-matters and it is much weaker than the first report's conditional extrapolation ("If it remains the case that AI is used only for certain tasks … the future might be one where most current jobs evolve rather than disappear"). No conditional, no policy vocabulary, no consequence named. For our own posts the lesson is the first report's, not this one's: state the inference, state what it depends on, keep the modal verb.
- **What comes next.** "In the coming months, we aim to continue tracking these metrics and developing new ones as capabilities improve and models continue to be applied across the economy." Committal about cadence, vague about content: "these metrics" is unenumerated, where the first report named the two quantities it would monitor. "Aim to" is softer than the first report's "we'll repeat". The promises with real content are elsewhere in the piece and are better made — the extended-thinking dataset released "To enable further research in this space", the cluster analysis "left to future work", the task curve that "we will continue to monitor". A close that gathered those three would have been the stronger ending.
- **Recommendation.** None, to anyone. No policy sentence, no refusal of a policy sentence, no invitation, no feedback form (the researcher input form is cited mid-body, as the *source of a request already answered*, which is the best institutional move in the piece: a promise made elsewhere, discharged here, with the requester named as a constituency). Where the first report's close explicitly declined to prescribe, this one does not raise the question.
- **How it avoids a template.** Partly, and less than the first report does. Three things save it. (1) Zero numbers, as in the first report: a close that restates findings without restating figures cannot be mistaken for an abstract, and forces the reader back into the body for any quantity. (2) The first sentence is about the world and about the report's own obligation, not about the report's achievements. (3) The closing paragraph is one sentence long, and the whole close is roughly 120 words for a 1,400-word piece — it ends by stopping. What it does *not* avoid is the structure of a template: findings enumerated in body order, then a cadence promise. It is short enough that the enumeration costs little, and length is the discipline doing the work here.
- **Title against ending.** Title: "Anthropic Economic Index: Insights from Claude 3.7 Sonnet" — a series name, a colon, and a model. Ending: measurement must advance as models do, and the tracking continues. The two match, and the match is structural rather than substantive: the title pegs the piece to a model release and the close promises the same treatment at the next one, which is exactly what a numbered wave in a series should say. No finding is named in the title, and correspondingly no finding is promoted in the close. For a single-finding post the inverse holds — if the title names a finding, the close must end on that finding's consequence.
- **First person.** Throughout, and here for *findings* as well as for institutional acts: "we find relatively modest increases", "We find that Claude's new extended thinking mode is used…", "we aim to continue tracking". The first report reserved the first person for commitments and left findings agentless. Our house style forbids the first person altogether; the substitute for "we find X" is the agentless finding the body of this very report already uses ("the proportion of usage … has increased modestly"), and the substitute for "we aim to continue tracking" is to attribute the commitment to the series. The rhetorical work — a promise that binds the author — has to be replaced, not merely de-personalised.
