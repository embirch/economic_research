# skill-formation-rct-2026-01 — style annotation

## Source

- **Two titles, one study.** Web page H1: "How AI assistance impacts the formation of coding skills". Paper: "How AI Impacts Skill Formation" (PDF p. 1; the same string on the arXiv abstract page and in the page's own BibTeX block, `title = {How AI Impacts Skill Formation}`). The page title names the domain and the mechanism; the paper title names neither and reads as the research question. Neither title contains a finding. What follows from that is annotated in `## Close`.
- **Date.** Page dateline: "Jan 29, 2026", set under the H1. arXiv: "[Submitted on 28 Jan 2026 (v1), last revised 1 Feb 2026 (this version, v2)]". The v2 PDF cover carries "February 3, 2026" — a cover date two days after the version it belongs to was submitted. Three dates for one artefact; `wiki/INDEX.md` carries 2026-01-29, the page's.
- **URLs fetched:** https://www.anthropic.com/research/AI-assistance-coding-skills (page); https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.20245 (abstract page); https://arxiv.org/pdf/2601.20245 (v2 PDF, 31 pages, 2,860,457 bytes, PDF 1.7). Licence on the abstract page: CC BY-NC-ND 4.0. Subjects: "Computers and Society (cs.CY); Artificial Intelligence (cs.AI); Human-Computer Interaction (cs.HC)" — and the page's BibTeX gives `primaryClass = {cs.LG}`, a fourth class that arXiv does not list. v2 comment: "V2: Fixed typos in Table 1 (Balance Table), corrected osf link".
- **Which is the document of record.** Per `room/director-2026-09-16-pdf-web-ruling.md` the PDF is the record and page references point to it; the page is the companion. This pair is the corpus's sharpest case for that rule, because the two documents do not merely differ in length — **they publish different quantities for the same result** (the paper gives the difference in quiz points, the page gives the two group levels; neither gives both). Every such divergence is recorded at the point of use below and adjudicated nowhere.
- **Type.** A pre-registered two-arm randomised controlled trial (n=52) with four pilot studies, written up as an arXiv working paper with a companion research post. This is the only RCT in the style corpus and the only piece in it whose evidence is primary experimental data rather than Claude usage data.
- **Bylines, and the asymmetry.** The paper has a full academic byline on p. 1 — "Judy Hanwen Shen∗ Alex Tamkin†" with footnotes "∗ Work done as a part of the Anthropic Fellows Program, judy@anthropic.com" and "† Anthropic, atamkin@anthropic.com". **The page has no byline at all.** Authorship appears only in an H3 "Acknowledgments" after the Conclusion: "This project was led by Judy Hanwen Shen and Alex Tamkin. Editorial support for this blog post was provided by Jake Eaton, Stuart Ritchie, and Sarah Pollack." So the page is institutionally voiced (unsigned, like the Economic Index reports) while the paper is personally voiced, and the page discloses that it was copy-edited by three named people. The prose difference this produces is visible in every section below and is summarised at the end of `## Close`; it is the most useful single fact in this file, because our posts have one text that has to pass both readings.
- **Filed under Alignment, not Economics.** The page's subject tag above the H1 reads "Alignment"; the three "Related content" items are Frontier Red Team evaluations, a cybersecurity alignment assessment, and Fermat's Last Theorem. A labour-economics experiment about junior developers is shelved with oversight research, and the framing sentence that earns the shelf is in the opening: "humans will still need the skills to catch errors, guide output, and ultimately provide oversight for AI deployed in high-stakes environments." **The stake chosen determines the shelf; the shelf then determines the audience the prose addresses.**
- **Length and shape.** Paper: 31 pages, roughly 7,900 words of body prose across pp. 1–20 (the wiki author's estimate from the extracted text, excluding tables, figure text, references and appendices), plus four appendices A–D, 27 numbered figures and 7 numbered tables. Page: roughly 2,000 words of body prose, three H2 sections, two H3 subsections, two uncaptioned figures, one footnote. The page is a quarter of the paper and carries about a third of its findings.
- **The audience each addresses.** The paper glosses nothing about software engineering and everything about learning theory (Kolb, problem-based learning) and CS-education assessment design (item response theory, local item dependence); its citations are bracketed author–year. The page glosses nothing at all, links instead of citing, and translates every statistic into a schoolroom unit ("nearly two letter grades", "the hand-coding group"). Neither document defines Trio for an economist; both define it for a programmer — "a new asynchronous programming library", "which requires understanding concepts related to asynchronous programming, a skill often learned in a professional setting". **The page's one act of translation is into grades, not into economics**, which is worth noticing: an economics reader gets no wage, no cohort, no aggregate anywhere in either document.
- **Share-card metadata, which overstates.** `og:description`, `twitter:description` and `<meta name="description">` all read: "A randomized trial with 52 developers found AI assistance lowered comprehension scores, except when used for explanations rather than delegation." The clause after the comma asserts a conditional effect that the page itself declines two paragraphs earlier — "Our qualitative analysis does not draw a causal link between interaction patterns and learning outcomes". The share card is the only text most readers see. **A description field is a quantitative claim and takes the same discipline as a sentence; this one is the exact fault our claims list exists to prevent.**
- **The measured tool is not Claude, and neither document says so in the same place.** The paper names it once, in the methods: "The base model used for this assistant is GPT-4o, and the model is prompted to be an intelligent coding assistant" (p. 6). The page never names a model. Claude appears on the page three times and never as the thing under test: the Claude.ai link in the second sentence (a prior Anthropic study), footnote 1 ("this setup is different from agentic coding products like Claude Code"), and the Claude Code Learning/Explanatory mode link in the close. The naming consequence for our programme is the inverse of our usual rule and is annotated at the end of `## Findings and their caveats`.

## Section order

**The paper.** Headings in order, with what each does and roughly how long it runs (word counts are the wiki author's estimates from the extracted text). Bold run-in headings set inline are the paper's main device below the subsection level, as in `economic-index-2025-02-paper`.

1. **Front matter (p. 1)** — title, two authors with footnotes, cover date. No affiliation line, no keywords, no JEL codes.
2. **Abstract (p. 1)** — ~170 words, one paragraph. Premise from the literature, the gap, the mechanism worry, the design, the negative finding, the null, the subgroup result, the typology, the policy conjecture. **It contains no numeral except the spelled-out "six".**
3. **1 Introduction (pp. 1–3)** — ~640 words. History, two analogies, the supervision through-line, the literature, the motivating questions. Figure 1 (the whole-paper overview) sits on p. 2 inside it.

Two literatures the paper must join: labour-economics productivity evidence and CS-education assessment.
6. **3 Framework (pp. 4–5)** — ~280 words, three run-ins: **Professional Skill Acquisition**, **AI for Coding Usage Patterns**, **Research Questions**. Ends with RQ1 and RQ2 as display bullets. Figure 2 (a conceptual diagram, not data) sits here.

Preamble and the four axes as bullets; **6.1 AI Interaction** (run-ins **Interaction Time**, **AI Queries**, **Adopting AI Advice: Pasting vs Manual Code Copying**); **6.2 Encountering Errors** (**Error Frequency**, **Errors and Trio Skill**); **6.3 Shifts in Active Coding Time**; **6.4 Participant Feedback**. Figures 17–27 and Tables 5–7 live in the appendices.

**Three structural facts about the paper worth carrying forward.**

- **There is no Limitations section and no Conclusion section.** The limitations are the six bullets under a heading that says *Future Work*; the conclusion is an unlabelled paragraph underneath them. A reader scanning the table of contents finds neither word. The April 2025 report's promotion of Limitations to a top-level heading remains the better practice and our template keeps it.
- **The pilots are reported as results, not as methods.** §5.1 is the first thing in the Results section, and it publishes four failed designs with their failure rates — "35% of participants in the no AI condition used AI assistance" (Table 2, Pilot A), "around 25% in the control group participants still used AI" (p. 8). Putting the design history where the results go is unusual and it is the right instinct: **the reason the main study is believable is the four studies that were not believable, so they belong in front of the reader, not in an appendix.** Our template's "what was set in advance" section is the place this goes.
- **Every finding is one paragraph from the procedure that produced it**, exactly as in `economic-index-2025-02-paper`: the mechanism section defines its annotation events in the sentence before it uses them ("We capture the time invested in interacting with AI by labeling the time between when users start to type a query (annotated as an AI Interaction event) and when an answer from the AI assistant is produced (annotated as an AI Query event)").

**The page.** Five unheaded lede paragraphs → **H2 "Study design"** (two paragraphs on sample and stages, one on the interface, then an uncaptioned figure) → **H3 "Evaluation design"** (the four question types as bullets, then what was emphasised) → **H2 "Results"** (one paragraph, then an uncaptioned figure) → **H3 "Qualitative analysis: AI interaction modes"** (nine paragraphs and six bullets — the largest block on the page) → **H2 "Conclusion"** (six paragraphs) → H3 Acknowledgments, BibTeX block, H3 Footnotes. A "Read the paper" button sits above the prose and "Read the [full paper](…) for details." closes it: **the page brackets itself with two pointers to the record.**

**Where the findings sit, and the proportions.** Paper: four times — abstract (words only), §1.1 (numbers), §5.2.2 (numbers with figures), §7 (words plus two range bands). Page: twice — lede paragraph five, and the Results paragraph. The page has exactly one paragraph of quantitative results and nine paragraphs of mechanism; the paper devotes ~490 words to the main result and ~2,400 to the mechanism. **Both documents spend four times as many words on *how* the effect happened as on the effect.** For a trial with one significant estimate and one null, that allocation is the correct one and it is the structural lesson of this pair: when the design yields few estimates, the post is carried by the account of the process, and the account has to be built from something measured (here, annotated screen recordings), not from speculation.

**Where the method sits.** Page: after the headline, in its own H2, in three paragraphs, with the evaluation instrument as a subsection. Paper: in §4, before any result. Both defer the *randomisation procedure* — neither document states how allocation was performed (the balance claim is "Participants were balanced across the following attributes", p. 9); only the pre-registration has the stratification rule, and `wiki/reports/skill-formation-rct-2026-01.md` records it. **The one methods sentence a referee wants first is in neither published document.**

## Opening move

### The page, verbatim — the whole lede, five paragraphs, ending on the headline

> Research shows AI helps people do [parts](…) of their job faster. In an observational [study](…) of [Claude.ai](…) data, we found AI can speed up some tasks by 80%. But does this increased productivity come with trade-offs? Other research shows that when people use AI assistance, they become [less engaged with their work](…) and [reduce](…) the effort they put into doing it—in other words, they offload their thinking to AI.

> It's unclear whether this cognitive offloading can prevent people from growing their skills on the job, or—in the case of coding—understanding the systems they're building. Our latest study, a randomized controlled trial with software developers as participants, investigates this potential downside of using AI at work.

> This question has broad implications—for how to design AI products that facilitate learning, for how workplaces should approach AI policies, and for broader societal resilience, among others. We focused on coding, a field where AI tools have rapidly become standard. Here, AI creates a potential tension: as coding grows more automated and speeds up work, humans will still need the skills to catch errors, guide output, and ultimately provide oversight for AI deployed in high-stakes environments. Does AI provide a shortcut to *both* skill development and increased efficiency? Or do productivity increases from AI assistance undermine skill development?

> In a randomized controlled trial, we examined 1) how quickly software developers picked up a new skill (in this case, a Python library) with and without AI assistance; and 2) whether using AI made them less likely to understand the code they'd just written.

> We found that using AI assistance led to a statistically significant decrease in mastery. On a quiz that covered concepts they'd used just a few minutes before, participants in the AI group scored 17% lower than those who coded by hand, or the equivalent of nearly two letter grades. Using AI sped up the task slightly, but this didn't reach the threshold of statistical significance.

### The paper, verbatim — the abstract in full, and the first paragraph of the Introduction

> AI assistance produces significant productivity gains across professional domains, particularly for novice workers. Yet how this assistance affects the development of skills required to effectively supervise AI remains unclear. Novice workers who rely heavily on AI to complete unfamiliar tasks may compromise their own skill acquisition in the process. We conduct randomized experiments to study how developers gained mastery of a new asynchronous programming library with and without the assistance of AI. We find that AI use impairs conceptual understanding, code reading, and debugging abilities, without delivering significant efficiency gains on average. Participants who fully delegated coding tasks showed some productivity improvements, but at the cost of learning the library. We identify six distinct AI interaction patterns, three of which involve cognitive engagement and preserve learning outcomes even when participants receive AI assistance. Our findings suggest that AI-enhanced productivity is not a shortcut to competence and AI assistance should be carefully adopted into workflows to preserve skill formation – particularly in safety-critical domains.

> Since the industrial revolution, skills in the labor market have continually shifted in response to the introduction of new technology; the role of workers often shifts from performing the task to supervising the task [Autor et al., 2001]. For example, the automation of factory robots has enabled humans to move from manual labor to supervision, and accounting software has enabled professionals to move from performing raw calculations to identifying better bookkeeping and tax strategies. In both scenarios, humans are responsible for the quality of the final product and are liable for any errors [Bleher and Braun, 2022]. Even as automation changes the process of completing tasks, technical knowledge to identify and fix errors remains extremely important.

**Annotation.**

- **The page's opening move is the one our programme should steal outright: it opens on Anthropic's own published number and turns it into a question.** Sentence two states the prior result and sources it ("In an observational study of Claude.ai data, we found AI can speed up some tasks by 80%"); sentence three is the stake, in nine words — "But does this increased productivity come with trade-offs?" The house's own finding is the premise, the new study is the test of its cost, and the reader's attention is bought with a number the reader may already have seen. **A post founded on an Anthropic release has exactly this move available, and it is cheaper and stronger than any framing that starts from the world.** Note also that the prior number is attributed and hyperlinked and never used again until the close, where it is reconciled (see `## Comparisons`).
- **The hypotheses and the way to tell them apart are two sentences, both interrogative, and they are the last thing in the framing.** "Does AI provide a shortcut to *both* skill development and increased efficiency? Or do productivity increases from AI assistance undermine skill development?" One design, two outcomes, two rival worlds: in the first, speed and learning move together; in the second, they trade off. The very next paragraph enumerates the two outcomes in the same order, numbered — "1) how quickly software developers picked up a new skill … 2) whether using AI made them less likely to understand the code they'd just written". **The rival hypotheses are stated as a disjunction and the outcome measures are numbered in the same order in the next sentence, so the reader can see which measurement discriminates which world.** That is precisely what our template's "hypotheses and how to tell them apart" asks for, and this is the corpus's cleanest instance of it.
- **The stake is triple and it is stated abstractly, then narrowed with a reason.** "for how to design AI products that facilitate learning, for how workplaces should approach AI policies, and for broader societal resilience, among others. We focused on coding, a field where AI tools have rapidly become standard." Three constituencies (product, employer, society), then the domain restriction with its justification in an apposition. "among others" is the one loose phrase; a why-it-matters list that ends in "among others" is a list that was not closed.
- **The paper's opening is the lineage opening, not the gap opening.** Two hundred and fifty years, two worked analogies (factory robots, accounting software), and a single through-line — performing to supervising — established before AI is mentioned. The same device as the February 2025 report's Spinning Jenny, with one improvement: **each analogy is carried to the same conclusion, that liability stays with the human**, which is what makes the study's outcome variable (comprehension, not output) the obviously right one. By the fourth sentence the reader has been told why a *quiz* is the dependent variable of an economics experiment.
- **The abstract of a randomised controlled trial that contains no effect size.** Not one numeral: the design is "randomized experiments", the effect is "impairs conceptual understanding, code reading, and debugging abilities", the null is "without delivering significant efficiency gains on average", the typology is "six distinct AI interaction patterns". The first number in the paper arrives about 1,000 words later, in §1.1. This is a deliberate register choice and it has a cost and a benefit. The benefit: the abstract cannot be misquoted numerically and every clause survives a re-analysis. The cost: a reader cannot tell whether the impairment is large, and the paper's own re-expression of it ("17% or two grade points") is the thing that made the headline. **Our template's answer is the opposite and stays the opposite — every finding sentence carries its number and its caveat together — but the abstract's verb discipline is worth copying: "impairs", "without delivering", "showed some", "preserve" are graded, and none of them is "shows" or "proves".**
- **Where the first number is, in each document.** Page: "80%" in the second sentence — but it is someone else's finding (the house's own earlier work); the study's own first number is "17% lower" about 180 words in, and it arrives *after* the design, not before. Paper: no number until §1.1. **Both documents state the design before the first number of their own**, which is the ordering a trial needs and the inverse of the observational pieces in this corpus.
- **The single word that carries the page's whole argument.** "mastery" — "led to a statistically significant decrease in mastery". The instrument is a 14-question quiz taken minutes after the task; the construct claimed is mastery. The paper is more careful in the same position, naming the instrument inside the noun phrase: "the level of skill formation gained by completing the task, **measured by our quiz**, is significantly reduced" (p. 9). **The fence belongs inside the sentence that first states the finding, not in a methods section the skimmer will not reach**, and the page is the version that drops it.
- **First person, and what it costs us.** Both documents run on "we" — institutional in the page's "we found", "we examined", disciplinary-collective in the page's close ("the way we interact with AI while trying to be efficient affects how much we learn"), and authorial in the paper throughout ("We conduct", "We find", "We hypothesize"). Every finding sentence here rebuilds agentless without loss: "We find that AI use impairs conceptual understanding" → "AI use impairs conceptual understanding". The one construction that does not survive is the page's collective "we", and the substitute is to name the population ("developers learning a new library"), which is weaker; knowing it is weaker is the point of recording it.

## Findings and their caveats

Four findings, two of which are pre-registered, one exploratory and one qualitative — and the paper labels which is which, in the sentence, every time. That labelling is the most transferable thing in this section.

### Finding 1 — the effect on comprehension

Paper, §1.1 (p. 2), the summary form:

> We find that using AI assistance to complete tasks that involve this new library resulted in a reduction in the evaluation score by 17% or two grade points (Cohen's d = 0.738, p = 0.010).

Paper, §5.2.2 (p. 9), the full form:

> Figure 6 shows that while using AI to complete our coding task did not significantly improve task completion time, the level of skill formation gained by completing the task, measured by our quiz, is significantly reduced (Cohen d=0.738, p=0.01). There is a 4.15 point difference between the means of the treatment and control groups. For a 27-point quiz, this translates into a 17% score difference or 2 grade points. Controlling for warm-up task time as a covariate, the treatment effect remains significant (Cohen's d=0.725, p=0.016).

Page, the whole Results paragraph:

> On average, participants in the AI group finished about two minutes faster, although the difference was not statistically significant. There was, however, a significant difference in test scores: the AI group averaged 50% on the quiz, compared to 67% in the hand-coding group—or the equivalent of nearly two letter grades (Cohen's *d*=0.738, *p*=0.01). The largest gap in scores between the two groups was on debugging questions, suggesting that the ability to understand when code is incorrect and why it fails may be a particular area of concern if AI impedes coding development.

**Annotation.**

- **The paper's four-sentence shape is the model: estimate in standardised units → the raw difference in the instrument's own units → the re-expression for a lay reader, with the denominator named → the robustness check.** "There is a 4.15 point difference…" then "For a 27-point quiz, this translates into…" — the base ("a 27-point quiz") is inside the sentence that does the converting, and the word "translates" marks the re-expression as a re-expression rather than a second result. This is the same discipline as the coding-agents post's "In percentage terms…", and it is the right way to give a number twice.
- **The covariate-adjusted estimate is one sentence, immediately after, with both statistics restated.** "Controlling for warm-up task time as a covariate, the treatment effect remains significant (Cohen's d=0.725, p=0.016)." No table, no discussion, no claim that the adjustment was pre-specified (it was; the pre-registration lists warm-up time among the permitted covariates, recorded in `wiki/reports/skill-formation-rct-2026-01.md`). **A robustness result belongs in the sentence after the headline, in the same units, with its own p — a paragraph of its own overstates it and an appendix hides it.**
- **The two documents publish different halves of the same finding, and neither publishes both.** The paper gives the difference (4.15 points) and never the group means; the page gives the group means (50% and 67%) and never the difference. The corpus's rule — a difference is reported in its units and both levels are given — is satisfied by the pair and by neither member of it. And because the two expressions are in different documents, the reader who has only the page cannot check the re-expression, while the reader who has only the paper cannot size the gap against the scale. `wiki/reports/skill-formation-rct-2026-01.md` records that the published expressions do not reconcile arithmetically; **for our purposes the writing lesson is prior to the arithmetic one: one effect, four expressions (points, per cent, letter grades, two levels), never two of them in one sentence, is how a discrepancy survives publication.**
- **The page's "hand-coding group" is a better label than "control".** It says what the arm did rather than what role it played in the design, and it is symmetrical with "the AI group". Copy it: naming arms by their behaviour rather than their statistical function costs nothing and removes a gloss.
- **The subarea result is attached to the headline sentence on the page and separated from it in the paper.** Page: "The largest gap in scores between the two groups was on debugging questions, suggesting that…" — in the same paragraph as the headline, with "suggesting" doing the hedging and no mention that the decomposition was exploratory. Paper: "**In exploratory data analysis (not pre-registered)**, the quiz score was decomposed into subareas and question types (Figure 8)" (p. 11). **The two-word parenthesis "(not pre-registered)" is the single most valuable phrase in this pair for our template**, and the page deletes it — which is how an exploratory ordering ends up in the headline paragraph and in the share-card description. Ours goes in the sentence, not in a methods note.

### Finding 2 — the speed null

Paper, §1.1: "Meanwhile, we did not find a statistically significant acceleration in completion time with AI assistance (Figure 6)."

Paper, p. 11: "Our results do not conclusively find a speed up or slow down using AI in this task."

Paper, §7 (p. 18): "Contrary to our initial hypothesis, we did not observe a significant performance boost in task completion in our main study. While using AI improved the average completion time of the task, the improvement in efficiency was not significant in our study, despite the AI Assistant being able to generate the complete code solution when prompted."

Page: "Using AI sped up the task slightly, but this didn't reach the threshold of statistical significance." / "On average, participants in the AI group finished about two minutes faster, although the difference was not statistically significant."

**Annotation.**

- **"do not conclusively find a speed up or slow down" is the best null sentence in the corpus.** It names *both* directions as unexcluded, which is what absence of evidence actually means, and it does so without the word "no effect". Compare the February 2025 report's "there wasn't evidence in this dataset of jobs being entirely automated" and the coding-agents post's "We find no evidence that…": both are correct and both leave the reader free to read them as zero. **A null stated as a two-sided failure to exclude is the honest form, and it is one clause longer than the dishonest one.** What it still is not is a minimum detectable effect: nothing anywhere in either document says what speed difference this design could have ruled out, and with 26 per arm that is the first thing a referee asks. Our template requires the MDE beside the null; this pair shows how far good prose gets without one, and where it stops.
- **"Contrary to our initial hypothesis" is the pre-registration sentence.** Five words at the head of the Discussion's second paragraph, naming the failed prediction as the authors' own before interpreting it. Not "surprisingly", not "interestingly" — the expectation is identified as a hypothesis, which implies it was held in advance, and the pilot section has already shown the reader why it was held (Pilot D found the speed effect at d=1.11). **A finding that contradicts the author's own registered expectation is worth more than one that confirms it, and saying so in five words is the whole cost.**
- **The concessive carries direction and denies significance in one sentence, in both documents.** "While using AI improved the average completion time of the task, the improvement in efficiency was not significant"; "Using AI sped up the task slightly, but this didn't reach the threshold of statistical significance". The direction is never suppressed and never promoted. The page then quantifies it — "about two minutes faster" — which the paper's prose does not, and this is the one place the page is *more* forthcoming than the record. It is also the riskiest sentence on the page: a point estimate with no interval inside a sentence that denies significance invites exactly the reading it disclaims. **If a null is quantified, the interval or the MDE goes in the same sentence.**
- **The null is then explained rather than excused, and the explanation was measured.** "By analyzing how participants in the AI condition completed the task, the reason for the lack of improved productivity was due to the time spent interacting with the AI assistant" (p. 14), with the annotation events defined in the next sentence and a distribution figure behind it. The page's version: "One surprising result was how much time participants spent interacting with the AI assistant. Several took up to 11 minutes (30% of the total time allotted) composing up to 15 queries. This helped to explain why, on average, participants using AI finished faster although the productivity improvement was not statistically significant." **A null with a measured mechanism behind it is a finding; a null with a story is an excuse.** Note the page's "This helped to explain why … participants using AI finished faster", which reads as explaining the positive difference rather than the absence of a significant one — the sentence has been compressed past its own logic.
- **The forward-looking hedge on the null is stated as a scope condition, not as a defence.** Page: "We expect AI would be more likely to significantly increase productivity when used on repetitive or familiar tasks." One sentence, in the indicative of expectation, naming the setting where the opposite result should be expected. That is how a null is bounded without being explained away.

### Finding 3 — heterogeneity used as a design check

> Across all levels of prior coding experience, users scored higher on average in the control (no AI) than in the treatment (AI assistance) group (Figure 7). This shows that our choice of tasks and task design did not critically hinge on the participants' experience level of the but presented new skills to be acquired for every experience group. *(p. 11; the garbled clause is the source's)*

> While we do observe a slightly lower average completion time in the AI group among novice programmers, due to the small group size of the 1-3 year participant group (n=4), the difference in task time was not significant. *(p. 11)*

**Annotation.**

- **A heterogeneity plot is repurposed as evidence that the task was new to everyone, which is a validity argument rather than a finding.** The consistency of the sign across all three experience bands is used to show that the *task* worked as designed, not that the effect is homogeneous. That inversion is worth having in the toolkit: **a subgroup figure that shows the same sign everywhere is evidence about the instrument, and saying so is more informative than reporting three insignificant subgroup estimates.** The sentence that does it is the worst-written in the paper (an editing failure that survived two arXiv versions), which is a separate lesson about the value of the page's copy-editing pass.
- **The small-cell result is withheld in the same sentence that reports it, with the n as the stated reason.** "due to the small group size of the 1-3 year participant group (n=4), the difference in task time was not significant". The cell size is not in a footnote, not in a caption, not in a limitations bullet — it is the subordinate clause that cancels the claim. **This is exactly the construction our noise-check rule wants: the number that disqualifies a result sits inside the sentence that would otherwise make it.**

### Finding 4 — the typology, and how a six-cluster taxonomy of 24 people is written

> Using these axes, we develop a typology of six AI interaction patterns based on query types, number of queries, queries per task, and active time. As a result of this categorization, these six patterns yield different outcomes for both completion time and skill formation (i.e., quiz score). … Although each behavior pattern cluster is small, the difference between low-scoring clusters and high-scoring clusters is stark. *(p. 12)*

> **AI Delegation (n=4):** Participants in this group wholly relied on AI to write code and complete the task. This group completed the task the fastest and encountered few or no errors in the process. *(p. 12)*

> **Generation-Then-Comprehension (n=2):** Participants in this group first generated code and then manually copied or pasted the code into their work. After their code was generated, they then asked the AI assistant follow-up questions to improve understanding. These participants were not particularly fast when using AI, but demonstrated a high level of understanding on the quiz. Importantly, this approach looks nearly the same as the AI delegation group, but additionally uses AI to check their own understanding. *(p. 13)*

Page, the same material: "**High-scoring interaction patterns:** We considered high-scoring quiz patterns to be behaviors where the average quiz score was 65% or higher." … "Our qualitative analysis does not draw a causal link between interaction patterns and learning outcomes, but it does point to behaviors associated with different learning outcomes."

**Annotation.**

- **Every cluster carries its n in its own label, before its description.** "(n=4)", "(n=2)", "(n=7)" — in the paper and on the page, in the bold run-in, not in a caption. **This is the minimum discipline that lets a two-person cluster be published at all**, and it is why a reader never mistakes the typology for a result about developers. Our figures and tables inherit the same rule: the n travels with the label, not with the exhibit.
- **The definition of the high band is a threshold stated before the members are listed.** "clusters of behaviors where the average quiz score is 65% or higher" (paper) / "behaviors where the average quiz score was 65% or higher" (page). An arbitrary cut, disclosed as a cut, in the sentence that introduces the category. Compare the belief-scale thresholds in the coding-agents post ("above a 5", "at 8 or above"): same practice, and the same absence of any statement of what the cut does to the grouping.
- **"Although each behavior pattern cluster is small, the difference … is stark" is the sentence to avoid.** Concession then assertion, with no number on either side of the "but": neither the cluster sizes nor the difference appears in the sentence, and "stark" is doing the work an estimate should do. The material to fix it exists two lines away (the paper's own bands, "65%-86%" against "24%-39%", p. 18). **When a concession about sample size is followed by an assertion of magnitude, the magnitude goes in the same sentence or the concession is decorative.**
- **The disclaimer is placed *after* the six clusters on the page and is absent from the paper's §6.** "Our qualitative analysis does not draw a causal link between interaction patterns and learning outcomes, but it does point to behaviors associated with different learning outcomes." — the last line of the section, after a reader has absorbed six named personas with their scores. The coding-agents post put its equivalent downgrade *before* the figure it governs, and that is the better order. **A downgrade upstream of the exhibit tells the reader how to read it; a downgrade downstream asks them to reread it.** The paper never states the point at all inside §6, which makes the page the only place the non-causal reading is written down — and the share-card description then contradicts it.
- **The comparison that makes the typology worth anything is stated as a near-identity.** "this approach looks nearly the same as the AI delegation group, but additionally uses AI to check their own understanding" — the highest-scoring cluster and one of the lowest-scoring clusters are described as differing in one behaviour. **A taxonomy earns its place when two of its cells are shown to be one decision apart**; that sentence is the paper's strongest piece of mechanism writing, and it costs no statistics.
- **Counts, not percentages, at this sample size — and the two places the paper breaks its own practice.** "21 out of 25 participants in the treatment group asked an explanation question"; "the median participant in the treatment group encountered only one error in the entire task, while the median for the control group was three errors"; "Among the 12 participants who completed both tasks without encountering errors, only two were in the control group." Counts throughout, and medians with quartiles for the skewed error counts (Table 4). Then: "Only 16 of 25 or two thirds of the participants used AI to generate code" and "this group only accounted for around 20% of the participants in the treatment group" — two conversions to fractions that gain nothing and lose the count. **Below about thirty observations, report counts; a percentage of 25 people invites a comparison with a percentage of a million.**

**Naming, and why this piece inverts our rule.** Our standard is: the question says AI, every finding says Claude. Here the measured object is a GPT-4o-based assistant, so every finding correctly says "AI assistance", "the AI group", "the AI condition", "the treatment condition (using an AI assistant, web search, and instructions)". The page never names the model at all, and the one sentence that mentions an Anthropic product does so to exclude it: "Importantly, this setup is different from agentic coding products like Claude Code; we expect that the impacts of such programs on skill development are likely to be more pronounced than the results here." (footnote 1). Three consequences to carry:
1. **A post that measures a competitor's model cannot use our naming rule, and must say what was tested in the body, not in a methods PDF.** The page's omission of the model name is the fault a referee raises first — a reader cannot tell from the page what was under test.
2. **Naming a product only to exclude it, and conjecturing that the excluded product would be worse, is a claim without evidence in the same sentence as a disclaimer.** The footnote does it twice (here and in §7.1's "This should be a lower bound"). It is defensible because it is signed and hedged ("we expect… likely"), and it is still a claim about Claude Code made by a study that did not test Claude Code.
3. Anything our programme builds on this paper says "an AI assistant" of every finding and never "Claude" — recorded in `wiki/reports/skill-formation-rct-2026-01.md`, "What it did not test", item 1.

## Comparisons

The design is one comparison; the interest is in the four others the write-up builds around it.

### The arms — defined by what each had, not by what each was called

> In a randomized controlled trial, participants were assigned to the treatment condition (using an AI assistant, web search, and instructions) or the control condition (completing tasks with web search and instructions alone). *(p. 18)*

**Annotation.** The contrast is defined by the difference between two bundles, one parenthesis each, with the shared elements listed in both so the reader can subtract. "web search and instructions" appears on both sides; "an AI assistant" appears on one. **A two-arm comparison is legible when both bundles are enumerated and the difference is the set difference** — this is one sentence and it is the whole identification argument. It sits in the Discussion, on p. 18, which is late; in our posts it goes in the sentence that first names the design. Note what the sentence does not say and the page does not either: that the two arms were given differently-worded goals, one emphasising speed with AI and one emphasising working with a new library (the instruction screenshots, Figures 23–24; recorded in `wiki/reports/skill-formation-rct-2026-01.md`). **A bundle definition is only as honest as its enumeration.**

### The main study against its own pilot — the comparison that makes the null credible

> Figure 5 presents the treatment effects on both outcome measures: Task Time and Quiz Score. The treatment (AI) group completed the Trio tasks faster (Cohen's d=1.11, p=0.03), demonstrating improved task efficiency. However, the AI group performed significantly worse on the knowledge quiz (Cohen's d=1.7, p=0.003), indicating reduced retention of learning. For our complete power analysis and pre-registration of the study,[2] we assumed a conservative effect size of d = 0.85 (half of the observed learning effect) to account for the potential effect size inflation typical in pilot studies. *(pp. 8–9)*

**Annotation.** This is the best-written passage in either document and it is about the design, not the result. A pilot found both effects; the power analysis then **halved** the pilot's effect size before computing the sample; the reason is given in the same sentence and named as a general phenomenon ("the potential effect size inflation typical in pilot studies"); and the main study's realised effect came in below the halved assumption. The sequence pilot → discount → registered assumption → realised effect is a complete account of why the study is the size it is, in four sentences, before any main result appears. **Our pre-registration section should read like this paragraph:** the assumed effect, where it came from, why it was discounted, and what was actually observed. Note also that the pilot's speed result is *not* quietly dropped — it is published, with its d and p, which is what makes the main study's null informative rather than embarrassing.

### Early pilots against later pilots — publishing the failures

> Non-Compliance We conducted 4 pilot studies before running the full study (Table 2). … We tested different mechanisms to ensure participants in the control condition (No AI) did not use AI for the task. However, despite more explicit instructions, around 25% in the control group participants still used AI. We conducted two pilot studies with a second crowdworking platform (P2), each with 20 participants. Using screen recordings of participant progress, we verified that participants did not use AI in the control group nor for the quiz. *(p. 8)*

**Annotation.** Four designs, two platforms, and the failure rate of each printed in a table with a "Challenges" column written in prose. The narrative is: the control arm cheated, twice, at measurable rates, and the fix was a platform that records screens. **A design decision justified by a published failure rate is unanswerable; the same decision asserted ("we used screen recording to ensure compliance") is not.** What the paper then omits is the symmetric number for the main study — no compliance rate, no exclusions-for-non-compliance count is reported for the trial itself, only for the pilots. The comparison that the reader most wants (main-study compliance against the 25–35% pilot rates) is the one that is missing, and its absence is invisible because the pilot numbers are so forthcoming. **Publishing a failure rate creates an expectation that the same rate will be published for the study that matters.**

### Across question types — a comparison of gaps

> For both tasks, there is a gap between the quiz scores between the treatment and control groups. Among the different types of questions, the largest score gap occurs in the debugging questions and the smallest score gap in the code reading questions. This outcome is expected since treatment and control groups may have similar exposure to reading code through the task, but the control group with no access to AI assistance encountered more errors during the task and became more capable at debugging. *(p. 11)*

**Annotation.** The finding is an ordering of differences — which is one claim tested at five points rather than five claims — and it is exactly the shape our standard prefers. Three faults to avoid while copying it. First, no magnitudes and no interaction test: "largest" and "smallest" are the entire quantitative content, and whether the debugging gap differs from the code-reading gap is never tested. Second, the explanation is given in the indicative ("This outcome is expected since … the control group … encountered more errors … and became more capable at debugging") — an unhedged causal chain whose two links are measured in a different section and whose conclusion ("became more capable at debugging") is the finding itself, so the sentence explains the result with the result. Third, it is exploratory, and the "(not pre-registered)" label is two sentences earlier rather than in this sentence. **The ordering is the finding; the mechanism sentence needs a modal and the exploratory label needs to travel with the claim.**

### Against the outside literature, and against Anthropic's own prior number

Paper, Background: "Peng et al. found that crowd-sourced software developers using copilot completed a task 55.5% faster than the control group and novice programmers benefited more from AI coding assistance." … "AI-generated code completions provide a 26. 8% boost in productivity as measured by pull requests, commits, and software product builds [Cui et al., 2024]."

Page, Conclusion: "Prior studies have found mixed results on whether AI [helps](…) or [hinders](…) coding productivity. Our own [research](…) found that AI can reduce the time it takes to complete some work tasks by 80%—a result that may seem in tension with the findings presented here. But the two studies ask different questions and use different methods: our earlier observational work measured productivity on tasks where participants already had the relevant skills, while this study examines what happens when people are learning something new. It is possible that AI both accelerates productivity on well-developed skills and hinders the acquisition of new ones, though more research is needed to understand this relationship."

**Annotation.** The paper puts the outside productivity numbers in the Background and never returns to them; its own speed null is never set beside Peng's 55.5% or Cui's 26.8%, and a reader has to do that comparison unaided. **The page does what the paper does not**, and it is the single most instructive paragraph in this pair for our programme, because it is the paragraph an Anthropic post has to write whenever a new result sits awkwardly beside an old one. Its four moves, in order: (1) name the tension in the reader's words and do not soften it — "a result that may seem in tension with the findings presented here"; (2) locate the difference in the *question and the design*, not in the credibility of either study — "ask different questions and use different methods", then one clause per study saying which; (3) offer the reconciling proposition explicitly and modally — "It is possible that AI both accelerates productivity on well-developed skills and hinders the acquisition of new ones"; (4) decline to settle it — "though more research is needed to understand this relationship". **Name the tension, locate it in the design, state the reconciliation as a conjecture, refuse to close it.** Our posts, which are built on Anthropic's published numbers, will need this construction constantly; it is four sentences and it should be copied nearly verbatim in shape.

### Within the treatment arm — the comparison the design cannot license

> Among participants who use AI, we find a stark divide in skill formation outcomes between high-scoring interaction patterns (65%-86% quiz score) vs low-scoring interaction patterns (24%-39% quiz score). The high scorers only asked AI conceptual questions instead of code generation or asked for explanations to accompany generated code; these usage patterns demonstrate a high level of cognitive engagement. *(pp. 18–19)*

**Annotation.** Randomisation gives the arm contrast; it gives nothing about behaviour *within* the arm, because how a participant chose to use the assistant is not assigned. The paper reports the divide as two bands rather than as an estimate, which is the right register for a post-hoc grouping — bands of observed averages, no test, no interval, no claim of an effect. The word carrying the whole inference is "divide", not "effect", and the two bands are printed so the reader can see that they do not overlap. Even so, this is the comparison that the share-card description converts into a causal exception ("except when used for explanations rather than delegation"), which the page's own disclaimer forbids. **A within-arm comparison in a randomised trial is descriptive no matter how clean it looks, and the sentence that survives compression into a headline has to carry that word.**

## Figure captions

**The paper: 27 numbered figures, 7 numbered tables, captions below figures and above tables, "Figure N:" / "Table N:" prefix, no bold title clause.** The caption grammar has two forms and the paper mixes them:

*(a) Description then finding.* The commonest and the better one — what is plotted, then a final sentence stating what it shows.

> Figure 7: Task completion time and quiz score by years of coding experience. Error bars represent 95% CI. The control group (No AI) average quiz score is higher across all levels of coding experience.

> Figure 8: Score breakdown by questions type relating to each task and skill area. Debugging questions revealed the largest differences in average quiz score between the treatment and control groups.

> Figure 16: Active coding time vs. quiz score: active coding time represents the amount of time actually spent coding and is often a very small fraction of total task time. The No AI condition participants spent more active time coding and achieved higher quiz scores.

*(b) Description with the method only.* Used for the two exhibits that carry the headline estimates.

> Figure 6: Difference in means of overall task time and quiz score between the control (No AI) and treatment (AI Assistant) groups in main study (n=52). Error bars represent 95% CI. Significance values correspond to treatment effect. * p<0.05, **<0.01, ***<0.001

> Figure 5: Difference in means of overall task time and quiz score between the control (No AI) and treatment (AI Assistant) groups in Pilot Study D. Error bars represent 95% CI. Significance values correspond to treatment effect. * p<0.05, **<0.01, ***<0.001

And the whole-paper overview caption, which is the abstract in miniature with panel cues:

> Figure 1: Overview of results: (Left) We find a significant decrease in library-specific skills (conceptual understanding, code reading, and debugging) among workers using AI assistance for completing tasks with a new python library. (Right) We categorize AI usage patterns and found three high skill development patterns where participants stay cognitively engaged when using AI assistance.

Table captions are one line and carry the convention that matters:

> Table 4: Number of errors encountered per participant by condition. Values are median (Q1–Q3).

> Table 3: Examples of different types of queries received by AI assistant and counts of each type of query. 11 queries have multiple (two) labels.

**Annotation.**

- **Description-first, finding-last is this paper's pattern and it is the opposite of the coding-agents post's bold-title-first.** Both work; the difference is what a reader who sees only the caption takes away. Title-first gives them the claim and makes them hunt for the method; description-first gives them the method and makes them read to the end for the claim. For an experiment with two arms and small n, description-first is the safer order, and **the sentence that states the finding should be last and should name the arms** ("The control group (No AI) average quiz score is higher across all levels of coding experience").
- **The estimate figures get the neutral caption.** Figures 5 and 6 — the two exhibits carrying every headline number — are the only ones whose captions state no finding. The same deliberate restraint as the coding-agents post's Figure 5, and for the same reason: **the exhibit that must not be over-read gets the descriptive caption.**
- **What the captions do carry, and in what order.** Arms named with their in-chart labels ("the control (No AI) and treatment (AI Assistant) groups"), so the caption and the axis agree; the n, in parentheses, on the two main-study exhibits; the uncertainty and its coverage ("Error bars represent 95% CI"); what the stars attach to ("Significance values correspond to treatment effect"); and the star thresholds. Five jobs, four short sentences. **The clause "Significance values correspond to treatment effect" is the one most captions omit and most readers need** — it says the stars are not group-vs-zero tests.
- **What no caption carries.** No caption states the units of its axes (they are in the in-image axis labels only), no caption states the estimand, and outside Figures 5 and 6 **no caption states an n** — including every figure in the qualitative section, where the relevant denominators are 25 or 24 rather than 52. The same fault as the coding-agents post, at a sample size where it matters far more.
- **Table captions carry the conventions that prevent a misreading, and they do it in one clause.** "Values are median (Q1–Q3)" tells the reader the error counts are skewed and not to average them; "11 queries have multiple (two) labels" tells the reader the type counts do not sum to the query total. **Where counts can double-count or a statistic is not a mean, the caption says so** — this is the practice our tables in the evidence drawers should follow, verbatim in form.
- **A caption that contradicts its own figure, and a draft artefact left in a chart title.** Figure 20's caption reads "We designed the evaluation to test three different software skills: conceputal understanding, code reading, and code writing" — but the paper's §4.2 states that code-writing questions were excluded, and the panel is headed "Debugging"; the misspelling is the source's. And the text inside Figure 17 renders as "Coding Context Distribution (Multi-Select) haha" — a working note left in a chart title, shipped through two arXiv versions. **Captions and in-image text are part of the text and get the same proofreading; our rule that nothing the claims list forbids appears anywhere "including captions" is exactly this failure mode.**
- **The page has no captions at all.** Both body figures are bare `<img>` elements inside `<figure>` tags with **no `<figcaption>`, no numbering, and empty `alt` attributes**; the page's prose never refers to a figure by number, so an image lifted from the page carries no statement of what it plots. Per `room/director-2026-09-16-caption-amendment.md` the caption is the emphasised sentence under an image — here there is none, in either figure, and that absence is recorded rather than worked around. The only alt strings on the page are the hero image's (the article title repeated) and the share card's ("Sheet music mostly covered by red dots, representing understanding obscured beneath the surface"); neither contains a number, so **nothing in this file is marked `(alt text)`**. Per `room/director-2026-09-16-figure-values-ruling.md`, **no value has been read off any chart image and recorded in this file** — that includes the group means and intervals plotted in Figures 6 and 8 and the persona labels printed inside Figure 11, which `wiki/reports/skill-formation-rct-2026-01.md` records with their provenance markings and this file does not.
- **The consequence for a screen-reader user, and for us.** On the page, a reader who cannot see the images gets nothing: no caption, no alt, no in-text figure reference. Every number that exists only in a chart is unavailable to them, and the two charts are the only place the study's distributions appear. Our page builder puts the finding in the caption and the table in the drawer, so the same reader gets both.

## Limitations

**There is no section headed "Limitations" in either document.** In the paper they are the six bullets of §7.1, under a heading that says *Future Work* and a preamble that says *limitations*. On the page they are three sentences in the closing run, plus a footnote, plus the qualitative disclaimer. The allocation runs across four layers, as it does in the coding-agents post, but here the top layer has been renamed after its remedy.

**Layer 1 — §7.1, the preamble and the six bullets, verbatim:**

> Our work is a first step to understanding the impact of AI assistance on humans in the human-AI collaboration process. We hope that this work will motivate future work that addresses the following limitations:

> **Task Selection**: This study focuses on a single task using a chat-based interface. This should be a lower bound for cognitive offloading since agentic AI coding tools would require even less human participation. In our work, users who relied on AI without thinking performed the worst on the evaluation; a completely agentic tool would create a similar effect. Future work should investigate the impacts of agentic coding tools on learning outcomes and skill development.

> **Task Length**: Ideally, skill formation takes place over months to years. We measured skill formation for a specific Python library over a one-hour period. Future work should study real-world skill development through longitudinal measurement of the impacts of AI adoption.

> **Participant Realism**: While participants in our study were professional or freelance programmers, there was not the same incentive to learn the library as if it were required for their actual job. Future studies should aim at studying the skill acquisition fro novice workers within a real company.

> **Prompting Skills**: We collect self-reported familiarity with AI coding tools, but we do not actually measure differences in prompting techniques. An extension to our would also involve testing the level of prompting fluency beyond self-report.

> **Evaluation Design**: Our study measures skill formation through a comprehensive quiz. Other studies could use the completion of another task or design coding as alternative evaluation strategies.

> **Human Assistance**: We do not include the counterfactual of how skill formation would be impacted by receiving assistance from humans. Since human assistance and feedback takes place in a diverse settings (e.g., classroom, pair programming, code review), future work can compare the effect of feedback from AI vs humans in all these settings on skill formation.

**Layer 2 — finding-level, at the finding, verbatim:** "In exploratory data analysis (not pre-registered), the quiz score was decomposed into subareas and question types" (p. 11); "due to the small group size of the 1-3 year participant group (n=4), the difference in task time was not significant" (p. 11); "Our results do not conclusively find a speed up or slow down using AI in this task" (p. 11); "Although each behavior pattern cluster is small…" (p. 12); "Although this effect might be due to the short duration of our task…" (p. 14).

**Layer 3 — number-level, in a footnote at the number:** "The screen recording for one participant in the AI condition was not available." (p. 12, footnote 3, attached to the sentence that gives the qualitative sample).

**Layer 4 — the page, verbatim:**

> Our qualitative analysis does not draw a causal link between interaction patterns and learning outcomes, but it does point to behaviors associated with different learning outcomes.

> Though preliminary, these results suggest important considerations as companies transition to a greater ratio of AI-written to human-written code.

> This study is only a first step towards uncovering how human-AI collaboration affects the experience of workers. Our sample was relatively small, and our assessment measured comprehension shortly after the coding task. Whether immediate quiz performance predicts longer-term skill development is an important question this study does not resolve.

> Importantly, this setup is different from agentic coding products like Claude Code; we expect that the impacts of such programs on skill development are likely to be more pronounced than the results here. *(footnote 1)*

**Annotation.**

- **Each bullet is limitation → why it bites → the study that would fix it, in two or three sentences, with the remedy last.** The bold run-in names the dimension in one or two words (*Task Selection*, *Task Length*, *Participant Realism*, *Prompting Skills*, *Evaluation Design*, *Human Assistance*), so the list is scannable as a research agenda. This is the same join the coding-agents post makes inside its caveats paragraph — conceding a weakness and promising the fix as one move — done six times at bullet length. **Copy the shape: the dimension as a two-word label, the concession, then the study.** What it costs is navigability: the heading says *Future Work*, so a reader looking for the limitations cannot find them by scanning.
- **The best limitation in the corpus is the first one, because it is signed.** "This should be a lower bound for cognitive offloading since agentic AI coding tools would require even less human participation." The restriction (one chat interface, one task) is converted into a *direction*: whatever the real-world effect is, it is at least this large. A signed limitation strengthens the finding it limits. It is also a conjecture with no evidence in the study, and the sentence says "should" rather than "is", which is the fence that makes it admissible. **Every limitation we write should be asked the question this one answers: which way does it push the estimate?**
- **Two limitations name the sentence the study is not entitled to write.** "We do not include the counterfactual of how skill formation would be impacted by receiving assistance from humans" and "there was not the same incentive to learn the library as if it were required for their actual job". The corpus is unanimous that this is where a limitation earns its keep; the second is the sharper of the two because it names an economic feature of the setting (the incentive) rather than a missing arm.
- **The paper's list is about generalisation and the page's is about measurement, and neither is about inference.** Six bullets on scope (interface, duration, realism, prompting, instrument, counterfactual); three page sentences on sample size and timing. **Nothing in either document states a minimum detectable effect, discusses the 35-minute censoring of the time outcome, mentions multiplicity across the five subarea contrasts, or gives a compliance rate for the main study** — the four things a referee raises first, all recorded in full in `wiki/reports/skill-formation-rct-2026-01.md` under "What it did not test". The limitation sections are honest about what the study cannot generalise to and silent about what its own numbers can and cannot support. **A limitations section that is entirely about external validity is a limitations section that has not been refereed.**
- **The page's version is three sentences and the third is the best one.** "Whether immediate quiz performance predicts longer-term skill development is an important question this study does not resolve." The limitation is stated as an unanswered question about the *validity of the outcome measure itself*, which is the deepest one available — if a quiz score minutes later does not predict skill months later, the headline is about quizzes. Putting it in the close, unhedged, immediately after "Our sample was relatively small", is the right placement and the right order: sample, then timing, then the construct.
- **"Our sample was relatively small" with no n, in a document that gave the n once, 1,500 words earlier.** Relative to what is never said. Compare the paper, which puts "(n=52)" in two figure captions, a table caption and two figure titles. **A size concession with no number is a phrase, not a limitation.**

## Close

**The paper's close — the final paragraph of §7.1, sitting after the six limitation bullets:**

> For novice workers in software engineering or any other industry, our study can be viewed as a small piece of evidence toward the value of intentional skill development despite the ubiquity of AI tools. Our study demonstrates the benefits of deploying cognitive effort when encountering a learning opportunity to master a new tool even if barriers (e.g., errors) may be encountered in the process of mastery. Exerting cognitive effort can be assisted by AI; beyond the patterns we describe, major LLM services also provide learning modes (e.g., ChatGPT Study Mode, Claude Code Learning / Explanatory mode). Ultimately, to accommodate skill development in the presence of AI, there needs to be a more expansive view of the impacts of AI on workers. Participants in the new AI economy must care not only about productivity gains from AI but also the long-term sustainability of expertise development amid the proliferation of new AI tools.

**And the Discussion's own last sentences, which are the paper's real conclusion (p. 19):**

> Together, our results suggest that the aggressive incorporation of AI into the workplace can have negative impacts on the professional development workers if they do not remain cognitatively engaged. Given time constraints and organizational pressures, junior developers or other professionals may rely on AI to complete tasks as fast as possible at the cost of real skill development. Furthermore, we found that the biggest difference in test scores is between the debugging questions. This suggests that as companies transition to more AI code writing with human supervision, humans may not possess the necessary skills to validate and debug AI-written code if their skill formation was inhibited by using AI in the first place.

**The page's close — first and last paragraphs of the Conclusion, and the recommendation between them:**

> Our results suggest that incorporating AI aggressively into the workplace, particularly with respect to software engineering, comes with trade-offs. The findings highlight that not all AI-reliance is the same: the way we interact with AI while trying to be efficient affects how much we learn. Given time constraints and organizational pressures, junior developers or other professionals may rely on AI to complete tasks as fast as possible at the cost of skill development—and notably the ability to debug issues when something goes wrong.

> Though preliminary, these results suggest important considerations as companies transition to a greater ratio of AI-written to human-written code. Productivity benefits may come at the cost of skills necessary to validate AI-written code if junior engineers' skill development has been stunted by using AI in the first place. Managers should think intentionally about how to deploy AI tools at scale, and consider systems or intentional design choices that ensure engineers continue to learn as they work—and are thus able to exercise meaningful oversight over the systems they build.

> For novice workers in software engineering or any other industry, our study can be viewed as a small piece of evidence toward the value of intentional skill development with AI tools. Cognitive effort—and even getting painfully stuck—is likely important for fostering mastery. This is also a lesson that applies to how individuals choose to work with AI, and which tools they use. Major LLM services also provide learning modes (e.g., [Claude Code Learning and Explanatory](…) mode or [ChatGPT Study Mode](…)) designed to foster understanding. Knowing how people learn when using AI can also help guide how we design it; AI assistance should enable humans to work more efficiently *and* develop new skills at the same time.

> Ultimately, to accommodate skill development in the presence of AI, we need a more expansive view of the impacts of AI on workers. In an AI-augmented workplace, productivity gains matter, but so does the long-term development of the expertise those gains depend on.

**Annotation.**

- **The same close, edited, is the best thing this pair gives a style corpus.** The paper's final sentence — "Participants in the new AI economy must care not only about productivity gains from AI but also the long-term sustainability of expertise development amid the proliferation of new AI tools" — becomes, on the page, "In an AI-augmented workplace, productivity gains matter, but so does the long-term development of the expertise those gains depend on." The edit deletes a coinage ("Participants in the new AI economy"), a deontic ("must care"), an abstraction ("sustainability of expertise development") and a filler clause ("amid the proliferation of new AI tools"); it replaces them with a relative clause that does real work — "**the expertise those gains depend on**" makes the dependence the point, which the paper's version only implies. **Shorter by leaving things out, not by compressing, in one worked example.** Read the two sentences side by side before writing any close.
- **Both closes restate in words and leave the numbers in the findings.** No estimate, no p-value, no group mean appears in either close; the page's close carries exactly one quantity ("80%") and it belongs to the earlier study being reconciled. The corpus is unanimous across four very different pieces on this point.
- **The escalation to watch, in one paragraph.** "our study can be viewed as a small piece of evidence" → "our study demonstrates the benefits of" → "must care". Self-deprecation, then a demonstration claim, then a prescription, in three consecutive sentences of the paper's final paragraph. The page keeps the self-sizing sentence, drops "demonstrates the benefits" entirely, and moves the prescription into a separate paragraph addressed to managers, where it has someone to be addressed to. **A close that sizes itself honestly in its first sentence has spent its credit and cannot then say "demonstrates".**
- **The recommendations are on the page and not in the paper, and they are addressed to three different actors.** Managers ("Managers should think intentionally about how to deploy AI tools at scale, and consider systems or intentional design choices…"); individuals ("This is also a lesson that applies to how individuals choose to work with AI, and which tools they use"); and product designers, including the house's own — "Knowing how people learn when using AI can also help guide how we design it; AI assistance should enable humans to work more efficiently *and* develop new skills at the same time." **That last clause is the model for our template's "recommendations to Anthropic": it states what the product should achieve, in the study's own terms, in one sentence, without naming a feature.** The paper addresses only workers, once, in the Discussion's first paragraph ("workers acquiring new skills should be mindful of their reliance on AI during the learning process"), which is advice with no addressee who can act.
- **What comes next is a list of questions, not a promise.** The page, in its penultimate paragraph: "There remain many unanswered questions we hope future studies will investigate, for example the effects of AI on tasks beyond coding, whether this effect dissipates longitudinally as engineers develop greater fluency, and whether AI assistance differs from human assistance while learning." Three named follow-ups, all of them delegated to "future studies" rather than committed to. Compare the coding-agents post, which promises its own next wave and names what it will contain. **A close that delegates its follow-ups is honest; a close that commits to them is checkable. Ours should commit.**
- **Title against ending — the one thing this pair does not do.** The paper is titled "How AI Impacts Skill Formation" and ends on "a more expansive view of the impacts of AI on workers"; the page is titled "How AI assistance impacts the formation of coding skills" and ends on the same sentence in cleaner prose. Neither ending conjugates its title into a finding, and the sentence that would — "AI use impairs conceptual understanding, code reading, and debugging abilities, without delivering significant efficiency gains on average" — appears only in the abstract, which the page does not have. **A title that names the question needs a close that answers it in the same words; here the answer exists, in one clause, in the one place the general reader will not go.** Our rule stands: the title matches the ending, and where the title is a question the last paragraph answers it in the vocabulary of the title.
- **The "we" of the close, and the substitute.** "the way we interact with AI while trying to be efficient affects how much we learn"; "Knowing how people learn when using AI can also help guide how we design it". The first is the collective "we" of everyone who uses these tools; the second is the institutional "we" of the firm that builds them, and they are one sentence apart. Neither is available to us. The substitutes — "how developers interact with AI while trying to be efficient affects how much they learn", and a recommendation addressed to Anthropic by name — are both weaker in the first case and stronger in the second, because a recommendation that names its addressee can be acted on.
- **The proofreading gap between the two documents is itself the lesson.** The paper carries, uncorrected through two arXiv versions whose comment says "Fixed typos": "in particicular", "cognitatively engaged", "fro novice workers", "An extension to our would", "how participants from new skills", "the participants' experience level of the but presented", "a online interview platform", "how to used the new library", "conceputal understanding" in a caption, and "haha" inside a chart title. The page, which credits three people with editorial support, has none. **We publish one text, and it has to pass both readings: the referee's and the copy-editor's.**
