# labor-market-impacts-2026-03-appendix — style annotation

## Source

- **Title on the cover:** "Appendix to "Labor market impacts of AI"" — set in two lines of large serif under the ANTHROP\C wordmark, the parent paper's title inside typographic quotation marks, followed by a full-width horizontal rule. The same construction as the two Economic Index appendices (`Appendix to "…"`) and not the construction of `coding-agents-social-sciences-2026-05-appendix`, which carries the parent's title bare behind the word "Appendix:". No sub-title, no appendix letter, no author list, no byline, no abstract, no contents page, no acknowledgements.
- **Date on the cover:** "March 2026" — month and year only, below the rule. No day anywhere in the document, although the parent paper is dated to the day.
- **URL fetched:** https://cdn.sanity.io/files/4zrzovbb/website/e5f77fc0e77c0185110b5e4b909602791ae76eae.pdf — 11 pages, 993,038 bytes, PDF 1.4, producer "Adobe PDF Library 18.0" from "Adobe InDesign 21.2 (Macintosh)", `/CreationDate` 2026-03-04 21:47 −08:00 (2026-03-05 05:47 UTC), tagged, `/Lang` `en-US`, no document `/Title`. One build only; `wiki/INDEX.md` records no second copy for this slug. (The *parent* paper has two CDN builds; that is its own entry's problem and not visible here.)
- **Other URLs it points at:** exactly one, in the last endnote — https://oui.doleta.gov/unemploy/DataDownloads.asp, the Department of Labor data product behind one figure. It is set blue and underlined, as is the string "Claude.ai" in note 3, but the file carries **no link annotations at all**: both are styled as links and neither is clickable. There is no URL for the parent paper, no dataset link, no code link and no bibliography — the five works the appendix leans on are cited author-year in the body and never listed.
- **Document type:** a combined methodology and robustness appendix to a paper. Unlike the Learning-curves appendix, which hands over machinery and makes no argument, this one argues: it defines a new measure, shows two results the paper did not have room for, attacks the quality of its own input data, and lays ten alternative versions of the measure side by side. It is the only appendix in this corpus with an argumentative spine.
- **Length:** about 2,320 words of extractable text across eleven pages — cover 8; "Defining exposure" ~765; "Additional results on employment impacts" ~495; "Task granularity" ~385; "Comparing measures of occupational exposure exposure" ~395, of which ~350 are the nine measure bullets; endnotes 275; figure captions 60. Four figures, no tables, four displayed equations, six endnotes, four headings.
- **Audience:** a referee who has the paper open. "In the main text, we designate…", "the same difference-in-differences regression and control group as before", "described above", "echoing the main results that use only CPS data" — four different phrases assume a document the reader is holding, and none of them names it. Nothing is explained twice and nothing is recapitulated.
- **Typography, because it shapes the register:** body in Anthropic Serif, section headings in Anthropic Sans at body-plus size, captions in small bold sans, running footer in small grey sans. Inline notation falls back to Times-Roman and TiemposText on pages 3, 4 and 9 (the Greek letters and the tilde-r), so the symbols in the prose do not match the symbols in the displayed equations, which are pasted-in images.

## Section order

Headings in order, with what each does and how long it runs. There are four, all at one level, none numbered.

1. **Cover (p. 1)** — wordmark, quoted parent title, rule, "March 2026", and the footer "anthropic.com". ~8 words. No sentence anywhere says "this appendix accompanies…"; the quotation marks in the title are the whole of the relationship.
2. **"Defining exposure" (pp. 2–4)** — the measure, built bottom-up in one unbroken run of prose and four equations, in this internal order: the unit of analysis; the weighted usage count (equation); the two counts defined, one per sentence, each with the reason it is filtered the way it is; the usage gate with its justification and its consequence; the shared-task allocation rule; what the measure is and is not meant to be; task-level exposure (equation); the β term borrowed from Eloundou et al.; the automation factor α (equation); α at its two bounds; a one-sentence portrait of a high-exposure job; a worked example of one task in one occupation; the job-level measure (equation); how to read a difference in it. No sub-headings, no notation table, no summary of the section.
3. **"Additional results on employment impacts" (pp. 4–6)** — prediction, observation, the pooled null in words; then the threshold sweep; then the CPS's weakness as the reason for an administrative-data check, that data's own cost, a validity check on the coarser grouping, and the check's result. Three figures, all on the page after the prose that introduces them.
4. **"Task granularity" (pp. 7–8)** — not a results section but a construct-validity section: the pitfall in O\*NET, an example pair of near-identical tasks, three candidate remedies of which two are rejected by example, the one that was implemented and its rank correlation with the main-text grouping, and a call for work on the framework.
5. **"Comparing measures of occupational exposure exposure" (pp. 8–9)** — the duplicated word is in the printed heading. A 42-word lead-in that defines the statistic, then nine bullets defining ten measures, then the heatmap on p. 10.
6. **Endnotes 1–6 (pp. 10–11)** — under a short centred rule on p. 10, immediately below the Figure 4 caption, numbered superscripts, running to a third of p. 11.
7. **Running footer** — on every page after the cover: "Appendix to "Labor market impacts of AI"" left, page number right. The cover carries "anthropic.com" in that position instead. The footer is the only thing on any inner page that says what the document is.

**Exhibit numbering.** "Appendix Figure 1" … "Appendix Figure 4" — the word *Appendix* does the disambiguating work that the Learning-curves appendix does with "A.1" and the social-sciences appendix with "A1". Three appendices in this corpus, three conventions. The advantage of this one is that a figure lifted into a slide still says where it came from; the cost is a three-word label before every title.

**Where exhibits sit relative to prose.** Every figure sits at the top of the page *after* the paragraph that discusses it, breaking out of the text column to the full page width, with its caption below it and flush to the page margin rather than the text margin. Prose therefore always precedes its exhibit, sometimes by a page turn, and the reader meets the claim before the picture. Figure 4 gets a page of its own, shared with the start of the endnotes; the foot of p. 9 is left white rather than the heatmap being pulled up. No figure is discussed after it appears, and no figure is referred to by number in the section that introduces it except once ("In Appendix Figure 3, we show impacts on the insured unemployment rate").

**What the order tells you.** Definition → results → an attack on the inputs → a lattice of alternatives. The document becomes less committed as it goes: it opens by building one measure and closes by showing that nine others rank occupations much the same way. A paper's appendix ordered this way answers the referee's questions in the order they arise — *what is the measure, does the result hold, is the input data good enough, would another measure have done* — and the concession that costs most (task statements are arbitrarily specific) is placed third, where it is a prelude to the robustness section rather than a coda to the results.

**What is shown and what is not.** Shown in full: every term of the measure and every gate; the numeric threshold, in two units, with an external calibration in a footnote; the allocation rule for shared tasks; a worked example with each gate's value for one task; the reading of a 0.10 difference; the two employment robustness exercises; the three candidate task groupings with the examples that killed two of them; all ten measure variants in prose; the named government data product behind one figure. Not shown anywhere: the difference-in-differences equation, its fixed effects, its clustering, its weighting or its sample beyond ages 22–25; any sample size; the number of occupations behind any correlation; which Economic Index releases or date windows the counts come from; the classifier; the embedding or similarity model; the Ridge specification; any code or data file; and a minimum detectable effect for a document whose headline is a null. The rule it follows is *everything about how the measure was built, nothing about how the regression was run.*

## Opening move

The cover, verbatim:

> Appendix to "Labor market impacts of AI"
>
> March 2026

Then p. 2, verbatim — the first heading, the first paragraph and the lead-in to the first equation:

> ## Defining exposure
>
> We begin at the task level. We only consider a task as covered if it sees sufficient traffic in our Economic Index samples (Handa et al. 2025), with more weight given to API usage (indicating deeper integration in production systems):

**Annotation.**

- **There is no opening move in the sense the criteria ask for**, and the file says so rather than inventing one: no why-it-matters, no framing paragraph, no restatement of the paper's question, no roadmap. The first heading is the first thing on the page and the first sentence is five words long.
- **"We begin at the task level."** The opening commitment is the *grain*, not the finding, not the constraint and not the sample. Compare the Learning-curves appendix, which opens on privacy ("Our results are based on privacy-preserving analysis"), and the social-sciences appendix, which opens on field dates. Three appendices, three opening facts — and the choice tells you what each document thinks it is for. This one is a measure, so it opens on the unit the measure is built from.
- **The inclusion rule and its reason arrive in the same sentence.** "with more weight given to API usage (indicating deeper integration in production systems)" — the weighting is announced and justified before the algebra that implements it, and the justification is a substantive claim about what API traffic means, parenthesised so it cannot be mistaken for a result. A design choice stated with its reason can be argued with; one stated alone cannot.
- **The source is cited and not described.** "our Economic Index samples (Handa et al. 2025)" is the only provenance the document ever gives: no release name, no folder, no window, no counts. The author-year citation stands in for a data section. For our own posts this is the line that has to be replaced by a release and a date range.
- **Colon, then equation, four times.** Every displayed equation is introduced by a sentence ending in a colon that names what is about to appear — "…given to API usage (indicating deeper integration in production systems):", "Exposure of a task t is then given by r̃t:", "It is defined as follows:", "Then our job-level measure is calculated as:". No equation is numbered and none is ever referred to back; each is identified by the name of its left-hand side. A reader can discuss the whole measure without saying "equation 2".
- **The sentence after an equation begins with the symbol it defines.** "ClaudeWorkUsaget is the count of tasks t in Claude.ai that were classified as work-related"; "where βt is the task's exposure from Eloundou et al. (2023)"; "where AutoSharet is the share of Claude.ai usage that is automative"; "where To is the set of tasks for job o". Three of the four open with "where". Notation is never left to a glossary and never introduced twice.
- **How soon the first number appears.** Not until the second paragraph after the first equation, and when it comes it is a threshold rather than a quantity: "must be 100 or 0.0025% of traffic". Notation first, parameters second, estimates last — the reverse of a report.
- **Register.** First person plural in almost every sentence of the document (some thirty "we"/"We", thirteen "our"), for methodological acts and for editorial choices alike: "we impose", "we group", "we explored", "We describe above", "We leave the success rate out". House style forbids it; the substitute is the passive with the choice and its reason kept — "a task is treated as covered only where it sees sufficient traffic …, with API usage weighted more heavily because …" — which costs nothing here because every "we" sentence in this document already carries its reason.

## Findings and their caveats

The appendix reports results, but its prose carries almost no estimates: the two pooled employment estimates and the whole correlation matrix live inside figures, and per `room/director-2026-09-16-figure-values-ruling.md` no chart-read number is recorded in `wiki/style/`. What follows is the phrasing — first of the measure, then of the results — which is what this file exists to carry. The numbers themselves are catalogued in the sibling entry `wiki/reports/labor-market-impacts-2026-03-appendix.md`.

### How a hard threshold is written

> The strict gate we impose is that WorkUsaget must be 100 or 0.0025% of traffic.³ The task counts we observe have a long tail of low-count uses that likely reflect uncommon behavior, testing, or classifier errors. The exact cutoff has little impact on the job rankings. Tasks that do not meet the gate of WorkUsaget ≥ 100 are assigned an exposure of 0.

And the footnote that calibrates it:

> Usage of 100 represents 0.0025% of traffic using the previous two Anthropic Economic Index reports (2M from Claude.ai and 2M from 1P API), which is similar to the median share of time spent on a particular O\*NET task (calculated by combining our time fraction estimates and employment estimates from BLS), 0.0014%. See Tomlinson et al. (2025) for a related gating approach.

**Annotation.** Four sentences doing four jobs, in the only order that works: the rule; the mechanism of the noise it removes; a robustness claim; the consequence for the data. Three features are worth taking.

- **The threshold is given in two units at once** — a count and a share of traffic — so the reader can see it both as an observation requirement and as a rarity cut-off, without doing arithmetic that would need a denominator the document never gives in the body.
- **The footnote defends the cut-off by converting it into the units of the phenomenon.** A threshold on Claude traffic is compared to the median share of time a worker spends on an O\*NET task, computed from the paper's own time fractions and BLS employment. That is the most copyable move in the document: *a cut-off is justified by showing what it equals in the thing being measured*, not by asserting that it is conventional. The comparison work is done in a footnote, in one sentence, and it ends by pointing at a paper that gates the same way.
- **The consequence is stated as a value, not as a deletion.** "Tasks that do not meet the gate … are assigned an exposure of 0" — the failing tasks stay in the denominator of the job-level average, which is a substantive modelling decision hidden in a sentence that reads like bookkeeping. Compare the Learning-curves appendix's "otherwise such cells are dropped prior to estimation": same sentence position, opposite treatment, and in both cases the reader learns the estimator's sample rule from the sentence that defines the rule.
- **The one sentence not to copy:** "The exact cutoff has little impact on the job rankings." A robustness claim with no number, no figure and no variant table, in a document that spends its last three pages tabulating variants by rank correlation — the machinery to support it was already built. In this house the sentence reads "job rankings under gates of X, Y and Z correlate at ρ = …", or it does not appear.

### How a negative definition is written

> This measure is meant to identify initial steps toward implementation and automation. It does not measure the intensive margin of AI use (increased adoption in debugging vs. email drafting, for example), because the connection with usage and work performed is fuzzy and highly dependent on the task. At the level of a job, however, coverage will increase as more of its tasks are observed and automated.

**Annotation.** Intent, then exclusion, then the mechanism of the exclusion, then the level at which the measure survives it. The limitation is not in a list and not deferred: it is the second sentence of the construct's definition, it is concrete enough to be checked ("debugging vs. email drafting"), and it carries its own "because". The third sentence is the rescue, and note how narrow it is — the claim is restored *at the level of a job*, not repaired at the level of a task. **State the aggregation level at which a noisy measure becomes usable, in the same paragraph in which you concede the noise.**

### How the units of a constructed index are written

> This measure can be loosely interpreted as the share of a job being performed or accelerated by an LLM, though the automation weighting means it is not a pure percentage of task coverage. Comparing two jobs that differ by 0.10 on the measure, the higher-coverage job could have a 10 percentage point higher share of their day covered by AI, or a 20 percentage point higher automation share in our data.

**Annotation.** The model sentence pair in the document, and the one to copy whenever a post builds an index. Move one: give the natural reading, fenced by an adverb ("loosely interpreted"). Move two: deny the reading the number's scale invites, with the reason ("the automation weighting means it is not a pure percentage of task coverage"). Move three: instead of leaving the units unidentified, give the *two extreme readings of a difference* — the same 0.10 gap is a 10-point coverage difference at one bound of α and a 20-point automation difference at the other. A reader now knows the index's units are an interval, not a point, and can bound any comparison made with it. No limitations section could do this work, because the ambiguity belongs to the number and travels with it.

### How a worked example replaces a table

> In this framework, the jobs that have high exposure will be those whose tasks have at least some usage, are theoretically doable with an LLM, and are mostly used in automative patterns.

> With over 2,000 observations, it passes the count gate. Eloundou et al. (2023) give it a β value of 0.5, and the automation factor is 0.96 since over 90 percent of the usage is in the 1P API data. This contributes to high coverage for Health Information Technologists.

**Annotation.** The portrait sentence first — three clauses, one per component of the measure, in the order the equation multiplies them — then one task in one occupation walked through the same three components with its actual values, each value followed by the reason it takes that value ("since over 90 percent of the usage is in the 1P API data"). Three gates, three numbers, one example, no table. Note the closing verb: "This **contributes to** high coverage for Health Information Technologists" — not explains, not drives, not accounts for. A single example is allowed to illustrate a mechanism and not to decompose it, and the verb is what keeps the distinction.

### How a prediction is written before the result

> If unemployment risk were increasing for young workers in exposed jobs, we would expect the gap between the two series to shrink and possibly reverse following the release of ChatGPT.

> Instead, the gap has remained roughly constant.

**Annotation.** The best two sentences in the appendix. The hypothesis is stated as a prediction about the *geometry of the exhibit the reader is about to see* — two series, a gap, shrinking, possibly reversing — so the test is one a reader performs by looking, before reading the coefficient. Then "Instead," and the observation, in seven words. This is what the criteria mean by hypotheses stated so that they can fail: the falsifying pattern is described in advance and in the units of the picture. Copy the pair verbatim in shape; it is the cheapest way to make a null informative.

### How a null is phrased

> The difference-in-differences regression in the lower panel reflects this, with estimates close to zero throughout the post-treatment period. The pooled estimate suggests that overall change in unemployment for the exposed younger workers compared to the less exposed workers is negative and indistinguishable from zero.

> The pooled estimate in the post-ChatGPT period is 0.1 percentage point and insignificant, echoing the main results that use only CPS data.

**Annotation.** "Negative and indistinguishable from zero" gives the sign and the precision in five words and never says "no effect" — the direction is reported even though it cannot be distinguished, which is honest about what the point estimate is and about what it supports. The second quotation, from the administrative-data check, gives a rounded magnitude in prose ("0.1 percentage point"), its significance, and its relation to the main analysis ("echoing") in one sentence: *magnitude, inference, concordance.*

Two things this phrasing does not do. It never puts the coefficient or its standard error in the text — both sit in a boxed annotation inside the figure, which means neither number is quotable from the document's prose, and a verification script of the kind this team runs would find nothing to match. And no minimum detectable effect appears anywhere, so "indistinguishable from zero" has no lower bound attached: a reader cannot tell which effects the data could have ruled out. That is the single most consequential omission in the appendix, and the one place where copying this document would break our own criteria.

### How a robustness sweep is phrased

> We estimate pooled effects using the same difference-in-differences regression and control group as before, except every point estimate uses a different treated group. As we increase the threshold, zooming in on increasingly exposed occupations, the impact on unemployment remains small and insignificant.

**Annotation.** Sentence one says what is held fixed and what moves, with "except" carrying the whole of the variant — one thing changes, and the reader is told which. Sentence two describes the sweep as motion along a dial ("As we increase the threshold, zooming in on increasingly exposed occupations") and the result as an absence of motion ("remains small and insignificant"). The gerund "zooming in" earns its place: it tells the reader what raising a percentile cut-off *does to the sample*, which is the thing a reader of the exhibit needs and the axis label cannot say.

### How a limitation becomes the next exercise

> The CPS is a survey, which makes it more prone to mismeasurement than administrative data. As one check on the results, we study what happens when we use data from unemployment insurance recipients, who report their former occupation when initially claiming benefits and may lose benefits if they respond inaccurately. These responses are aggregated at the state-quarter level by the Department of Labor into major SOC groups, so our measurement of exposure is less precise.

**Annotation.** Three consecutive sentences: the limitation, the remedy, the remedy's own cost. Nothing is hedged and nothing is repeated later. Two details to copy. First, the new data's credibility is argued from **incentives rather than from authority** — respondents "may lose benefits if they respond inaccurately" is a better reason to trust an occupation code than the phrase "administrative data" is. Second, the cost of the fix is stated in the same paragraph and in the currency the paper cares about: coarser occupation groups mean "our measurement of exposure is less precise". A robustness check written this way cannot be read as a free win.

### How a validity check is placed before the result it licenses

> Still, when we make quartiles using these categories, there's a stark difference in average observed coverage: the bottom quartile has 1% coverage, on average, compared to 31% in the top quartile. The four highly exposed categories are: Computer & Mathematical, Office & Administrative Support, Business & Financial, and Sales.

> These estimates are qualitatively similar to the main results using only the CPS data. Apart from during the COVID-19 pandemic, the gap between the most and least exposed groups has remained consistent. (This gap is smaller than in the main results, likely because these broad categories group together several different occupations.)

**Annotation.** "Still," concedes the previous paragraph and then rescues the exercise: the coarse grouping is imprecise, but it still separates exposure, and the evidence is a two-number comparison with "compared to" doing the work. Then the four qualifying categories are **named**, which lets a reader judge face validity in a way a coverage share cannot — and quietly shows that the treated group mixes software work with clerical work, a composition point the appendix never returns to. The reconciliation with the paper is a parenthesis with "likely because": a discrepancy acknowledged, a mechanism conjectured, the conjecture marked as one, no attempt to resolve it. **A disagreement with your own main result belongs in parentheses in the sentence that reports the check, with the word that marks it as conjecture.**

### How a construct-validity problem is written as a section

> One potential pitfall in the O\*NET task database is that the level of specificity in a task statement is somewhat arbitrary.

> These are most likely describing the same activities, but Allergists may look more exposed based on AI usage just because their task happens to be described in a generic way, earning a preference from our classifier.

**Annotation.** The pitfall is stated as a property of the *external* data and then traced to a bias in *this* measure through a named mechanism: generic task wording attracts classifier matches, so the occupation whose task happens to be phrased generically scores higher. The illustration is a pair of real task statements from two real occupations, which the document quotes in full before drawing the inference, and the damage is stated in the conditional ("may look more exposed") because the appendix has not measured it. A section-length limitation written this way — data property, mechanism, worked example, undetermined magnitude — is more useful than a bullet, and it is also where our house standard bites: the mechanism is named and never bounded, and no validation of the classifier appears anywhere in the document.

## Comparisons

The second half of the appendix is nothing but comparison, and the word "robust" never appears in it. The vehicles are two: rank correlation between measures, and rejection by counter-example.

### The statistic is defined before the exhibit

> There are many potential ways to measure job exposure. Here we explore the correlation between several different formulations. In the heatmap below, each cell gives the Spearman (rank-rank) correlation between the two job-level coverage measures.

**Annotation.** Forty-two words, and they do everything a lead-in should: concede that the measure is one choice among many, name the exercise as exploratory, define what a cell of the exhibit contains, and gloss the statistic in-line for a reader who does not know it — "Spearman (rank-rank)". The parenthesis is the whole technique: **gloss a statistic in three words at its first use rather than assuming it or explaining it in a footnote.** What the lead-in does not say is over how many occupations the correlations are computed, which is the referee's first question and is never answered.

### Variants are one edit from the baseline, named and compared

> Baseline: this is our primary measure, observed coverage, described above.

> DWA-level: Baseline, except tasks are aggregated to the DWA level before taking the occupation level averages. We describe above why we did not use DWA or IWA to aggregate tasks.

> Baseline (coreweight): Same task-level values as Baseline, but aggregated to the occupation level using O\*NET coreweight instead of time fractions.

**Annotation.** The list's grammar is the finding. Every variant is written as an edit of one named reference — "Baseline, except …", "Same task-level values as Baseline, but …", "Same as Eloundou β (time-frac) but …" — so a reader can see that exactly one thing changed and what it was. Six of the nine bullets use that frame. No bullet re-describes the baseline, none changes two things at once except the usage-only variant (which drops the gates, α and the API term together, and therefore cannot be read as the contribution of any one of them), and the bullets are set in plain roman with the name run into the sentence: this is a list of edits, not a glossary, and it does not pretend to be one.

Note the two bullets that carry a *reason* instead of a result:

> Baseline + Ridge impute: Here we used a Ridge regression and task embeddings to impute usage for tasks that were not observed in the data. This was to address the potential issue that some tasks may be implemented on our platforms but do not appear often enough in our sample to meet the privacy threshold for inclusion in the data, which would underestimate the coverage of low-employment occupations. In practice, the imputed measure looks very similar to β.

A variant introduced by the *bias it is meant to repair* — privacy thresholds hide rare tasks, rare tasks concentrate in small occupations, so small occupations look less exposed than they are — and the direction of the bias is named ("would underestimate"). Then the result, and the result is a **resemblance to another measure rather than a verdict on robustness**: "the imputed measure looks very similar to β." Judging a variant by which of the other measures it moves toward is more informative than "results are robust", because it tells the reader what the variant has become.

### Result and inference inside the same bullet

> Baseline x success: Baseline, except the task penetration measure is multiplied by the percent of conversations where the user's goal was achieved. In practice, this almost perfectly correlates with our baseline measure, suggesting that the success rate is not strongly correlated with occupation.

> We incorporated success in an earlier measure of exposure, but its ranking correlates with this simpler version with r=0.999. This suggests that the success rate is not strongly correlated with occupation. We leave the success rate out for this reason, and to make it easier for integration with other data streams.

**Annotation.** Definition, result, inference — three clauses, in that order, and the inference is marked as one by "suggesting". "In practice" appears four times in the document and always at the same hinge: from what was designed to what happened. The endnote then does something the bullet cannot: it gives the correlation as a number, states the inference again, and gives **two** reasons for dropping the variable, one evidential and one practical ("to make it easier for integration with other data streams"). A design decision defended by both its evidence and its convenience, with the convenience admitted, is more credible than one defended by evidence alone.

### Rejection by counter-example, not by statistic

> Combining tasks by the 2,087 DWAs would group very different work activities, damaging the signal. For example, the DWA "Translate information for others" includes the tasks "Recover data or decrypt seized data" and "Travel with or guide tourists who speak another language." There are even fewer IWAs, so this would make the problem worse.

> One could also group by DWA and then condition on some degree of semantic similarity between tasks. This does not work either, because the DWA groupings can cleave otherwise identical tasks.

> The tasks differ by one word, but they are in different DWAs.

**Annotation.** Two candidate methods are killed by two examples and no measurement: one group that contains two absurdly distant members, and two near-identical tasks that fall in different groups. The comparison *is* the finding — no within-group variance, no dispersion statistic, no count of affected tasks. It works because the examples are extreme and quoted verbatim, and because each is followed by the consequence in the measure's own terms ("damaging the signal", "cleave otherwise identical tasks"). It is also the weakest evidence in the document: nothing tells the reader how common either pathology is. **Copy the rhetorical economy; do not copy the missing denominator — an example that rejects a method should come with the share of cases it describes.**

The same section stacks three counts to make the grain matter — "332 IWAs (Intermediate Work Activities)", "2,087 DWAs (Detailed Work Activities)", "the 18,000 task statements" — each with its expansion at first use, and then draws the inference in nine words: "There are even fewer IWAs, so this would make the problem worse."

### The comparison that is a number without an exhibit

> A potential fix we explored is grouping O\*NET tasks with (i) a shared IWA and (ii) semantic similarity of at least 0.7. This leverages the grouping done by O\*NET researchers to validate a seeming conceptual match across two tasks. In practice, this mostly agrees with our slightly simpler task grouping described in the main text, with a Spearman correlation coefficient of 0.9.

**Annotation.** The only robustness result in the document that appears solely as a sentence. Note the construction of the rule — two numbered conditions inside the sentence, "(i) a shared IWA and (ii) semantic similarity of at least 0.7" — and the justification, which borrows authority from the data provider ("leverages the grouping done by O\*NET researchers"). Then the verdict in the same shape as the others: "mostly agrees with …, with a Spearman correlation coefficient of 0.9". What is absent is a sweep over the 0.7 threshold, the similarity model's name, and the occupations that move most; the sentence asks to be believed on one number.

### What the exhibit's design does and the prose does not

Figure 4 is a lower-triangle matrix with a blank leading diagonal, the *Baseline* column boxed in a heavy rule, and a colour scale labelled "Spearman ρ" whose floor sits well above zero, so weak agreement between two measures reads as orange rather than as neutral. Ten measures are compared; the prose defines them in nine bullets, because one definition ("Eloundou β (time-frac)") runs into the end of the previous bullet without a break. And the names drift between prose and exhibit: "Baseline x success over 50%" in the list is "Baseline success ≥ 50%" in the matrix, "Baseline x success" is "Baseline × success", "Claude.ai usage" is "Claude.ai Usage". Per the figure-values ruling no cell value is recorded here; the design points stand without them. Three lessons: **a reference measure should be marked in the exhibit as it is in the prose** (the heavy box is the right instinct); **a colour scale that does not start at zero must be read as a ranking device, not as a magnitude**; and a variant's name is a token — once chosen it appears identically in the list, the exhibit and the text, or a reader cannot join them up.

## Figure captions

Four captions, one per figure, all verbatim:

> **Appendix Figure 1: Trends in the unemployment rate for young (22-25 year old) workers with high AI exposure and no AI exposure, Current Population Survey**

> **Appendix Figure 2: Sensitivity of the results to the percentile cutoff**

> **Appendix Figure 3: Main results using UI claims to measure the unemployment rate**

> **Appendix Figure 4: Spearman correlation across different measures of occupational exposure**

**The pattern.** Small bold sans, set *below* the exhibit and flush to the page margin rather than the text margin, a numbered label with the word "Appendix" in it, a colon, then a single noun phrase. No terminal period. **No caption has a second sentence**, no caption begins "This plot shows", none gives a date range, a sample size, a unit, a source line (except Figure 1's dataset), a direction of movement or a significance statement. Ten to twenty-five words each; 60 words for all four together.

- **Figure 1 is the fullest and the model of the four.** Quantity ("Trends in the unemployment rate"), population with its bracket ("young (22-25 year old) workers"), the two groups being compared ("with high AI exposure and no AI exposure"), and the dataset, appended after a comma ("Current Population Survey"). That is the comparison and its provenance in one line, and it is the caption to imitate: *what is plotted, for whom, against what, from where.* What it omits is the date range and the n; the axis carries the first and nothing carries the second.
- **Figure 2's caption names the exercise, not the quantity.** "Sensitivity of the results to the percentile cutoff" — the reader learns what the figure is *for* and must go to the axis labels for what it plots. Acceptable for a sweep, where the quantity is the same one the reader has just read about; not acceptable for a figure that introduces a new quantity.
- **Figure 3's caption names the change of data.** "Main results using UI claims to measure the unemployment rate" — ten words to say *same specification, different outcome source*, with "Main results using X" as the compact frame. Worth copying for any replication-on-other-data exhibit.
- **Figure 4's caption names the statistic and the object.** "Spearman correlation across different measures of occupational exposure". The statistic is in the caption, its gloss is in the prose, its scale is in the colourbar; nothing is said twice.
- **Everything quantitative is inside the image.** In-figure text, quoted here as in-figure and never as caption: the axis labels "Unemployment rate (%)", "DiD coefficient", "Insured unemployment rate", "Pooled post-period ATT (unemployment)", "Treatment percentile cutoff" and the colourbar label "Spearman ρ"; the legend entries "Top quartile (most exposed)", "No exposure", "Bottom quartile (least exposed)", "ChatGPT release" and "Baseline (p75)"; and a boxed "Pooled post: …" annotation in the corner of Figures 1 and 3. Two consequences worth naming. The treatment date enters the document as a **dashed line with a legend entry**, not as a sentence, which is why the prose can say "following the release of ChatGPT" without ever dating it. And the pooled estimate is an in-figure annotation, so the document's central result is legible only from the picture — the opposite of the rule this team works to.
- **"AI" in the caption, platforms in the method.** Figure 1's caption says "high AI exposure and no AI exposure" while the machinery underneath is Claude.ai conversations and 1P API transcripts, and the body's one slip runs the other way ("Allergists may look more exposed based on AI usage"). The document's vocabulary is three-way and mostly principled: platform names for counts ("Claude.ai", "1P API"), "LLM" for a borrowed general capability score ("theoretically doable with an LLM"), "AI" for the concept and the exposure label. Our rule is stricter at the finding level: the question says AI, every finding says Claude, so a caption describing *usage* says Claude even where the construct it feeds is called AI exposure.
- **No exhibit has alt text.** All four figures are raster images (Figure 1 a JPEG 2000, the rest PNG) tagged `/Figure` with no `/Alt` entry. The document's only alt string is on the cover wordmark and is machine-written; per `room/director-2026-09-16-alt-text-ruling.md` there is nothing else to record. The four displayed equations are images too, and they carry neither captions nor numbers nor alt text.

## Limitations

**There is no limitations section and no limitations heading.** The words "limitation" and "caveat" do not occur in the document; neither does "robust"; "Sensitivity" occurs once, in a figure caption. Limitation work is done in six places, each with a different mechanic:

1. **Inside the construct's definition.** "It does not measure the intensive margin of AI use (increased adoption in debugging vs. email drafting, for example), because the connection with usage and work performed is fuzzy and highly dependent on the task."
2. **Inside the rule that creates it.** "Since we do not record the contextual information to assign these to one job or another, we group these identical and highly similar tasks and allocate the task count equally across jobs according to employment shares." — the reason for the allocation is the concession; the reader gets why and how in one sentence.
3. **As the transition to the next exercise.** "The CPS is a survey, which makes it more prone to mismeasurement than administrative data."
4. **As the cost of the remedy, in the same paragraph.** "These responses are aggregated at the state-quarter level by the Department of Labor into major SOC groups, so our measurement of exposure is less precise."
5. **As a parenthesis reconciling a discrepancy.** "(This gap is smaller than in the main results, likely because these broad categories group together several different occupations.)"
6. **As a whole section.** "Task granularity" is a limitations section that never calls itself one: "One potential pitfall in the O\*NET task database is that the level of specificity in a task statement is somewhat arbitrary", followed by the example, the mechanism and two failed remedies. Plus the endnotes, which carry the two concessions the body could not fit.

**The endnote that defends an asymmetry** is the best-built paragraph in the document and worth reading as a template:

> Of course, some non-work conversations could impact the relevant jobs. People getting nutrition plans and detailed biology instruction from AI may rely less on professional biology tutors and nutritionists. In practice, counting these transcripts tends to increase noise overall given the range of inquiries around neurology and animal care, for instance. There may be personal use cases of API workflows. We still weight these the same as work-related Claude.ai tasks because personal questions (say, nutrition advice) that are routed through an API call seem more likely to represent incipient automation.

Five moves: concede the objection in the objector's own terms ("Of course"); illustrate it with two cases that make it look strong; give the empirical reason for the choice anyway ("In practice, counting these transcripts tends to increase noise"); concede the *symmetric* problem on the other side ("There may be personal use cases of API workflows"); and defend the asymmetric treatment with a mechanism rather than a convenience ("seem more likely to represent incipient automation"). **When a measure treats two data sources differently, the note that defends it concedes both directions and names the mechanism that breaks the tie.**

**Register of the concessions.** Flat declarative, first person plural, no softeners: "One potential pitfall", "This does not work either", "There is still ample room for improvement", "so our measurement of exposure is less precise". Conjecture is marked by verbs and adverbs that carry their own uncertainty — "seem" and "seems" four times, "likely" four times, "may look more exposed", "could have" — and never by a hedging adverb of the "importantly" or "notably" kind. No sentence re-asserts the contribution after a concession, and no concession is introduced by a phrase that announces itself ("it should be noted that", "as with all measures"). Nothing is aggregated into a list, so nothing can be skipped by a reader who skips a section — and nothing can be counted either, which is the cost the Learning-curves appendix pays too.

**What a referee would raise first, and this appendix does not answer.** No minimum detectable effect beside either null, so "indistinguishable from zero" is unbounded. No estimating equation, fixed-effect structure, clustering or weighting for the difference-in-differences, and no pre-trend test despite an event-time plot. No sample size anywhere, and no count of occupations behind any correlation, so nine of the ten measures are dropped or kept on rank correlations with no uncertainty and no N. The gate-invariance claim is asserted and never shown. The comparison is rank-only, although the employment analysis uses a *quartile cut* on levels. The wave imputation in note 1 is never validated against the wave that has the primitive. And no release folder or date window is named, so nothing in the document is reproducible from the document. The full enumeration is in `wiki/reports/labor-market-impacts-2026-03-appendix.md` under "What it did not test"; it is not repeated here.

**The lesson to take and the lesson to leave.** Take the placements: a caveat in the definition of the variable, a limitation used as the bridge to the check that answers it, the remedy's cost in the remedy's own paragraph, and the disagreement with the main result in parentheses beside the number that disagrees. Take the endnote template for a defended asymmetry. Leave the arithmetic: in this house the null carries its MDE, the specification is written down, and a correlation that decides which measure ships carries its N and an interval.

## Close

**There is no close.** The last section ends with the nine bullets on p. 9; the heatmap and its caption occupy the top of p. 10; the endnotes run under a short rule to the top third of p. 11; and the last text in the document is a data-source pointer:

> The specific data product is ETA 203 "Characteristics of the insured unemployed," available at this link: https://oui.doleta.gov/unemploy/DataDownloads.asp

The bottom two-thirds of p. 11 are white. There is no conclusion, no summary, no restatement of what the appendix established, no verdict on which of the ten measures is right beyond "this is our primary measure", no pointer back to the paper, no reference list, no data-availability statement, no acknowledgements and no contact.

**Annotation.**

- **The nearest thing to a close is buried mid-document.** The end of "Task granularity", on p. 8: "There is still ample room for improvement in the O\*NET task framework. Calibrating the specificity of tasks appropriately, and representing their inter-dependency, could be a useful next step." With it, a page earlier, the invitation "Researchers could aggregate the 18,000 task statements to the DWA or IWA level." That is a what-comes-next — and it is addressed to the field rather than to the authors, attached to the limitation that provoked it rather than to the document's contribution, and placed three pages before the end. An appendix can afford to put its forward look where the problem is; a post cannot, because the reader who needs it is the one who reached the end.
- **Ending on an endnote is the genre's ending, and the third variant of it in this corpus.** The Learning-curves appendix ends on a footnote withdrawing comparability with earlier releases; the social-sciences appendix ends on a table's *Notes* line; this one ends on a URL for a government data product. Three appendices, three endings that are pieces of apparatus. An appendix stops when it has handed over the last object it was holding.
- **Title against ending.** The title points outward to the parent paper; the ending points outward to the Department of Labor. Nothing in the document points at the document. That is consistent, and it is why no close is needed here — but it also means the appendix never says what it thinks it showed, and a reader who wants that verdict has to form it from a heatmap.
- **The document gets less committed as it ends, and this is deliberate.** The last substantive thing it does is show that nine alternative measures rank occupations much as the baseline does — which defends the baseline and simultaneously concedes that the choice among them was not forced. An appendix is allowed to leave that standing. A post is not: if the measure could have been built nine ways, the post's close says which way it was built, what that buys, and what a reader should do with the difference.
- **What this licenses in our own posts, and what it does not.** Licensed: a methodology section that opens on its unit of analysis and stops without a summary; an equation introduced by a colon and identified by its left-hand side; a threshold defended by conversion into the units of the phenomenon; a variant list written as one-edit-from-baseline; a forward look attached to the limitation that provoked it. Not licensed: a post that ends on an endnote, a result that exists only inside a figure, a null without its MDE, or a robustness claim without the table it points at. The title of a post has to match its ending, and this document's title and ending match only because neither is about the work.
