## Section order

Headings in order, with what each does and how long it runs.

1. **Tag "Economics" + H1 + date + hero image.** The hero is a 1000×1000 SVG with alt text equal to the title; decorative, uncaptioned. No "read the paper" link above the prose, because the post is the paper.
2. **H2 "Summary"** — five bullets, ~150 words, the whole block set in italic. Contains the sample, two headline rates, two disparity ratios, the output result with its caveat attached, and the belief result. This is the summary block the house style forbids, and it is the most consequential structural fact about the page (see the annotation in `## Opening move`).
3. **H2 "How are AI coding agents changing how we study the economy and society?"** — four paragraphs, ~330 words. Why it matters, the literature, the two-sided conjecture, and the design. The framing question is the heading; the section never answers it.
4. **H2 "A new survey on AI coding agent use among quantitative social scientists"** — two paragraphs, ~150 words. The entire method section: field window, target population, the non-representativeness concession with its cause and its one check, discipline composition, career-stage composition. Everything else is deferred to the appendix.
5. **H2 "Coding agents haven't reached most social scientists"** — three paragraphs plus **Figure 1** and its caption, ~200 words. The two adoption measures, each introduced by its verbatim question stem; the 81% / 20% pair; the tool mix among adopters.
6. **H2 "Adoption is highly uneven"** — four paragraphs plus **Figure 2** and **Figure 3** and their captions, ~280 words. Field gradient (reading Figure 1, which sits in the previous section), career stage, gender and university, then the robustness paragraph on the gender gap.
7. **H2 "Researchers mainly use AI to code and edit, not write"** — two paragraphs plus **Figure 4**, ~200 words. What AI is used for, adopters against other AI users.
8. **H2 "Coding agent users are posting more working papers and sending out more grant proposals, but not submitting more to journals"** — four paragraphs plus **Figure 5**, ~360 words. The longest section and the paper's core. Order inside it: the motivating question and the descriptive-only downgrade in one paragraph, *then* the figure, then two paragraphs reading it, then the null and its two readings.
9. **H2 "Researchers expect AI tools to raise productivity, but are less confident that they will improve social science overall"** — three paragraphs plus **Figure 6**, ~230 words. The two belief measures and the within-respondent gap.
10. **H2 "Discussion"** — four paragraphs, ~300 words. Restatement without numbers; the distributional finding with its conjectures; the caveats paragraph; the close.
11. **Back matter** — H3 Authors, H3 Acknowledgements, H3 Appendix, H3 Citation (BibTeX), H3 References (five, APA), H4 Footnotes (one).

**Where the findings sit.** Three times, at three depths: the Summary bullets, one declarative H2 each, and one sentence each in the Discussion. The H2s are the strongest organising device on the page — **five of the seven substantive headings are the finding**, stated as a sentence:

- "Coding agents haven't reached most social scientists"
- "Adoption is highly uneven"
- "Researchers mainly use AI to code and edit, not write"
- "Coding agent users are posting more working papers and sending out more grant proposals, but not submitting more to journals"
- "Researchers expect AI tools to raise productivity, but are less confident that they will improve social science overall"

A reader who reads only the table of contents has the paper. This is a third device, distinct from the February report's bold lead-ins inside a single "Results" section and from the April report's how/what/who question headings: here the heading *asserts*, and the two longest headings carry their own counter-clause ("but not submitting more to journals"; "but are less confident"). **A heading that contains the word "but" is the most compressed form of number-plus-caveat available**, and it is the single most copyable thing on this page. The cost is stated in `## Comparisons`: the two headings that assert without a "but" ("Coding agent users are posting more working papers…", read against the same section's "Differences should not be interpreted as causal") put an indicative claim in the largest type on the page and the disclaimer in the body.

**Where there is no Limitations section.** There is none. Nothing on this page is headed "Limitations" or "Caveats". The caveat apparatus runs on four layers instead — a summary bullet, a method-section concession, per-section downgrades, and a caveats *paragraph* inside the Discussion — and the allocation is annotated in full under `## Limitations`. This is the first piece in the corpus to carry more qualification than the April 2025 report while giving it no heading of its own.

**Where the method sits.** In two paragraphs before the first finding (§4), with the questionnaire items distributed into the findings sections where they are used, the discipline definitions and the regression specification deferred to the appendix, and the one measurement exclusion in the footnote. The consequence for the reader is unusual and worth recording: the *sample* is described before the findings, but the *instrument* is described inside them — each finding opens by quoting the question that produced it.

## Opening move

The page opens twice: once in the Summary and once in the framing section. Both are quoted here, because the second cannot be read as an opening without the first.

**The Summary, verbatim** (the whole block is set in italic in the source):

> ## Summary
>
> - *We present results from a survey of 1,260 social scientists about AI and coding agent use, fielded in February and March 2026.*
> - *The vast majority of respondents (81%) have tried using AI chatbots in research, particularly for writing code and editing prose. But only 20% have adopted coding agents—tools like Claude Code that autonomously write and execute analysis code—into their work.*
> - *There are sharp disparities in use of coding agents. Twice as many researchers with typically male names use coding agents as those with female names. Researchers at top universities are 40% more likely than others to use coding agents.*
> - *Users of coding agents post more working papers and grant proposals than others in the same discipline and career stage, but this could reflect pre-existing differences among early adopters.*
> - *Researchers are more optimistic about AI helping write publishable papers than about the effects of AI on the social sciences as a whole.*

**The framing section, verbatim** — the heading and all four paragraphs (hyperlink URLs stripped; anchor text marked below):

> ## **How are AI coding agents changing how we study the economy and society?**
>
> The human sciences are shifting: for the first time, core research tasks can be handed off to machines. AI chatbots increasingly contribute to scientific research, including in the most prestigious publications and in the social sciences. This has spurred optimism that AI could boost research productivity—while also stoking fears about overloaded peer review and a deluge of academic AI slop.

> But while turn-taking AI chatbots have primarily been used for writing assistance, coding agents could restructure social science research more radically. Agentic coding platforms like Claude Code and Codex can take a research idea and a dataset, write and run an analysis, interpret the output, and iterate autonomously. What had been irreducibly human steps in empirical research can, for the first time, be automated. At the extreme, researchers have built multi-agent pipelines to automate computer science research and autonomously execute social science research ideas.

> These tools could accelerate science and make it more daring: fast research execution should mean cheap and plentiful discovery. They could also amplify disparities in research resources and exacerbate congestion in the scholarly record. More deeply, as AI handles a broadening swath of research tasks, its distinctive analytical choices could stamp our collective understanding of our economy, our society, and ourselves.

> In this post, we offer a first look, drawing on a survey of 1,260 quantitative social scientists fielded in early 2026. The survey is the baseline wave of a larger ongoing study of how coding agents affect research productivity, including a randomized experiment providing researchers with access to Claude Code. We will publish results from this experiment in the future. For now, we report what the baseline survey reveals about who is using these tools and for what; how output differs between users and non-users; and what researchers expect about the implications of growing adoption.

Anchor text carrying links, in order: "prestigious publications" → Nature Human Behaviour; "social sciences" → Sociological Science; "writing assistance" → the *same* Sociological Science article; "automated" → NBER w34202; "computer science research" → Nature; "science research ideas" → SocArXiv/OSF.

**Annotation.**

- **The question is the heading, and it is the widest question the data could possibly bear.** "How are AI coding agents changing how we study the economy and society?" The survey is one cross-section of 1,260 self-selected researchers with no time dimension; the heading asks about change in the production of collective knowledge. The gap between the two is not hidden — the next sentence of the body is "we offer a first look" — but the heading is the largest type on the page and makes no concession. The device to take is the *shape*: a question naming the phenomenon, answered at the scale the evidence supports, with the scale stated in the paragraph that introduces the data. The device not to take is putting "changing" in a headline over a single wave (see `## Close`, where "diffusing" and "increasingly" do the same thing).
- **Why it matters, in one clause, and it is an epistemic stake rather than a labour-market one.** "for the first time, core research tasks can be handed off to machines." Not jobs, not wages, not output — *tasks in the reader's own workflow*. The February report opened on the whole labour market; the April report conceded a narrow sector and elevated it; this one opens on the reader's own craft, which is the cheapest possible route to why-it-matters when the audience is the population. **When a post's audience is its sample, the stake is the audience's own practice, and it can be claimed in a subordinate clause.**
- **The two-sided conjecture is stated before any finding and given its own paragraph.** Paragraph three is upside then downside then something larger: "could accelerate science and make it more daring", against "could also amplify disparities in research resources and exacerbate congestion in the scholarly record", then "its distinctive analytical choices could stamp our collective understanding". Three modals in three sentences, no evidence offered, and every one of them is later discharged — accelerate → the output section; amplify disparities → the disparities section; congestion → the belief section's field-impact gap. **The opening's conjectures are the post's section list**, which is why a reader never feels the speculation is idle. The third conjecture is the exception and the page knows it: nothing in the survey measures analytical choices, and it is not restated in the Discussion's findings paragraph.
- **How soon the first number appears.** Immediately, in the Summary: "1,260" in the first eight words, "81%" and "20%" in the second bullet. But in the *body* the first number is 1,260 again, about 280 words in, and it is the sample size; the first body finding number is 81%, about 450 words in. So the page runs both orders at once — numbers-first for the skimmer, stakes-first for the reader — and pays for it with the summary block.
- **What the summary block costs, stated plainly because our template forbids it.** Three of the five bullets say something the body does not:
  - "Researchers at top universities are **40%** more likely than others to use coding agents" — this number **appears only in the Summary**. The body says "High-status and private universities also see notably higher use", with no figure anywhere.
  - "**Twice** as many researchers with typically male names use coding agents as those with female names" — the body says "**more than twice** the rate". Two statements of one ratio at two strengths; neither is wrong, and a reader quoting the Summary understates the paper.
  - "have tried using **AI chatbots** in research" — the question asked was about "genAI models" (quoted verbatim in §5), which is broader than chat interfaces. The Summary narrows the construct that the body then contrasts against coding agents.
  A summary block written after the body drifts from it, in the direction of the sharper number. This is the same fault the April 2025 report showed with its 13%/23.8% enterprise share, in a different mechanism: there the body restated the summary, here the summary sharpens the body. **One entry per number, quoted identically everywhere, is the only defence**, and it is why the house template has no summary block to drift.
- **The one hedge that survives into the Summary is the right one.** Bullet four: "Users of coding agents post more working papers and grant proposals than others in the same discipline and career stage, **but this could reflect pre-existing differences among early adopters**." The comparison group is inside the claim ("in the same discipline and career stage"), and the rival explanation is in the same sentence, after a "but", not in a following sentence that can be dropped. Copy this construction: **a summary-level finding carries its comparison group and its rival explanation in one sentence or it is not summary-ready.**
- **The design paragraph does four things in four sentences and refuses one.** Sample and vintage ("1,260 quantitative social scientists fielded in early 2026"); the study it belongs to and what is coming ("the baseline wave of a larger ongoing study … including a randomized experiment"); the promise ("We will publish results from this experiment in the future"); and then the scope of *this* piece, enumerated with semicolons in the order the sections follow — "who is using these tools and for what; how output differs between users and non-users; and what researchers expect". The refusal is the strongest part: the causal question is named, assigned to the experiment, and withheld. **Naming the result you are not reporting, and where it will be reported, is what buys a baseline-wave post the right to a framing question this wide.**
- **An anchor placed mid-phrase.** "autonomously execute social science research ideas" links from "science research ideas" only, so the hyperlink starts in the middle of the noun phrase. Immaterial to the prose; recorded because the anchor text of a citation is the only citation a web reader sees, and half a noun phrase is not a legible one.

## Findings and their caveats

Five findings, one per declarative heading. Every one of them is a survey finding, and the page has a single consistent grammar for them: **the question stem first, verbatim and in quotation marks, then the share.** That is the most transferable thing in this section and it is annotated at each occurrence.

### Finding 1 — 81% have tried AI, 20% use coding agents

Summary version, verbatim:

> *The vast majority of respondents (81%) have tried using AI chatbots in research, particularly for writing code and editing prose. But only 20% have adopted coding agents—tools like Claude Code that autonomously write and execute analysis code—into their work.*

Body version, verbatim, the three paragraphs of "Coding agents haven't reached most social scientists":

> We measured overall AI use in two ways. First, we asked "Have you previously used genAI models to aid your research process?" 81% of respondents said yes.

> But what about those who have actually adopted increasingly capable coding agents into their workflow? Here, we asked "Do you regularly (more than once a week) use an AI coding assistant integrated into your command line (such as Codex, Cursor, or Claude Code)?" In a follow-up question, we verified that they used one of those tools (or Google Antigravity).[1]

> Only 20% of respondents use coding agents. Our survey came around two months after a flurry of discussion about Claude Code and Opus 4.6 that kicked off in late December of 2025. Yet even among interested respondents who self-selected into our survey, only ⅕ had adopted agents into their workflow. Claude Code is the most common coding agent tool reported, with 86% of users reporting Claude Code use (31% report using Codex, the next most common tool).

And footnote 1, verbatim:

> We launched the survey only a month after the release of Claude Cowork and before the release of OpenAI's Codex app, so we focused this question on Command Line Interface tools for interaction with coding agents. This means we do not count researchers who use AI agents exclusively via more general purpose desktop apps.

**Annotation.**

- **The question is the definition.** "First, we asked "Have you previously used genAI models to aid your research process?" 81% of respondents said yes." Fourteen words of question stem, then the share, then nothing. No construct name is invented, no gloss is offered, and the reader can see exactly what 81% is a share of — including its looseness ("previously used", "aid", no time bound, no intensity). **For a survey finding, quoting the item is stricter than defining the construct, because the item is what the respondent answered and the construct is what the author hoped they answered.** This is the pattern to use for every survey number we ever report.
- **And the narrow measure gets the same treatment, at greater length.** The coding-agent item is quoted in full with its frequency threshold inside the stem ("regularly (more than once a week)"), its interface restriction ("integrated into your command line") and its three examples. Then the *verification step* is disclosed in the next sentence — "In a follow-up question, we verified that they used one of those tools (or Google Antigravity)" — so the reader knows the 20% is not a bare self-report on a leading question. A measurement procedure disclosed in the sentence before the number is worth more than a limitation bullet about self-report.
- **The exclusion that the item creates is in a footnote at the item, with its reason and its date.** Footnote 1 names what the measure misses (desktop-app agent users), why the measure was built that way (Claude Cowork one month old, the Codex app not yet released), and states the consequence as a countable fact: "we do not count researchers who use AI agents exclusively via more general purpose desktop apps." Direction of the resulting bias is left to the reader, but it is unambiguous from the wording — 20% is a floor. Same architecture as the April 2025 report's footnotes 2 and 3: **a caveat that applies to one number goes at that number.**
- **The rhetorical question as a section hinge.** "But what about those who have actually adopted increasingly capable coding agents into their workflow?" This is how the page moves from the broad measure to the narrow one, and it does the work a transition sentence would do while also telling the reader that 81% was not the finding. "actually" is carrying an argument.
- **The comparison is the finding, and the second number is the one in the heading.** 81% and 20% mean nothing apart; the heading takes the 20% ("Coding agents haven't reached most social scientists") and the Summary takes both with "But only" between them. Note the two interpretive words: "The vast majority" before 81%, "only" before 20%, so the reader is told the direction of each before reading either.
- **The restatement in a different notation, and what it buys.** "only ⅕ had adopted agents into their workflow" — the same 20% as a vulgar fraction, four sentences after the percentage, inside a sentence about self-selection: "Yet even **among interested respondents who self-selected into our survey**, only ⅕ had adopted agents." The restatement exists to carry the selection concession, not to restate the number: the sentence's argument is that 20% is an *upper* bound for the population even though footnote 1 makes it a lower bound for the construct. Two bounds pointing opposite ways, both stated within four sentences, neither reconciled. Recorded as a writing observation — **when two selection arguments push a number in opposite directions, the post has to say which it thinks dominates, or say that it does not know.**
- **The one piece of context that dates the number.** "Our survey came around two months after a flurry of discussion about Claude Code and Opus 4.6 that kicked off in late December of 2025." A vintage stamp on an adoption rate, with the event that makes the vintage matter named. An adoption share without a date is uninterpretable, and this is the cheapest way to date one.
- **Observed versus conjectured.** Paragraphs one and two are measurement only, with no interpretive word in either. The single interpretive clause in the section is "Yet even among interested respondents who self-selected into our survey", and it *deflates* rather than inflates. No mechanism for low adoption is offered anywhere in the section — no "this likely reflects" — which is a restraint the corpus's other pieces do not show.
- **Claude, and the one place it carries a number.** "Claude Code is the most common coding agent tool reported, with 86% of users reporting Claude Code use (31% report using Codex, the next most common tool)." Two fences and a competitor: "reported" / "reporting" attributes the tool mix to respondents rather than to logs, "of users" restates the denominator as the 20% rather than the 1,260, and Codex is given in the same parenthesis so the 86% cannot be quoted as a market share. The shares are stated as not summing ("the most common", multi-select implied by the pair) but the page never says the item was multi-select — the reader infers it from 86 + 31 > 100. **Where shares can exceed 100, the sentence says so.**

### Finding 2 — adoption is uneven by field, career stage, gender and university

Verbatim, the four paragraphs of "Adoption is highly uneven":

> Figure 1 shows there is large variation in the overall adoption rate, from 39% of economists and 25% of political scientists to single digits for public health (6%), education (4%) and communication (6%). This gradient roughly tracks differences across fields in overall AI use, but differences in coding agent adoption are steeper on average.

> Just over a quarter of doctoral students and postdocs use coding agents at least weekly; among tenured professors that rate falls by more than half. The researchers adopting coding agents are the juniors—more technologically fluent, more likely to be working directly with code and data, and facing stronger career pressures to produce research.

> Adoption differences extend beyond discipline and career stage. We classify researcher names according to gender and find that those with typically male names have adopted coding agents at more than twice the rate of respondents with typically female names. High-status and private universities also see notably higher use. All of these differences are significant at the p<0.05 level. These differences are starker than the differences in overall AI use, and suggest higher inequality, at least in this early period of coding agent adoption.

> The gender gap in coding agent use does not just reflect a gap in rates of trying AI. Among respondents who have tried using AI for research, there is even a slightly larger gender gap in regular coding agent use than in the overall sample. These differences also persist when comparing across genders in the same disciplines and career stages.

**Annotation.**

- **The measure is named before the result, in the same sentence, every time.** "We classify researcher names according to gender **and find that** those with typically male names…" — the derivation and the finding share one sentence, so the reader cannot take the gender gap without the name classifier. The phrase used throughout is "typically male names" / "typically female names", never "men" and "women"; the Discussion then drops the fence and says "men". **The fence on a derived variable has to survive into the close, or the close is claiming a different variable.** This is the clearest instance in the corpus of a construct quietly widening between finding and conclusion, and it is the one to guard against in our own drafts.
- **A rate given as a decline, not as a level.** "Just over a quarter of doctoral students and postdocs use coding agents at least weekly; among tenured professors that rate falls by more than half." One level, then a relative change, and the tenured level is never stated — so the reader can compute roughly 12% only by assuming "just over a quarter" is 26% and "more than half" is exactly half. The April 2025 report's "nearly 20% higher than their Claude.ai usage" was the same fault; the lesson is unchanged and this is a second illustration: **a difference is reported in its units, and both levels are given.** The house rule earns its keep here twice, because the same paragraph also reports the gender gap as a bare ratio ("more than twice the rate") with neither rate published.
- **"at least weekly" restates the threshold at the point of use.** The item's frequency condition ("more than once a week") is re-stated in the sentence that reports the career-stage rate, so a reader arriving at this section cold knows what adoption means. The threshold appears four times on the page — in the item, in the Figure 1 caption, here, and in the Figure 2 caption — and that repetition is right.
- **The mechanism paragraph is marked by punctuation, not by a hedge.** "The researchers adopting coding agents are the juniors—more technologically fluent, more likely to be working directly with code and data, and facing stronger career pressures to produce research." Three explanations after an em dash, none measured anywhere in the survey, and the sentence carries no modal at all. This is the weakest-written sentence in the findings: an unhedged list of unmeasured causes, in a paper that elsewhere hedges scrupulously. Compare the February report's "This likely reflects…" and the April report's "Speculatively," — both of which mark the same move. **An explanation the data cannot see takes a modal or an adverb, even when it is obvious.**
- **Significance is asserted for a family of comparisons in one clause.** "All of these differences are significant at the p<0.05 level", restated in the Figure 3 caption as "All differences in adoption between groups are statistically significant with p < 0.05." A universal claim over an unspecified number of group contrasts, with no correction and no n per cell. The writing lesson, not a judgement on the analysis: **"all of these differences are significant" names no test, no comparison count and no cell size, and a sentence of that shape cannot be checked** — ours state the estimate, its interval and its n, or defer to a table in the drawer.
- **The inequality claim is a comparison of comparisons, and it is hedged on scope rather than on magnitude.** "These differences are starker than the differences in overall AI use, and suggest higher inequality, **at least in this early period** of coding agent adoption." "suggest" fences the inference; "at least in this early period" fences the extrapolation; nothing fences the comparison itself, which is made across the two panels of Figure 3 without a statistic. Two fences and no number, in the sentence the section heading is drawn from.
- **The robustness paragraph is written as a rival explanation defeated, not as a check passed.** "The gender gap in coding agent use **does not just reflect** a gap in rates of trying AI." The paragraph names the alternative reading first, then reports two conditional results against it: among those who have tried AI the gap is "even a slightly larger gender gap", and "These differences also persist when comparing across genders in the same disciplines and career stages." No conditional estimate is published for either. **Naming the rival explanation in the topic sentence and answering it in the next is the right shape; the estimate belongs in the sentence that claims the answer**, and in our posts it goes there with its interval.
- **Observed versus conjectured.** Observation: the field rates, the career-stage decline, the gender ratio, the two conditional persistences. Conjecture: the juniors' three mechanisms (unmarked), "suggest higher inequality" (marked). The boundary falls inside paragraphs here rather than between them, which is looser than the April report's practice of turning modal only at a paragraph break.
- **Where a number is absent and the absence is visible.** "High-status and private universities also see notably higher use" — no figure in the body, no figure in the caption, and the private/public gap is never quantified anywhere on the page. The top-university figure that does exist ("40% more likely") is in the Summary alone. So the paper's second-largest disparity claim is carried by the word "notably" in the body and by a number in a bullet the body does not contain.

### Finding 3 — what AI is used for

Verbatim, the two paragraphs of "Researchers mainly use AI to code and edit, not write":

> Among researchers using AI, whether through coding agents or chatbots, what are they actually using it for? Debate about AI in academic research has focused heavily on writing: hallucinated literature reviews, "it's not X, it's Y" strewn across formulaic introductions, and the possibility of fully automated paper writing.

> But Figure 4 shows that the most common use, for both coding agent users and others, is for coding up analysis of quantitative data: 97% of coding agent users and 77% of other AI users report using it to generate code. Next most common is editing prose, followed by asking for methods advice and background on prior research. Aggregating across coding agent users and others, only a third of all AI users have used it to draft prose at all. These patterns generally hold across disciplines, with only economists and management researchers commonly using AI to draft prose.

**Annotation.**

- **The finding is framed as a correction of the reader's prior, and the prior is stated in the reader's own vocabulary.** "Debate about AI in academic research has focused heavily on writing: hallucinated literature reviews, "it's not X, it's Y" strewn across formulaic introductions, and the possibility of fully automated paper writing." Three concrete instances of the expectation, one of them a joke the audience will recognise, then "But Figure 4 shows…". **When a finding's value is that it contradicts what everyone assumes, the assumption is written out in specifics first, and the more specific it is the larger the finding becomes.** This is the same *expectation → result* architecture as the February report's "As we predicted", run in the opposite direction.
- **The subject of the finding is the respondent, and the denominator is restated for each share.** "97% of coding agent users and 77% of other AI users report using it to generate code." "report using" attributes to self-report; the two groups are named rather than implied; and the base is people, not conversations or tasks. Note the denominator discipline in the second half of the paragraph: "Aggregating across coding agent users and others, only a third of **all AI users**" — the aggregation is announced and the new base is named in the same clause. The April 2025 report drifted across four denominators in one section; this page has two (AI users, coding-agent users) and names which one every time.
- **Rank without magnitude, and the shares stay in the figure.** "Next most common is editing prose, followed by asking for methods advice and background on prior research." Three categories, one ordering, no numbers, and the values exist only inside the Figure 4 image — so they are not recorded anywhere in this file, per `room/director-2026-09-16-figure-values-ruling.md`. The corpus pattern holds exactly: **shares that support the headline go in prose; shares that complete a distribution stay in the figure.** For our posts the completion goes into a table in the evidence drawer, and the sentence points at it.
- **The heading overstates by one word, and the body's own number says so.** "Researchers mainly use AI to code and edit, not write" against "only a third of all AI users have used it to draft prose at all". A third is a substantial minority; "not write" reads as a near-absence; and "editing prose" — the second most common use — is writing assistance by any reasonable reading. The page supplies the number that qualifies its own heading, in the same section, which is the honest version of this fault. **A declarative heading is a quantitative claim and takes the same discipline as a sentence: ours are checked against `results.json` too, captions and headings included.**
- **"only" does a lot of work and is used twice in one paragraph in opposite directions.** "only a third of all AI users have used it to draft prose" (deflating a share the heading wants small) and "with only economists and management researchers commonly using AI to draft prose" (restricting a set). Both are interpretations embedded inside the clause, as in the rest of the corpus.
- **The discipline check is stated and not shown.** "These patterns generally hold across disciplines" — a heterogeneity claim, with the exception named, delegated to the right panel of Figure 4 and to no number. "generally hold" is the whole quantitative content.

### Finding 4 — output differences, and the null

Verbatim, the four paragraphs of "Coding agent users are posting more working papers and sending out more grant proposals, but not submitting more to journals" (Figure 5 and its caption sit between the first and second paragraphs, where the ▸ is marked):

> Are coding agents making researchers more productive? That's the question motivating the broader study this survey kicks off. The experiment we are running on this question is still ongoing. But the baseline survey lets us compare coding agent users to others across a whole bunch of checkpoints in the research process. This comparison is purely descriptive: we compare researchers who select into coding agent use to those who do not, and expect that the two groups differ in a number of ways that we cannot adjust for. Differences should not be interpreted as causal, but as a first cut comparison between researchers using coding agents and those who are not.

▸ *(Figure 5 and its caption)*

> Figure 5 shows self-reported output over the six months before the survey at different stages of the research process, from projects started to papers submitted. The adjusted estimates compare coding agent users to others, controlling for career stage, discipline, and the week they completed the survey. Coding agent users are starting more projects, posting more working papers, submitting more grants, and possibly sending out more conference submissions.

> So are coding agent users writing more papers? First, consider the differences in early pipeline output between coding agent users and others. Coding agent users are starting projects at a pace of around a quarter of a paper more and posting around a half of a working paper more than non agent users. In percentage terms, coding agent users look around 10% (empirical projects started) to 75% (working papers posted) more productive than others in their discipline and career stage.

> However, this productivity difference only appears for these early pipeline measures. We find no evidence that coding agent users are submitting more new papers to journals or resubmitting papers more quickly. This could reflect the timeline of getting a paper to submission, as coding agent use is a recent phenomenon. But it could also reflect that coding agents are more useful at getting projects up and running than they are at the last mile of perfecting a paper for journal submission.

**Annotation.**

- **The section-level downgrade is a preamble and it arrives before the figure.** Three sentences do it: the causal question is named and assigned elsewhere ("The experiment we are running on this question is still ongoing"); the design is labelled ("This comparison is purely descriptive"); and the inference is forbidden in the indicative ("Differences should not be interpreted as causal, but as a first cut comparison"). The middle sentence is the strongest of the three because it states *why* — "we compare researchers who select into coding agent use to those who do not, and expect that the two groups differ in a number of ways that we cannot adjust for" — naming selection, naming the direction of the problem, and conceding that the controls do not solve it. Same device as the April 2025 report's "we therefore treat these findings as more preliminary than the ones described above", with one improvement: **here the downgrade is upstream of the exhibit, so a reader who looks at the chart before reading has already been told how to read it.** Copy the ordering.
- **The heading contains the null.** "…but not submitting more to journals". The negative result is given equal billing with the positive one in the largest type available, which is the opposite of the usual practice of burying a null in the fourth paragraph. **A heading that states both the finding and its limit cannot be quoted without the limit.**
- **Both the level difference and the percentage difference are given, in that order, with the units named in words.** "starting projects at a pace of around a quarter of a paper more and posting around a half of a working paper more than non agent users", then "In percentage terms, coding agent users look around 10% (empirical projects started) to 75% (working papers posted) more productive than others in their discipline and career stage." The level comes first and is in the reader's units (papers, over the stated six months); the percentage comes second, is announced as a re-expression ("In percentage terms"), and each end of the range is labelled with the outcome it belongs to so the 75% cannot float free of "working papers posted". The comparison group is restated inside the percentage sentence ("than others in their discipline and career stage"). **This is the best-written quantitative passage on the page and the model to copy: level, then ratio, each labelled, comparison group inside the sentence, hedge on every number ("around", "look around").**
- **"look around 10% … more productive" hedges the verb, not just the number.** "look" rather than "are", inside a sentence that has already been fenced by the section preamble. Layered hedging of exactly the kind the corpus rewards — and note that the word "productive" is allowed only after "look", never as a bare predicate.
- **The one hedged item in a list of four is hedged in place.** "Coding agent users are starting more projects, posting more working papers, submitting more grants, and **possibly** sending out more conference submissions." Three unhedged, one hedged, the adverb sitting immediately before the item it qualifies rather than at the head of the list. A reader who quotes the list keeps the hedge attached to the right outcome. Compare the Discussion, which lists only the three unhedged outcomes and drops conference submissions entirely — the correct handling of a marginal result in a close.
- **The null is stated as absence of evidence, twice fenced, and given two readings that point in opposite directions.** "We find no evidence that coding agent users are submitting more new papers to journals or resubmitting papers more quickly." Then: "This could reflect the timeline of getting a paper to submission, as coding agent use is a recent phenomenon. **But it could also reflect** that coding agents are more useful at getting projects up and running than they are at the last mile of perfecting a paper for journal submission." One reading makes the null temporary and uninformative; the other makes it a substantive finding about where in the pipeline agents help. Neither is chosen. This is the February report's handling of its own null ("there wasn't evidence in this dataset of jobs being entirely automated") extended by a second, competing interpretation, and it is better for it — **a null with one explanation is an excuse; a null with two competing explanations is a finding about what the design cannot separate.** What is missing, and what a referee raises first, is the third thing our template requires: a minimum detectable effect beside the null. Nothing on the page says what difference in journal submissions this sample could have ruled out.
- **The internal comparison is named as a shape, not as an interaction.** "However, this productivity difference **only appears for these early pipeline measures**." The finding is the contrast between two families of outcomes — early pipeline versus late — and the sentence states it as a pattern across the six estimates rather than as a tested difference between them. No interaction test, no formal comparison of the two families. The writing device is worth copying (name the pattern across outcomes in one sentence before interpreting it); the statistic to support it is ours to add.
- **Rhetorical questions carry the section's structure.** "Are coding agents making researchers more productive?" opens it; "So are coding agent users writing more papers?" turns it; "First, consider the differences in early pipeline output…" walks the reader through. Three signposts in five paragraphs, all in the second person's cadence. Effective, and cheap to reproduce without first person: the questions are agentless already.
- **Register note, and the one sentence to avoid.** "the baseline survey lets us compare coding agent users to others across a whole bunch of checkpoints in the research process." A seminar phrase in the sentence that introduces the paper's central exhibit. It is disarming and it is also the sentence a reader will quote when arguing that the six outcomes were not specified in advance. **Informality is free in a sentence about the world and expensive in a sentence about the specification.**

### Finding 5 — beliefs, and the gap between them

Verbatim, the three paragraphs of "Researchers expect AI tools to raise productivity, but are less confident that they will improve social science overall" (Figure 6 and its caption sit between the second and third paragraphs):

> We also asked researchers what they expected of AI tools. Does AI make social scientists more productive, in terms of writing publishable papers? And do they think AI will make the social sciences better or worse?

> Researchers are optimistic about AI raising paper-writing productivity. On a 1 to 10 scale, 88% of respondents were above a 5, and half were at 8 or above. Figure 6 shows that these ratings vary strongly with AI use. The left side of the plot shows researchers that use AI for more types of tasks are more optimistic. The right side shows coding agent users are more optimistic than others.

▸ *(Figure 6 and its caption)*

> The survey is drawing from people who are interested in trying these tools out, so it should not be surprising to see some optimism about productivity. But even among these optimists, there is a real gap between views about AI helping narrowly with publishable papers and broadly affecting the social sciences. 70% of respondents are more optimistic about paper productivity than about broader field impact. There are few researchers more optimistic about field impacts than about paper productivity, and many who are more pessimistic.

**Annotation.**

- **How a survey finding about a level is phrased when the level cannot be believed.** The level is given ("On a 1 to 10 scale, 88% of respondents were above a 5, and half were at 8 or above") and then *immediately disqualified as a finding*: "The survey is drawing from people who are interested in trying these tools out, so it should not be surprising to see some optimism about productivity." The paragraph that follows the disqualification then builds the finding out of a *within-respondent difference* instead — "70% of respondents are more optimistic about paper productivity than about broader field impact" — which is invariant to the sample's overall optimism. **When a sample is selected on the attitude being measured, the level is not the finding and the within-person contrast is.** This is the most instructive methodological-writing move on the page and it generalises far beyond surveys: a selected sample can still support a comparison it cannot support a mean for.
- **The scale is named before either number.** "On a 1 to 10 scale" precedes 88% and "half", and the threshold used to dichotomise it is stated in the same breath ("above a 5", "at 8 or above"). Two thresholds on one scale, both arbitrary, both disclosed. Neither the mean nor the distribution is reported, so the reader cannot see whether "above a 5" is a high bar on this instrument.
- **Both question stems are quoted, in the caption rather than the body.** The body paraphrases them as two questions of its own ("Does AI make social scientists more productive, in terms of writing publishable papers? And do they think AI will make the social sciences better or worse?"); the Figure 6 caption then gives the items verbatim, with their slider labels. So the item text is on the page, in the place the figure can be lifted with. The paraphrase and the item differ in an instructive way — the paraphrase asks whether AI *makes* social scientists more productive, the item asks "do you think AI makes social scientists less or more productive" — and the caption is the version that can be checked.
- **The gradient results are stated as chart positions, with no numbers.** "The left side of the plot shows researchers that use AI for more types of tasks are more optimistic. The right side shows coding agent users are more optimistic than others." Direction only, panel by panel, values inside the image and therefore not recorded here. The construction is honest about what it is doing — describing a picture — and it is also the point at which a reader cannot check anything. **A relationship described by pointing at a panel is a relationship the reader takes on trust.** Ours state the two group means, or the slope with its interval, in the sentence.
- **"a real gap" is the interpretation and it precedes its number.** "But even among these optimists, there is a real gap between views about AI helping narrowly with publishable papers and broadly affecting the social sciences. 70% of respondents are…". Characterise, then quantify — the same ordering as the February report's "a slight lean towards augmentation" ahead of 57/43, and the April report's "dramatically higher automation rates" ahead of 79%.
- **The asymmetry is stated in words where a number would be better.** "There are few researchers more optimistic about field impacts than about paper productivity, and many who are more pessimistic." The 70% is one cell of a three-way split (more optimistic about papers / equal / more optimistic about the field) and the other two cells are given as "few" and implied by subtraction. **When a share is one cell of a partition, the partition goes in the sentence or in the table.**
- **Observed versus conjectured, and the conjecture is held back to the Discussion.** Nothing in this section explains the gap. The explanation — congestion, competition for attention, selective reporting — appears in the Discussion, marked twice ("suggestive evidence", "Perhaps … perhaps"). Separating a belief measurement from the story about why people hold the belief, by a section boundary, is the right discipline and the page keeps it.

**Naming discipline across all five findings, and why it is different here.** In the Economic Index reports, "AI" is the phenomenon and "Claude" is the instrument, so every measured sentence says Claude. On this page Claude is not the instrument — the instrument is a survey, and Claude Code is one of the *objects* respondents report using. The result is a three-way split that is consistent throughout:

- **"AI"** for the phenomenon, the debate, the future and the beliefs: "AI chatbots increasingly contribute to scientific research"; "as AI handles a broadening swath of research tasks"; "Researchers mainly use AI to code and edit"; "Do you think AI will make the social sciences worse or better".
- **"coding agents"** — a class, not a product — for everything measured: "20% of respondents use coding agents"; "coding agent users are starting more projects"; "regular AI coding agent users" (Figure 5). The paper's headline construct is deliberately generic, which is what lets a survey run by Anthropic report an adoption rate without reporting a Claude adoption rate.
- **"Claude Code"** only where a named tool is the object of the sentence: in the definitional gloss ("tools like Claude Code that autonomously write and execute analysis code"), in the question stem, in the tool-mix sentence ("86% of users reporting Claude Code use"), and in the description of the experiment ("providing researchers with access to Claude Code").

The discipline to carry forward: **when the product under study is the sponsor's own, the measured construct is named as a class and the product appears only where it is the object of a sentence.** The inverse of the Index convention, for the same reason — to keep the measurement separable from the sponsor.

## Comparisons

The paper is a set of two-group comparisons, all of them cross-sectional, and the page is explicit that one of them is the point ("a first cut comparison between researchers using coding agents and those who are not").

### Adopters against non-adopters — the spine

> This comparison is purely descriptive: we compare researchers who select into coding agent use to those who do not, and expect that the two groups differ in a number of ways that we cannot adjust for. Differences should not be interpreted as causal, but as a first cut comparison between researchers using coding agents and those who are not.

> Coding agent users are starting projects at a pace of around a quarter of a paper more and posting around a half of a working paper more than non agent users. In percentage terms, coding agent users look around 10% (empirical projects started) to 75% (working papers posted) more productive than others in their discipline and career stage.

**Annotation.** The comparison is defined, disqualified as causal, and only then quantified — three moves in that order, across two paragraphs and a figure. The definition names the assignment mechanism ("researchers who select into coding agent use"), which is what makes the disqualification legible: a reader who has been told the groups self-selected does not need to be told twice. Both levels are given in the units of the outcome and the adjustment set travels with the estimate ("than others in their discipline and career stage"). The one thing a reader cannot do is check the comparison: neither group's mean is published, so "a half of a working paper more" has no base, and the percentage range is the only clue that the working-paper base is small. **A difference reported against an unpublished base is a difference the reader cannot size** — our posts give the two levels and the difference.

### Adopters against other AI users — the use-case comparison

> But Figure 4 shows that the most common use, for both coding agent users and others, is for coding up analysis of quantitative data: 97% of coding agent users and 77% of other AI users report using it to generate code.

**Annotation.** The comparison's rhetorical work is to establish a *similarity*, not a gap: "for both coding agent users and others, is for". The two shares that follow are 20 points apart and the sentence deliberately does not make anything of it — the finding is the shared ranking, so the numbers are given as a pair and left unglossed. Note that this is the one comparison on the page where both levels are published, which is what allows it to be quoted safely. **Where a comparison establishes agreement rather than difference, both levels go in and no ratio is asserted.**

### By field

> Figure 1 shows there is large variation in the overall adoption rate, from 39% of economists and 25% of political scientists to single digits for public health (6%), education (4%) and communication (6%).

> This gradient roughly tracks differences across fields in overall AI use, but differences in coding agent adoption are steeper on average.

**Annotation.** A range stated by its two ends, with the top two named individually and the bottom three bracketed as "single digits" and then given anyway. Reading order is high-to-low, which puts economics first — the sponsoring discipline and the authors' own. Three of the five largest discipline groups (sociology, management sciences, psychology) get no rate anywhere in the prose, though all five are the "evenly split" bulk of the sample: **the fields that carry the gradient's story are quantified and the middle of the distribution is left in the figure.** The second sentence is a comparison of two gradients — adoption against overall AI use — expressed entirely in adverbs ("roughly tracks", "steeper on average") with no spread statistic for either. The claim is checkable only against the two panels of Figure 3, by eye.

### By seniority

> Just over a quarter of doctoral students and postdocs use coding agents at least weekly; among tenured professors that rate falls by more than half.

> Overall, early adoption of coding agents has tilted toward early career researchers, men, and those at higher status universities.

**Annotation.** The semicolon is doing the comparison: one clause per group, one level and one relative decline, no connective word. Efficient, and it costs the tenured level, which is never published. The grouping is also worth recording — "doctoral students and postdocs" against "tenured professors" is not the same partition as the sample description's "full or associate professors / assistant professors / doctoral students", and postdocs appear in neither the composition sentence nor the belief section. **A comparison whose groups are not the groups the sample was described in needs its groups defined where the comparison is made.**

### By gender

> There are sharp disparities in use of coding agents. Twice as many researchers with typically male names use coding agents as those with female names.

> We classify researcher names according to gender and find that those with typically male names have adopted coding agents at more than twice the rate of respondents with typically female names.

> The gender gap in coding agent use does not just reflect a gap in rates of trying AI. Among respondents who have tried using AI for research, there is even a slightly larger gender gap in regular coding agent use than in the overall sample. These differences also persist when comparing across genders in the same disciplines and career stages.

> Overall, early adoption of coding agents has tilted toward early career researchers, **men**, and those at higher status universities.

**Annotation.** Four statements of one comparison, and the construct changes across them. The Summary says "Twice as many"; the body says "more than twice the rate"; the robustness paragraph reports the gap twice more conditionally, without estimates; and the Discussion says "men", dropping the name-classification fence entirely. The ratio is never accompanied by either rate, in any of the four. So the paper's most quotable disparity — the one in the Summary's second sentence — is a ratio with no numerator, no denominator, no cell size, and a construct that is "typically male names" in three places and "men" in the fourth. Recorded as a writing fault worth avoiding absolutely: **a ratio is published with both of its rates, and a derived variable keeps its fence in every sentence including the close.** The robustness sequence itself is the part to copy — condition on the broader behaviour, then condition within cells, and say which direction the gap moves ("even a slightly larger") rather than merely that it survives.

### By university status

> Researchers at top universities are 40% more likely than others to use coding agents.

> High-status and private universities also see notably higher use.

> Top 25 universities are defined based on Nature Index Leading Institutions.

**Annotation.** The number is in the Summary, the word is in the body, the definition is in a caption, and the private/public half of the claim is quantified nowhere. Three locations for one comparison, and the only one a reader will quote is the one the body does not contain. This is the clearest instance in the corpus of the cost of a summary block: **a number that exists only in the summary has no section to be checked against.**

### Pilot against main sample — the selection check

> We fielded the survey in late February and March 2026, targeting active quantitative social scientists. This was not a representative sample—respondents were recruited for a study that offered access to Claude Max accounts, so selection into the sample could tilt toward researchers curious about AI tools. However, the respondents were fairly similar to an earlier sample that received a more generic invitation (see Table A2 in the Appendix).

**Annotation.** The structure is exemplary and the execution is delegated. In one sentence: the design flaw is named ("This was not a representative sample"), its *cause* is named ("recruited for a study that offered access to Claude Max accounts"), and the direction of the resulting bias is named ("could tilt toward researchers curious about AI tools"). The em dash rather than a new sentence keeps the cause inside the claim. Then "However," introduces the only evidence offered against it — a composition comparison with an earlier, generically-invited sample — and that comparison is a pointer to a table in another document. On the page itself, the entire selection defence is the adverb "fairly": no variable list, no test, no n for the earlier sample, no statement of which characteristics were compared. **A selection check is the one comparison a non-representative sample cannot delegate**, because it is the comparison that licenses everything else; in our posts it goes under the finding it protects, with its table in the drawer. The incentive that created the selection — free Claude Max accounts, offered by the firm that makes Claude Code, in a paper that reports 86% Claude Code use among adopters — is disclosed plainly here and never revisited.

### Early pipeline against late pipeline — the comparison across outcomes

> However, this productivity difference only appears for these early pipeline measures. We find no evidence that coding agent users are submitting more new papers to journals or resubmitting papers more quickly.

**Annotation.** The comparison is between two families of the paper's own outcomes, and it is what turns six coefficients into one finding: agents show up where projects start and not where papers finish. It is stated as a pattern ("only appears for these early pipeline measures"), interpreted twice in opposite directions in the next two sentences, and put in the heading. **One claim tested at more than one point in a process beats six claims at one point** — this section is the corpus's best illustration of that, and the improvement our house version needs is only the interaction test and the MDE.

### Within respondent — the belief gap

> But even among these optimists, there is a real gap between views about AI helping narrowly with publishable papers and broadly affecting the social sciences. 70% of respondents are more optimistic about paper productivity than about broader field impact.

**Annotation.** The only comparison on the page with no between-group component: each respondent is compared with themselves on two items, and the reported statistic is the share for whom the ordering runs one way. Because the contrast is within person, the sample's selection on AI interest cancels out of it — which is exactly the argument the preceding sentence makes ("even among these optimists"). **The comparison that survives a selected sample is the one taken inside the unit of observation**, and the page earns its most interesting finding that way after explicitly giving up on the level.

### Against the debate — the comparison that is the premise

> Debate about AI in academic research has focused heavily on writing: hallucinated literature reviews, "it's not X, it's Y" strewn across formulaic introductions, and the possibility of fully automated paper writing.

> But while turn-taking AI chatbots have primarily been used for writing assistance, coding agents could restructure social science research more radically.

**Annotation.** Two comparisons that are premises rather than findings: the public debate against what the data will show, and chatbots against agents. Both are stated in words, hyperlinked to their sources, and carry no number the paper would then have to own — the same discipline the April 2025 report used for its inherited over-representation premise. The chatbot/agent contrast is the load-bearing one and it is defined by capability, in a list of four verbs: "take a research idea and a dataset, write and run an analysis, interpret the output, and iterate autonomously". **A comparison that organises a whole post is defined before the first finding, by what each arm can do, not by how each arm scored.**

## Figure captions

Six numbered figures, each with a caption set below the image. The caption pattern on this page is **new to the corpus and the most structured so far**: a bold sentence that *is the finding*, prefixed "Figure N:", followed by one to four italic sentences of method — what is plotted, how the variable was measured, how to read the panels, what the error bars are. Both parts sit in one italic run in the source markup, with the title bolded inside it. The February 2025 report's captions were bold and unnumbered with no title clause; the April 2025 report's were italic and unnumbered and three of five never said what was plotted. Here every caption has a title and every caption has method.

Per `room/director-2026-09-16-alt-text-ruling.md` and `room/director-2026-09-16-caption-amendment.md`, the emphasised sentences under each image are the caption and are quoted as such. Per `room/director-2026-09-16-figure-values-ruling.md`, **no value has been read off any chart image and recorded in this file.**

### Figure 1 — adoption rate by discipline

> ***Figure 1: Economists and political scientists report the most use of coding agents.** Coding agent use is yes to "Do you regularly (more than once a week) use an AI coding assistant integrated into your command line (such as Codex, Cursor or Claude Code)?" and a follow-up question verifying use of one of those tools, or Google Antigravity. Discipline categories described in appendix.*

**Annotation.** The title states the finding as a ranking with no number — "report the most use", and "report" keeps the self-report fence in the largest text of the caption. Then the entire operationalisation: the item verbatim, the frequency threshold inside it, the verification step, and a pointer for the category definitions. **A caption that contains its variable's definition verbatim is liftable**: extract this image and a reader still knows precisely what the bars measure. Two things are missing and both matter — the n (of the figure and of any bar) and the unit of the axis, which is never named in the caption at all. One transcription detail worth recording: the item is quoted here as "Codex, Cursor or Claude Code" and in the body as "Codex, Cursor, or Claude Code". The same question stem, punctuated two ways, in two places on one page.

### Figure 2 — AI use and coding-agent use by career stage

> ***Figure 2: Adoption is highest among early-career researchers.** The blue series shows the percent reporting AI use to aid in research, by career stage. The orange series below shows the percent reporting regular coding agent use for research.*

**Annotation.** Title as finding; then one sentence per series, each naming its colour, its measure and its cut. The parts in order: colour → what is measured ("the percent reporting AI use to aid in research") → the cut ("by career stage"). Colour is the series key, as in the February report's orange/gray chart. "reporting" appears in both sentences, so the self-report fence is on both series. "below" is a positional cue that tells the reader the two series do not overlap — useful, and it makes the caption depend on the picture's layout. No unit statement beyond "the percent", no n, no career-stage categories enumerated (and they differ from the sample-description categories; see `## Comparisons`).

### Figure 3 — adoption gaps by group, two panels

> ***Figure 3: Adoption of coding agents shows sharp disparities.** Differences in the percent of researchers using AI (left panel) and coding agents (right panel) by career stage, sex, and university attributes. Male and female categories are measured through name classification. Top 25 universities are defined based on Nature Index Leading Institutions. All differences in adoption between groups are statistically significant with p < 0.05.*

**Annotation.** The longest caption and the one carrying the most method: what is plotted ("Differences in the percent of researchers using AI … and coding agents"), the panel layout with inline cues, the three cuts, then a definition for each derived variable, then the inference statement. Four sentences, four jobs, no wasted clause. Two notes. First, the caption is where the name-classifier and the Nature-Index definitions live — **a derived variable is defined in the caption of the figure that cuts by it**, which is the right instinct and is the practice our template already requires. Second, the variable is called "sex" in the caption and "gender" in the body ("We classify researcher names according to gender"), for one measure derived from names; a caption is not the place to introduce a second name for a construct. The significance sentence asserts p<0.05 for every contrast in both panels with no correction, no cell sizes and no estimates — the caption states a result it cannot show.

### Figure 4 — use cases, two panels

> ***Figure 4: The most common use cases are code and editing.** The left panel shows the share of users who indicated each specific use case, split by whether they use coding agents or only genAI for research. Respondents can indicate multiple categories (the question is limited to research use cases). The right panel shows these same responses split by discipline.*

**Annotation.** Title as finding, then the unit ("the share of users who indicated each specific use case"), then the split, then — the useful part — **the two properties of the item that tell the reader how to read the bars**: "Respondents can indicate multiple categories (the question is limited to research use cases)." The first clause says the shares need not sum to 100; the second says the universe is research use only, so a low prose-drafting share is not a claim about the respondent's writing in general. Both facts are stated nowhere else on the page. **When an item is multi-select or universe-restricted, the caption is the place that says so**, because the caption travels with the exhibit that would otherwise mislead. The groups are named as "coding agents or only genAI", a third labelling of the comparison the body calls "coding agent users and others" and "other AI users".

### Figure 5 — output differences

> ***Figure 5: Productivity differences in research output between regular AI coding agent users and other researchers.** Outcomes are self-reported by respondents. Working papers posted, journal submissions and journal resubmissions are mutually exclusive categories. Model is described in appendix. Whiskers show 95% confidence intervals calculated using the robust standard errors scaled by the outcome mean. * p < 0.05, ** p < 0.01.*

**Annotation.** The only caption whose title is *not* a finding — it is a description of the exhibit, in a paper whose other five titles assert. Given that this figure carries the headline and that the heading above it already asserts, the choice reads as deliberate: **the exhibit that must not be over-read gets the one neutral title on the page.** The method sentences are the densest in the corpus and each does a distinct job: provenance and its weakness in five words ("Outcomes are self-reported by respondents"); a partition warning that prevents double counting ("Working papers posted, journal submissions and journal resubmissions are mutually exclusive categories"); a pointer for the specification ("Model is described in appendix"); the interval, its estimator and its scaling ("95% confidence intervals calculated using the robust standard errors scaled by the outcome mean"); and the star convention. What is absent is the n, the outcome means the intervals are scaled by, and the specification itself. **A caption that scales its intervals by a quantity should print that quantity** — without the outcome mean, the percentage differences in the body cannot be reconciled with the levels in the chart.

### Figure 6 — beliefs

> ***Figure 6: Researcher beliefs about how AI will impact productivity narrowly compared to beliefs about how AI will impact the overall field of research.** Beliefs are measured on a 10-point slider scale. Productivity is: "In terms of writing publishable papers, do you think AI makes social scientists less or more productive" with the slider labeled "less productive to more productive." Field impact is: "Do you think AI will make the social sciences worse or better," with the slider labeled "Worse to better." Number of AI use cases adopted sums the categories listed in Figure 4.*

**Annotation.** The best caption on the page for a purpose our template cares about: it prints **both question stems verbatim and both slider labels**, so the instrument is on the page even though no questionnaire is. For attitude data that is the whole ballgame — the wording and the endpoint labels are the measurement — and putting them in the caption rather than in prose keeps the figure self-contained. The last sentence defines the x-axis variable by reference to another figure's categories ("sums the categories listed in Figure 4"), which is economical and makes this caption depend on one 600 words earlier, the same trade the April 2025 report's appendix caption made. The title is a comparison rather than a finding — "productivity narrowly compared to … the overall field" — and it names the contrast the section is built on.

### Alt text

Per the ruling, alt text is recorded and marked, never quoted as prose or caption. **The six figures have empty `alt` attributes**: in the fetched rendering each is `![](…)` with no alternative text at all. The only image alt strings on the page are the hero — "Coding agents in the social sciences", i.e. the title repeated — and the OG/Twitter share-card alt, "Chat bubble containing code".

**Alt-text numbers: none.** No number on this page appears only in alt text, so nothing in this file is marked `(alt text)`. The accessibility consequence is worth recording because it is also a style consequence: a reader using a screen reader gets the caption and nothing else, which on this page is enough for Figures 1–4 and 6 (the captions define every variable) and not enough for Figure 5 (whose title does not state the finding and whose method sentences do not give the estimates). **When figures carry no alt text, the caption is the only description that exists, and the caption's title has to carry the reading.**

**The caption pattern in summary.** Numbered, "Figure N:" prefix, bold title that states the finding, then italic method sentences in this order: what is plotted and in what unit → how the variable was measured, quoting the item where the variable is a survey response → how to read the panels or series, with colour or position as the key → definitions of derived variables → uncertainty and star conventions → pointers to the appendix for anything not stated. This is the closest thing in the corpus to the house template we want, and it improves on the February 2025 pattern in two ways: the title carries the finding, and survey items are quoted verbatim in the caption. It falls short in one way, consistently, across all six: **no figure states its n.**

## Limitations

There is no Limitations section and no Caveats heading. The qualification is real and heavy, and it is distributed across four layers. The Discussion's caveats paragraph is the nearest thing to a list, and it is quoted here in full first.

**Layer 1 — the caveats paragraph, third paragraph of the Discussion, verbatim:**

> There are several caveats to the findings in this report. The data presented here is based on an email survey of quantitative social scientists, recruited explicitly to participate in a study on workflows and AI use. We expect that the respondents are both heavier users and more optimistic about LLMs than non-responders. The early-stage productivity differences we see should be interpreted descriptively. The early adopters of coding agents may be more productive and otherwise different from non-adopters in many ways that we cannot measure directly in the survey. Finally, we only look here at the number of projects researchers report, and report nothing about their quality. In future updates on this study, we will show results comparing coding agent users to a clean comparison group, and assess whether the content, and not just quantity, of coding agent augmented work looks different.

**Layer 2 — the method-section concession, verbatim:**

> This was not a representative sample—respondents were recruited for a study that offered access to Claude Max accounts, so selection into the sample could tilt toward researchers curious about AI tools. However, the respondents were fairly similar to an earlier sample that received a more generic invitation (see Table A2 in the Appendix).

**Layer 3 — the finding-level downgrades, verbatim, in the order they appear:**

> Users of coding agents post more working papers and grant proposals than others in the same discipline and career stage, but this could reflect pre-existing differences among early adopters. *(Summary)*

> This comparison is purely descriptive: we compare researchers who select into coding agent use to those who do not, and expect that the two groups differ in a number of ways that we cannot adjust for. Differences should not be interpreted as causal, but as a first cut comparison between researchers using coding agents and those who are not. *(output section, before the figure)*

> This could reflect the timeline of getting a paper to submission, as coding agent use is a recent phenomenon. *(on the null)*

> The survey is drawing from people who are interested in trying these tools out, so it should not be surprising to see some optimism about productivity. *(belief section, on the level)*

> These differences are starker than the differences in overall AI use, and suggest higher inequality, at least in this early period of coding agent adoption. *(disparities section, on scope)*

> We also find suggestive evidence that researchers fear that the immediate benefits of rising paper productivity may come along with field-level costs. *(Discussion, on the belief finding)*

> The increased number of project starts may be early evidence of productivity increasing. It could also indicate that early adopters were already more productive researchers. *(Discussion, on the output finding)*

**Layer 4 — the number-level exclusion, in a footnote at the number, verbatim:**

> We launched the survey only a month after the release of Claude Cowork and before the release of OpenAI's Codex app, so we focused this question on Command Line Interface tools for interaction with coding agents. This means we do not count researchers who use AI agents exclusively via more general purpose desktop apps. *(Footnote 1, on the 20%)*

And one inside a caption:

> Outcomes are self-reported by respondents. *(Figure 5)*

**Annotation.**

- **Where it sits, and what the absence of a heading costs.** The caveats paragraph is the *third of four* paragraphs in the Discussion, between the distributional finding and the close, so the close is read through it — the same placement effect the February and April reports achieved with a heading. What the missing heading costs is navigability: a reader skimming the seven declarative H2s never sees the word "caveat", and the page's table of contents contains no signal that the central comparison is non-causal. The April 2025 report's promotion of Limitations to a top-level heading is the better practice and our template keeps it. **A caveat a skimmer cannot find is a caveat addressed to referees only.**
- **The allocation across the four layers is the same rule the corpus has been converging on, and this page follows it most strictly.** Design-level limits (frame, non-response, descriptive interpretation, unobservables, outcomes not measured) go in the Discussion paragraph; finding-level downgrades go at the head of, or inside, the finding they downgrade; number-level exclusions go in a footnote at the number; provenance goes in the caption of the exhibit. Nothing is in the wrong layer. Compare April 2025, which had the same three-layer structure but let a construct-validity problem threatening its headline sit in a bullet at the same rank as its retention window.
- **The framing sentence is minimal and the first substantive sentence is the frame.** "There are several caveats to the findings in this report." No "as with all studies", no re-assertion of the contribution, no "some of these include" — five words and then straight into the design: "The data presented here is based on an email survey of quantitative social scientists, recruited explicitly to participate in a study on workflows and AI use." Naming the recruitment *purpose* is what makes the next sentence's direction credible. **The shortest honest framing sentence is the best one; the concession itself should be the second sentence.**
- **Non-response is given a direction and no magnitude, on two dimensions at once.** "We expect that the respondents are both heavier users and more optimistic about LLMs than non-responders." Two signed biases, both pointing against the paper's own headline readings (adoption too high, optimism too high), stated in the indicative of expectation ("We expect"). A direction without a magnitude is what our template calls half a limitation; it is still worth far more than a bullet that says representativeness is "unclear", because it tells the reader which way to discount. **Every selection statement on this page has a direction. None has a bound.** No response rate, no invitation count, no weighting, no bounding exercise appears anywhere on the page.
- **The strongest sentence in the passage names what the design cannot see.** "The early adopters of coding agents may be more productive and otherwise different from non-adopters in many ways that we cannot measure directly in the survey." It concedes that the controls (career stage, discipline, survey week) do not address the identifying problem, and it says so in the vocabulary of the problem ("may be more productive"). Our equivalent has to add the thing this sentence does not: what the difference would have to be for the finding to be an artefact.
- **The limitation that withdraws a claim, and the one that names a non-finding.** "Finally, we only look here at the number of projects researchers report, and report nothing about their quality." Two withdrawals in one sentence — quality, and anything beyond self-reported counts — in the construction the February report used ("we don't argue that the uses in our dataset are a representative sample") and the April report used ("We only studied what developers delegate to AI—not…"). **The corpus is unanimous: a limitation is worth most when it names the sentence the piece is not entitled to write.** Note also the recursion in "the number of projects researchers **report**": the measure is fenced inside the sentence that limits it.
- **The passage discharges itself into commitments, in the same paragraph.** "In future updates on this study, we will show results comparing coding agent users to a clean comparison group, and assess whether the content, and not just quantity, of coding agent augmented work looks different." Two limitations, two named remedies, in the sentence that ends the caveats. This is the best-integrated version of the April 2025 report's bullet 2 ("We will likely need to extend the automation/augmentation framework"): the caveats paragraph is also the what-comes-next paragraph, so conceding a weakness and promising the fix are one move. Copy the join.
- **What a referee would raise first, in order.** (1) No minimum detectable effect beside the paper's central null — "We find no evidence that coding agent users are submitting more new papers to journals" rests on roughly a fifth of 1,260 respondents and low-count outcomes, and nothing states what the design could have excluded. (2) No n anywhere below the full 1,260 — not per discipline, career stage, gender, university group or figure — while a caption asserts p<0.05 for every contrast in two panels. (3) No multiple-comparison discipline, and no statement of whether the six output measures or the group contrasts were specified before the data were seen. (4) The two rates behind the two headline ratios ("twice as many", "40% more likely") are never published. (5) The adoption measure and the outcome window overlap, so the pre-existing-productivity story the authors themselves name is unbounded — a retrospective pre-period count was available to a survey. (6) The name classifier is unnamed and unvalidated, with no ambiguous or unclassified category disclosed, and the construct becomes "men" in the Discussion. None of these is hidden by the page; all six are consequences of what it does not report.
- **What the page qualifies that a weaker piece would not.** The level of optimism (disqualified in the sentence after it is given); the direction of both selection problems; the exclusion created by its own questionnaire design; the dependence of the headline on an appendix specification; and the difference between "starting more projects" and "being more productive". Five qualifications that change what the reader may conclude, none of them in a section headed Limitations.

## Close

Verbatim, the Discussion's first, second and fourth paragraphs — the restatement, the distributional finding with its conjectures, and the last paragraph. (The third paragraph is the caveats passage, quoted in full under `## Limitations`.)

> ## Discussion

> Social scientists who are using coding agents are posting more working papers and applying for more grants. Relative to others in their discipline and career stage, they are also starting more projects. But as of March 2026, they are not yet driving a surge in journal submissions. The increased number of project starts may be early evidence of productivity increasing. It could also indicate that early adopters were already more productive researchers.

> Overall, early adoption of coding agents has tilted toward early career researchers, men, and those at higher status universities. Coding agent use is currently more unevenly distributed across these categories than LLM use more broadly. We also find suggestive evidence that researchers fear that the immediate benefits of rising paper productivity may come along with field-level costs. Perhaps more papers means congestion and competition for attention; perhaps respondents fear that some researchers will use AI tools in ways that exacerbate existing problems in social science, like selective reporting and risk-averse, incremental research.

> *(caveats paragraph — see `## Limitations`)*

> Notwithstanding these limitations, we show that coding agents are diffusing into the social sciences. The way we study the economy and politics, for example, is increasingly through analysis decisions made in part by AI coding agents. We plan to bring more evidence in future reports on the potential and risks of this kind of automation.

**Annotation.**

- **What was learned, restated with no numbers and with the comparison group intact.** Four findings in three sentences, and every one of them keeps its fence: "posting more working papers and applying for more grants" (no magnitude), "Relative to others in their discipline and career stage" (the adjustment set, restated in the close), "as of March 2026" (a date stamp on a null), "not yet driving a surge" (the null in the reader's vocabulary). **The close states what was learned in words and leaves the numbers in the findings** — the corpus is unanimous on this across three very different pieces. Note what is dropped: conference submissions, the one outcome the body hedged with "possibly", does not appear. A close that drops its weakest result rather than repeating the hedge is making the right trade.
- **The two readings are restated in the close, not resolved there.** "The increased number of project starts may be early evidence of productivity increasing. It could also indicate that early adopters were already more productive researchers." Two sentences, two modals, no adjudication, immediately after the findings. The February report's close extrapolated conditionally from its findings; this one refuses to extrapolate at all until the experiment reports. **A baseline-wave close names both readings of its own headline and leaves them open, because the design that separates them is the piece that comes next.**
- **The distributional finding gets the close's only unhedged claim, and its construct has drifted.** "Overall, early adoption of coding agents has tilted toward early career researchers, men, and those at higher status universities." No modal, and "men" where every measured sentence said "typically male names". One word, and it converts a name-classifier output into a demographic fact. This is the single clearest lesson the page offers about closes: **the close is where fences get dropped, so the close is where they have to be checked.** Our rule — every quantitative sentence maps to an entry in `results.json`, and the same entry is quoted the same way everywhere — has to extend to the *construct names* in the close, not only the numbers.
- **The conjectures are marked twice and given the sentence-shape of conjecture.** "We also find **suggestive evidence** that researchers fear that the immediate benefits of rising paper productivity **may** come along with field-level costs. **Perhaps** more papers means congestion and competition for attention; **perhaps** respondents fear that some researchers will use AI tools in ways that exacerbate existing problems in social science, like selective reporting and risk-averse, incremental research." Anaphoric "Perhaps … perhaps", two candidate mechanisms, no choice between them, and both named concretely enough to be tested later (congestion and attention; selective reporting and incrementalism). The April 2025 report quarantined its most dramatic conjecture in its own paragraph; this one quarantines its conjectures in the *second half* of a paragraph whose first half is observation, with the marker words carrying the boundary. Slightly weaker separation, equally clear marking.
- **Why it matters is the last paragraph, and it is a claim about the reader's own field asserted past the caveats.** "**Notwithstanding these limitations**, we show that coding agents are diffusing into the social sciences. The way we study the economy and politics, for example, is increasingly through analysis decisions made in part by AI coding agents." The concession comes first, in a prepositional phrase, and the claim follows in the indicative — the same front-loaded-hedge architecture as the April 2025 report's final sentence, but with the modal removed. That removal is the cost: "diffusing" and "increasingly" are both statements about change over time, and the page has one cross-section. The paper's own caveats paragraph does not cover this; the phrase "Notwithstanding these limitations" is asked to. **A close may assert past its caveats only in the tense its design supports** — with one wave, the available sentence is about level and composition, not about diffusion.
- **What comes next, split across two paragraphs, and only one half is a commitment.** The caveats paragraph carries the specific promises — a clean comparison group, and content rather than quantity. The last sentence carries the general one: "We plan to bring more evidence in future reports on the potential and risks of this kind of automation." The specific promise is checkable and the general one is not, and putting the checkable one inside the caveats is what makes the caveats feel like a plan rather than a disclaimer. Note the symmetry of "the potential and risks", which matches the opening's two-sided conjecture exactly.
- **Title against ending.** Title: "Coding agents in the social sciences" — a subject, a preposition and a population, with no finding and no verb. Ending: "coding agents are diffusing into the social sciences". The close takes the title's noun phrase and supplies its missing verb, which is as tight a match as the corpus contains — and it is why the title can survive a post with five findings none of which it names. The opening question ("How are AI coding agents changing how we study the economy and society?") is answered by the penultimate sentence ("The way we study the economy and politics, for example, is increasingly through analysis decisions made in part by AI coding agents"), in the same vocabulary, with "changing" answered by "increasingly". **Three points — title, opening question, last two sentences — written to the same words.** For a post whose title names a subject rather than a finding, this is the pattern: the close conjugates the title.
- **No recommendation to anyone.** No policy sentence, no advice to researchers, journals, funders or universities, and no refusal to give any — the February report at least declined explicitly ("our research gives data on how AI is being used, but it doesn't provide policy prescriptions"). The disparities finding is the most obviously actionable result in the corpus and the close draws nothing from it. Our template requires recommendations; the nearest model remains February's explicit refusal plus displaced invitation.
- **First person, and the two things it does here.** Institutional commitment ("We plan to bring more evidence", "we will show results"), which the February report also did; and *disciplinary membership* ("The way **we** study the economy and politics"). The second is not available to us and is not merely a matter of de-personalising: the sentence's force comes from the authors counting themselves among the affected. The substitute is to name the population and let the finding carry the exposure — "the way quantitative social scientists study the economy…" — which is weaker, and knowing that it is weaker is the point of recording it.
- **A missing summary block, and why the close does not need one.** The page opens with five summary bullets and closes without repeating any of them numerically. If the Summary were removed, the Discussion's first two paragraphs would serve as the reader's takeaway, in words, with the fences on. That is the test our template implies: **if the close can stand as the summary, the summary block was never load-bearing** — and on this page, three of its five bullets drifted from the body precisely because it was not.
