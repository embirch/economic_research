## Section order

Headings in PDF order, with what each does and roughly how long it runs.

1. **Title page (p. 1).** Title, "Published / June 16, 2026", the five authors, and the acknowledgements paragraph. No abstract, no summary, no figure, no contents.

This is the summary block the house template forbids, and it drifts from the body in two specific ways recorded in `## Opening move`.

**Where the findings sit.** Three times, at three depths: the Key findings bullets, the section prose with its figure, and "Looking ahead". Unlike the May 2026 social-sciences post, where five of seven headings were the finding, **only two headings here state a result** — "4. Success depends on what the people brings" and "4.2 Occupation may matter less than expertise", both in the last substantive section — and both are hedged claims rather than numbers ("depends on", "may matter less"). The other eleven name a topic or a population: "Who decides what", "Level of expertise", "The users", "The work". A reader who reads only the contents gets the framework and one conclusion, not the results — which is consistent with a paper whose stated contribution is a framework ("we introduce a framework for studying interactive agentic coding") rather than a number. **Where a heading does assert here, it asserts a relation and not a magnitude**, which is the form that survives a reader who never reaches the section.

**Where the method sits.** Distributed, and always immediately before the number it licenses: the work-mode classifier opens §2.1, the decision-attribution classifier sits inside §2.2, the expertise rubric is §2.3's first half plus Table 1, the occupation classifier opens §3.1, the task-value estimator is introduced in the sentence before its result, and the entire success apparatus is the untitled opener of §4. There is no methods section and no methods appendix pointer in a single place; there are six pointers to the appendix at six different points. **The consequence for the reader is that every number arrives with its instrument attached, and no number can be quoted from this paper without passing the paragraph that defines it.** This is the single most transferable structural fact about the piece, and it is the architecture our template's "findings in the order of the pre-registration, each with its figure" is trying to buy.

**Where the caveats sit.** Four layers, no heading: ten footnotes, in-sentence fences, the rival-explanation paragraph at the head of §4.1, and the "These findings are preliminary" paragraph inside "Looking ahead". Allocation annotated in `## Limitations`.

**Where the exhibits sit, and a PDF-only artefact.** Figures and tables float to the top or bottom of the nearest page, and three of them interrupt a sentence: Table 1 lands between "An accountant who has never" (p. 6) and "used Python…" (p. 7); Table 2 lands between "A novice-rated session" (p. 11) and "reaches our strictest measure…" (p. 12); Figure 3, which belongs to §2.3, appears on p. 9, after the §3.1 heading. The web version, which places every image at a paragraph boundary, has none of this. Recorded because the corpus's other PDFs do not do it and because it is the one respect in which the web reprint reads better than the document of record.

## Opening move

The paper opens twice, and the two openings are written to different rules.

**Key findings, verbatim, all four bullets:**

> **Key findings**
>
> - Building on prior work, we introduce a framework for studying interactive agentic coding based on a privacy-preserving analysis of ~400,000 Claude Code sessions from between October 2025 and April 2026. We evaluate the composition of tasks, human-AI collaboration, and success rates.
> - In a typical session, people make most of the planning decisions (what to do) and Claude makes most of the execution decisions (how to do it). The greater domain expertise a person brings to a session, the more work Claude does per instruction. On coding tasks, every major occupation succeeds—accomplishes what the person set out to do, with verifiable evidence like passing tests or committed work—at nearly the same rate as software engineers, on average.
> - The more domain expertise a person has, the more often the session ends in success—though the gap between intermediate and expert users is modest. Over the seven months we observe, the share of sessions spent debugging fell by nearly half, and usage shifted toward more end-to-end agentic use: deploying and running code, analyzing data, and writing non-code documents.
> - Over those seven months, the value of the typical task, which we estimate through a comparison to freelance job postings, rose in almost every kind of work, and about 25% on average.

**§1 Introduction, verbatim, all five paragraphs** (footnote markers shown as raised digits in the source are given here as trailing digits, as the extracted text renders them):

> **1. Introduction**
>
> Agentic coding has taken off. The share of Github projects with coding agent activity has more than doubled since late 2025,1 and Claude Code users now spend an average of 20 hours per week using the tool.2 Can people without formal coding experience successfully direct an agent through complex technical work? And what will rapid adoption and improvement of these tools mean for knowledge work broadly? While we don't have full answers to these questions yet, we look to Claude Code usage data for early signals.

> This report provides evidence on how Claude Code is used in practice, based on a privacy-preserving analysis of ~400,000 interactive sessions from ~235,000 people between October 2025 and April 2026. It builds on prior work focused on measures of autonomy in Claude Code sessions, and how Claude Code is changing work at Anthropic.3 Here, we introduce a framework for describing interactive AI coding-assistant usage: what kind of work is being done, who is doing it, and whether it succeeds. We focus on Claude Code usage through a command-line interface (CLI), claude.ai, or the Claude Code desktop app.4 By tracking how agentic coding usage changes as models get more capable, we can better understand how these tools affect the labor market for coding professionals and knowledge workers.

> What happens on Claude Code may be a preview of where knowledge work is headed, as agents become embedded in non-coding work. We find that Claude is handling more complex and more valuable tasks. At the same time, there remains a clear division of labor in agentic coding: People decide what to build, and the agent decides how to build it.

> We also see evidence that domain expertise, and not coding proficiency, amplifies effective use of the tool. In particular, domain experts, succeed more often, and more easily recover from errors and misunderstandings. However, the gap between experts and intermediates is modest—suggesting that proficiency in a domain is enough to use the tool almost as effectively as those with deep mastery.

> These findings give us an early read on possible transitions in the labor market. In our data, success is determined by how well a person understands the problem they are trying to solve, not whether they're trained in coding. If these patterns hold across the economy, it suggests that while agentic coding tools may be absorbing some implementation-heavy work, they are also rewarding those with firm understanding of the problems they solve on the job. Coding agents are not substituting for domain expertise—the more understanding a worker brings to an agent, the more quality work the agent is able to do.

**Annotation.**

- **The first sentence is four words and it is a claim about the world, not about the paper.** "Agentic coding has taken off." No hedge, no citation on the sentence itself, no "we find". The two sentences that follow supply the evidence for it — one external ("The share of Github projects with coding agent activity has more than doubled since late 2025") and one internal ("Claude Code users now spend an average of 20 hours per week using the tool") — and both carry footnotes that undercut them: footnote 1 says the external detection method "likely undercount[s] actual usage", footnote 2 says the 20 hours "measures hours in which Claude Code was actively running, not the user's hands-on time typing to Claude". **A flat opening assertion, two numbers that size it, and a footnote on each that says what the number is not.** That is the opening move to copy, and it costs three sentences.
- **Why it matters arrives as two questions, in the reader's terms, and neither is the paper's own question.** "Can people without formal coding experience successfully direct an agent through complex technical work? And what will rapid adoption and improvement of these tools mean for knowledge work broadly?" The first is answerable from the data; the second is not, and the very next sentence concedes it: "While we don't have full answers to these questions yet, we look to Claude Code usage data for early signals." **Posing a question larger than the design and disqualifying it in the same paragraph is what buys the paper a labour-market framing on product telemetry.** Compare the May 2026 post, which asked "How are AI coding agents changing how we study the economy and society?" in the largest type on the page and conceded the scale only in the body. Here the concession is adjacent to the question and in the same paragraph; that is the better version.
- **The sample is a sentence, not a section, and it names both units.** "~400,000 interactive sessions from ~235,000 people between October 2025 and April 2026." Sessions and people, both approximate, with the window. The word "interactive" is doing exclusion work that only footnote 4 discharges. **Two denominators and a window in one clause is the minimum for a telemetry paper, and it is all the front matter this one spends.**
- **The contribution is named as a framework, twice, before any result.** "we introduce a framework for studying interactive agentic coding" (bullet 1); "we introduce a framework for describing interactive AI coding-assistant usage: what kind of work is being done, who is doing it, and whether it succeeds" (§1). The colon-plus-three-clauses is the paper's section list — §2 what work, §3 who, §4 whether it succeeds — so the reader is given the contents page inside a sentence. Same device as the May 2026 post's semicolon list; here it also doubles as the claim to novelty.
- **Three paragraphs of findings with no numbers at all.** Paragraphs 3, 4 and 5 state every one of the paper's results — more valuable tasks, the division of labour, the expertise gradient, the modest expert–intermediate gap, the occupation result — and contain not one figure. The numbers are held for the sections. **The introduction states findings in words; the body states them in numbers; the close states them in words again.** The corpus is unanimous on the close; this is the first piece in it that applies the same rule to the opening, and it is why the introduction reads as an argument rather than as an abstract.
- **The slogan, and the one place it is not held.** "People decide what to build, and the agent decides how to build it." That exact sentence appears twice: in §1 p. 3, after a colon, and again in §2.2 p. 6, after an em dash, immediately after the 70/20 result. Two placements, one wording, and it is the paper's most quotable line. But the chart subtitle set inside Figure 2 — the one version that will travel on a slide without the paper around it — is **not** the same sentence: it reads "People decide what to do, Claude decides how to do it" Three substitutions in eleven words: "build" becomes "do" twice, and "the agent" becomes "Claude". The second is the consequential one, because the whole naming ladder annotated at the end of `## Findings and their caveats` depends on the class word carrying the generalising claim and the product name carrying only the measured one — and here the exhibit puts the product name into the sentence that is doing the generalising. The Key findings bullet is a fourth wording again ("people make most of the planning decisions (what to do) and Claude makes most of the execution decisions (how to do it)"), and Figure 2's caption is a fifth, stated as 70/80 rather than 70/20. **A finding that has to survive being quoted is written once and repeated verbatim, and the exhibit is the copy most likely to be quoted** — the discipline the May 2026 post failed when its summary said "Twice as many" and its body said "more than twice", failed here in the harder place, where the divergence is invisible to anyone reading the prose straight through.
- **Why it matters, in its final form, is conditional and it is about wages without saying wages.** "If these patterns hold across the economy, it suggests that while agentic coding tools may be absorbing some implementation-heavy work, they are also rewarding those with firm understanding of the problems they solve on the job." The antecedent ("If these patterns hold across the economy") is the whole external-validity concession, compressed into five words at the front of the sentence; "rewarding" is the only word in the paper that gestures at prices. Then the last sentence drops the conditional entirely: "Coding agents are not substituting for domain expertise—the more understanding a worker brings to an agent, the more quality work the agent is able to do." A flat negative claim about substitution, in the present tense, from seven months of one product's transcripts. **The introduction's last sentence is the most over-reaching sentence in the paper**, and it is worth recording precisely because everything before it is careful: the conditional was two sentences earlier and is not repeated.
- **What the summary block costs here.** Two drifts, both small and both of the kind our template exists to prevent.
  - **A number.** Bullet 4: "about 25% on average". Body, p. 10: "the estimated value of the average session rose by 27% between October and April." Two values for one quantity, three sentences apart in the same document, never reconciled, both reproduced identically in the web version. The bullet also says "the typical task" where the body says "the average session" — so it is not certain the two are even the same estimand, which is worse than a rounding disagreement.
  - **A construct.** Bullet 3: "the share of sessions spent debugging fell by nearly half". The body's measure is the work mode "fixing something broken", and the word "debugging" appears nowhere else in the paper. A summary that renames the measured category is a summary that cannot be checked against the figure.
  - Bullet 2 also compresses the occupation result to "every major occupation succeeds … at nearly the same rate as software engineers, on average", where the body reports a five-point gap on the strict measure and "within seven points" across ten groups. Nothing false; but "nearly the same rate" is the reader's takeaway and the seven points are the paper's.
- **The one thing the bullets do better than the body.** Bullet 2 defines verified success inline, in an em-dashed aside, at first use: "every major occupation succeeds—accomplishes what the person set out to do, with verifiable evidence like passing tests or committed work—at nearly the same rate as software engineers". A reader who never reaches §4 still knows what "succeeds" was made to mean. **A summary bullet that carries the definition of its own verb is the only kind worth writing** — and our template's substitute is to put that definition in the finding's first sentence.

## Findings and their caveats

The paper has one grammar for a finding and it holds almost everywhere: **the instrument is described in the preceding sentence or paragraph, the number is a share of *sessions*, and the unit is named inside the sentence.** The word "session" appears about 110 times in 18 pages; "AI" appears exactly twice. Where the grammar breaks — where a session-level rating becomes a property of a person — it breaks in the same three places every time: the Key findings, the Introduction, and the close. That is the single lesson this paper contributes to the corpus and it is annotated at each occurrence.

### Finding 1 — what the work is (§2.1)

> To understand what people are using Claude Code for, we classify each session into one of nine work modes—the single activity that best describes what the session is trying to accomplish.5 Four modes involve writing or maintaining code directly: building something new, fixing something broken, testing code, and orchestrating other agents or automated pipelines. Another category is operating software—deploying, configuring, running pipelines, monitoring systems. Two categories are more about working out what to do: understanding how an existing system works, and planning a change before making it. And two take actions unrelated to code, or where code is incidental to the final product: analyzing data, and communicating via presentations and other prose-based documents.

> About 56% of sessions consist of writing (25%) fixing (26%), or testing and orchestrating code 5%. Operating software comprises 17%, while 14% of sessions are planning or exploring, and 13% produce analysis or prose (Figure 1).

> We classify each session by having a model read its transcript, then using our privacy-preserving analysis tool, we check them against telemetry that's recorded automatically for every session, including whether any lines of code were added or deleted. The two sources have high agreement for instance, more than 90% of sessions our classifier labeled as creating or modifying code showed code changes in the telemetry. See the Appendix for details.

**Annotation.**

- **The taxonomy is defined by grouping before it is counted.** Nine modes, presented as four families with the logic of each family stated ("involve writing or maintaining code directly"; "more about working out what to do"; "unrelated to code, or where code is incidental"). The shares that follow are then reported *by family*, not mode by mode, while Figure 1 gives all nine. **The prose carries the grouping that makes the argument; the figure carries the distribution.** Our version puts the nine in a table in the drawer and the four in the sentence.
- **The single-label rule is stated in the definition, not in a caveat.** "the single activity that best describes what the session is trying to accomplish", repeated verbatim in Figure 1's caption. The reader is told once, in the place they will look, that a mixed session has been forced into one bin. What is never given is how often that forcing is close-run — no second-label share, no classifier confidence. **Stating a simplification in the definition is necessary and is not the same as bounding it.**
- **The aggregate is given before its parts, and the parts are parenthetical.** "About 56% of sessions consist of writing (25%) fixing (26%), or testing and orchestrating code 5%." The construct the argument needs is the 56%; the components are supplied so the reader can check it. This is the right order and it survives the PDF's missing comma and missing parentheses, which the web repairs.
- **The validation paragraph is placed after the first finding that depends on it and before the second.** Not in an appendix, not in a footnote: in the body, one paragraph, naming the independent source (telemetry recorded automatically), the comparison, and the number. The sentence is honest about what kind of agreement it is — "more than 90% of sessions **our classifier labeled as creating or modifying code** showed code changes in the telemetry" — the direction is inside the clause. **A validation sentence that names its direction cannot be over-quoted**; a reader can see it is a one-way check on one label family, and the converse is not claimed. Nothing analogous is offered in the main text for the expertise, occupation, decision-attribution or outcome classifiers, each of which is pointed at the appendix.
- **The PDF-only paragraph.** "Most sessions are anchored to an existing codebase: 48% primarily modify existing code and another 17% explore code, while 14% create new code from scratch. Roughly a fifth of sessions touch no codebase at all." A second, unnamed classification, with four shares, no figure, no validation and no further use anywhere in the paper — and absent from the web version. Two writing notes: a classification introduced for one paragraph and never crossed with anything else is a paragraph the piece could lose; and **a finding that exists in only one of a document's two published versions cannot be cited without saying which version it is from.**

### Finding 2 — who decides what (§2.2)

> We separate these decisions into planning (what to do, which approach to take, what counts as done) and execution (which files to change, what code to write, what language to write in, which commands to run). The classifier then attributes each decision to Claude or to the user, giving every session two numbers: the user's share of planning decisions and the user's share of execution decisions.

> On average, people make about 70% of the planning decisions but only 20% of the execution decisions (Figure 2). In practice, there is a clear division of labor in agentic coding—people decide what to build, and the agent decides how to build it.

> In a typical session, there are about 4 such turns. In our historical data from October to April, each prompt the user sends sets off a chain of around 10 actions taken by Claude on average—and sometimes over a hundred.6 In each turn, Claude reads files, edits code, runs commands, and writes on average 2,400 words of output.

> How much Claude does between check-ins largely tracks who is making the decisions. When the user keeps control of execution (i.e., makes over 80% of execution decisions), Claude takes fewer actions per turn (about 8 actions). And when Claude takes control of planning (i.e., makes over 80% of planning decisions), it takes on the highest number of actions (about 16).

**Annotation.**

- **Both halves of the construct are defined by enumeration, in parentheses, in the sentence that names them.** "planning (what to do, which approach to take, what counts as done)" and "execution (which files to change, what code to write, what language to write in, which commands to run)". Four examples for the concrete half, three for the abstract half, no abstract definition of either. **A derived category defined by its instances is checkable by a reader who has never seen the classifier prompt.** This is the best definitional sentence in the paper and the cheapest to imitate.
- **The output of the measure is stated as a data structure.** "giving every session two numbers: the user's share of planning decisions and the user's share of execution decisions." Naming what the classifier returns, per unit, before reporting anything, tells the reader exactly what the figure can and cannot show.
- **The interpretation is a separate sentence with no number in it.** "In practice, there is a clear division of labor in agentic coding—people decide what to build, and the agent decides how to build it." Measurement sentence, then meaning sentence, in that order, with the numbers only in the first. This is the corpus's standard practice and it is observed strictly through §2 and §3, less strictly in §4.
- **A mean reported as a typical case, and the caption says it the other way round.** Body: "On average, people make about 70% of the planning decisions but only 20% of the execution decisions (Figure 2)." Caption: "In the typical session, the user makes about 70% of planning decisions while Claude makes about 80% of execution decisions." Same fact, two arithmetic complements, two different central-tendency words ("On average" / "In the typical session"), and two different subjects (the user's share in the body, Claude's share in the caption title). The caption's own first sentence says the figure is a "Distribution across sessions", and no dispersion statistic appears anywhere in words. **When the exhibit is a distribution, the sentence that quotes one number from it says which number it is** — mean, median or modal bin — and this paper does not.
- **The tail is quantified in a footnote and nowhere else.** Body: "around 10 actions taken by Claude on average—and sometimes over a hundred." Footnote 6: "The tail of actions per prompt is long. About 2% of sessions average more than 100 actions per prompt, about 1 in 270 average more than 200, and about 1 in 2,300 average more than 500." The body gives a central value and a gesture; the footnote gives three quantiles in two notations. Correct allocation — the reader who wants the shape can have it, the reader who wants the fact is not detained — and it is the same architecture the April 2025 report used for its measurement exclusions.
- **The unit slips between paragraphs, three ways.** "actions per prompt" (body, §2.2 and §2.3), "actions per turn" (body, §2.2 and Figure 3's legend) and "per prompt" again in Figure 3's caption, with "2,400 words of output" given per *turn* and "600 words of output" given per *prompt* one page later. A turn and a prompt stand in one-to-one correspondence by the paper's own description of a session, which is why nobody caught it; a reader comparing the two numbers cannot know that. **One quantity, one name, everywhere including the legend.**
- **The threshold contrast is defined inside the sentence that uses it.** "When the user keeps control of execution (i.e., makes over 80% of execution decisions)…" — the plain-language phrase and its operational definition in the same breath, twice, for two different groups. No table, no cell sizes, no test, and the two groups are not exhaustive; the sentence claims only that the extremes differ, and says so with "largely tracks".

### Finding 3 — the expertise rating and what Claude does with it (§2.3)

> From each transcript, Claude rates the user's apparent expertise at the task on a five-point scale from novice to expert. The expertise classifier looks for three signals: how precisely the user frames their directions, what they ask Claude to verify, and whether the user tends to correct Claude or Claude tends to correct the user. Note that expertise is capturing something quite different from job title or general ability, and, crucially, it is task-specific. A senior engineer asking their first Rust question is a beginner at Rust. An accountant who has never used Python, but tells Claude exactly which reconciliation rules a Python script must enforce and catches the edge case it mishandles at month-end close, is an expert at that task.

> We quantify how expertise relates to Claude's output and activity per prompt. In typical novice sessions, each prompt sets off about 5 Claude actions and roughly 600 words of output, while expert sessions set off action chains more than twice as long (12 actions) carrying five times the output (3200 words) (Figure 3). This gap between novice and expert sessions appears within every kind of work and every band of task value.

**Annotation.**

- **The construct is fenced by two counterexamples rather than by a caveat.** "A senior engineer asking their first Rust question is a beginner at Rust. An accountant who has never used Python, but tells Claude exactly which reconciliation rules a Python script must enforce … is an expert at that task." Two sentences, two people who would be misclassified by the reading the term invites, one in each direction. A caveat sentence ("note that this is not general ability") would have been ignored; these will not be. **Define a derived construct by the two cases the reader would otherwise get wrong** — this is the most copyable paragraph in the paper and it costs forty words.
- **"apparent" and "at the task" are load-bearing and they are in the first sentence.** "Claude rates the user's **apparent** expertise **at the task**". Both fences are set before the construct has a name. They survive into the figure captions ("the user's rated expertise at the task") and into the findings ("A novice-rated session", "sessions rated expert", "sessions where the user appears to be a novice") — and they are dropped in the Key findings ("The more domain expertise a person has"), the Introduction ("domain experts, succeed more often") and the close ("domain experts are able to direct Claude to do more work"). Figure 3's title drops them too: "Claude does more per prompt for more expert **users**". **The fence on a derived variable has to survive into the summary, the caption and the close, or three of the four places a reader will quote from are claiming a different variable.** The May 2026 post did this once, with "typically male names" becoming "men"; this paper does it with a session-level rating becoming a person-level trait, in every non-findings section.
- **The unadjusted contrast is in the prose and the adjusted one is in the caption.** Prose: "about 5 Claude actions and roughly 600 words of output" against "more than twice as long (12 actions) carrying five times the output (3200 words)". Caption: "they remain significant (at +9% actions and +13% output per expertise level) in a regression controlling for work mode, task value, month, occupation, and model family, with standard errors clustered by user." Two very different quantities for one relationship, split across two registers, and the page never says they are different quantities or that the second is the defensible one. **The estimate that survives the controls belongs in the sentence that makes the claim; the raw contrast is the illustration, not the finding.** Note also that both prose numbers are geometric means (stated only in the caption, as "White dots are geometric means") — a reader who takes them for arithmetic means will mis-compare them with the 2,400-word figure a page earlier.
- **The heterogeneity check is one sentence with no evidence and it is the right sentence.** "This gap between novice and expert sessions appears within every kind of work and every band of task value." A universal claim over two partitions, unquantified, immediately after the result it protects. This is the paper's answer to "is it just composition?", and as written it is an assertion. Our version is the same sentence with a pointer: the table of within-cell estimates goes in the evidence drawer and the sentence names it.
- **The paper distinguishes its own new measure from its predecessor's, explicitly.** "These measures complement the autonomy measures in our prior report on Claude Code. Those measures tracked how long the agent runs and how often people approve its actions automatically. Our decision attribution measure, by contrast, captures who makes the substantive decisions in a session as a whole…" Three sentences: what came before, what this is, and the difference stated as a contrast of objects (duration and approvals against decisions). **When a post introduces a measure adjacent to one Anthropic has already published, it says how the two differ before using its own** — this is exactly the "states plainly what it adds and where it overlaps" criterion, executed in three sentences. Copy the shape. (This is also the sentence the PDF garbles and the web repairs; see `## Source`.)

### Finding 4 — who the users are, and the classifier instruction that protects the claim (§3.1)

> It is explicitly instructed not to treat the act of coding as evidence of a coding profession. A session is classified into the coding SOC code (Computer and Mathematical Occupations) only when there is clear signal that software or data work is the user's job. A session in which a lawyer builds a script to automatically flag missing clauses across a folder of contracts is mapped into Legal Occupations, even if the session's work is primarily software. The session is left unclassified when there is no signal about the user's occupation.

> We were able to infer occupation in about 70% of sessions. Within this set, Computer and Mathematical Occupations, a category which encompasses most software-related jobs, is unsurprisingly the largest group. The next largest are Business and Financial Operations, Arts, Design and Media, Management, and Life, Physical, and Social Sciences. The fastest-growing non-software occupation groups in our sample are management, sales, and legal occupations.

**Annotation.**

- **The instruction that prevents the obvious confound is published, with a worked example, before any result that depends on it.** The paper's headline occupational claim — that non-software workers succeed at nearly the software rate — is worthless if everyone who writes code is labelled a software worker. So the anti-circularity rule is stated in the negative ("not to treat the act of coding as evidence of a coding profession"), then in the positive ("only when there is clear signal that software or data work is the user's job"), then as a case (the lawyer and the contract script). **Where a classifier could manufacture the finding, the rule that stops it goes in the body, in the reader's language, with an example** — not in an appendix and not as a reassurance. This is the strongest measurement-writing move in the paper.
- **The unclassified share is given as coverage and never characterised.** "We were able to infer occupation in about 70% of sessions." Framed as an achievement ("were able to"), which is honest about the direction of effort, and the remaining 30% are described nowhere: not their modes, not their expertise ratings, not their success rates. The occupational finding rests on the classified subset and no reweighting or bounding is offered. **A coverage rate is a fact about the instrument; what the uncovered cases look like is a fact about the finding, and only the first is here.**
- **Rank without magnitude, twice, once with a growth claim and no rate.** "is unsurprisingly the largest group. The next largest are…" and "The fastest-growing non-software occupation groups in our sample are management, sales, and legal occupations." Not one share, not one growth rate, no window, no figure. The corpus's pattern is that shares completing a distribution stay in the figure — but here there is no figure for occupation composition at all, so these two sentences are the whole published evidence. Note also "unsurprisingly", which tells the reader the result is not the finding; the interpretive word is doing the work of a caveat.

### Finding 5 — how the work changed, and a value that is only ever a change (§3.2)

> The composition of the work done with Claude Code changed substantially between October 2025 and April 2026. The clearest change is that the share of sessions spent fixing broken code fell from 33% to 19% (Figure 4). In its place, we saw a greater share of the work that surrounds code. Operating software grew from 14% to 21% of sessions. Writing and data analysis roughly doubled, from about 10% to 20% of sessions.

> The tasks themselves also grew more valuable. We approximate each session's economic value by asking what the work would cost on a freelance marketplace, calibrated against a public dataset of real postings. By this measure, the estimated value of the average session rose by 27% between October and April. The rise holds across many kinds of work. Building, operating, and fixing tasks all grew more valuable by roughly a third or more (about 43%, 34%, and 32% respectively). These price estimates are coarse, so we use them primarily to compare tasks to one another over time, not as dollar values to be read literally.8

**Annotation.**

- **Three changes, three pairs of endpoints, all in the same unit.** "fell from 33% to 19%", "grew from 14% to 21%", "roughly doubled, from about 10% to 20%". The house rule — a difference is reported in its units and both levels are given — is met three times in three sentences, including in the one case where a ratio is offered first ("roughly doubled") and the levels follow in the same clause. This is the counter-example to the May 2026 post's "that rate falls by more than half" with no second level, and it is the model.
- **The compositional constraint is acknowledged in words.** "In its place, we saw a greater share of the work that surrounds code." Shares of sessions must sum, so a fall somewhere is a rise elsewhere; "In its place" says so without arithmetic and stops the reader treating the four changes as four independent findings.
- **A quantity whose level is not credible is reported only as a change, and the sentence that says so is adjacent.** "By this measure, the estimated value of the average session rose by 27%… These price estimates are coarse, so we use them primarily to compare tasks to one another over time, not as dollar values to be read literally." No dollar figure appears anywhere in the paper. **When the level is uninterpretable, publish only the change, and put the reason in the same paragraph** — and footnote 8 then supplies the mechanism and the residual assumption: "The dollar amount is based on comparisons to the freelancer market—not salaried work—and comes from an ultimately fuzzy match between the Claude Code session and the job posting. Since the relative estimates will remove any consistent bias from these issues, we place more emphasis there." Note what the footnote assumes and does not test: that the bias is *consistent over time*, which is precisely what a seven-month comparison needs. The assumption is stated, which is more than most papers do, and it is not checked.
- **"The rise holds across many kinds of work" is hedged where the equivalent expertise sentence was not.** "many kinds", not "every kind" — and the three modes that are named are the three largest. Figure 4's right panel labels a fourth series that the prose never mentions. **A robustness claim quantified over an unnamed subset is a claim about the sentence's own hedge**, and "many" is the honest word here; it is also an invitation the paper does not take up, since the mode whose value barely moved is visible in the exhibit and absent from the text.

### Finding 6 — success, and the returns to expertise (§4, §4.1)

> Before turning to the characteristics of successful sessions, we should be precise about how we measure success. We do not observe users' real-world outcomes, and we cannot ask them directly whether they got what they wanted out of Claude. Instead, we rely on two complementary transcript-based measures.

> One might worry that expertise isn't the real driver—perhaps experts simply pick different tasks, or differ in other ways. Throughout this section, we partially address this worry by comparing sessions doing the same kind of work, at the same estimated value, in the same month, on the same subject, from people in the same broad occupation group, and ask how outcomes differ by the person's rated expertise.

> A novice-rated session reaches our strictest measure, verified success, 15% of the time and at least partial success 77% of the time. A session rated intermediate or up reaches verified success 28-33% of the time and partial success 91-92% of the time (Figure 5).

> In each measure, most of the gain comes from moving between novice to intermediate; between intermediate and expert, the slope decreases.

> Among sessions that hit trouble, the share that are verified successes rises from 4% for novice-rated sessions to 15% for expert-rated ones, accounting for all the controls described above (Figure 5). Looking at the looser measures, we find that the share of at least partial success is 60% for novice and 80-81% for intermediate through expert sessions.

> We say a troubled session is abandoned if it is judged as failed and zero lines of code are written: 19% of sessions where the user appears to be a novice end abandoned, against 5-7% for everyone else. In other words, the least experienced users are more likely to give up when they are struggling to get the outcome they are after. Part of the value of expertise appears to be the ability to steer the agent in the right direction.9

**Annotation.**

- **The measurement section opens with what cannot be observed.** "We do not observe users' real-world outcomes, and we cannot ask them directly whether they got what they wanted out of Claude. Instead, we rely on…" The limitation is the premise of the method, not a concession after it, so every success number the reader meets afterwards is already known to be a transcript reading. **Open the measurement paragraph with the thing the measure is standing in for.** Compare the May 2026 post, which opened each finding by quoting the survey item; same instinct, different instrument.
- **The rival explanation is written in the reader's voice and answered with an enumerated matching set.** "One might worry that expertise isn't the real driver—perhaps experts simply pick different tasks, or differ in other ways. Throughout this section, we **partially** address this worry by comparing sessions doing the same kind of work, at the same estimated value, in the same month, on the same subject, from people in the same broad occupation group…" Worry, remedy, and the exact five things held fixed, in one sentence; the verb is hedged ("partially address") and the section scope is stated ("Throughout this section"). **This is the corpus's best plain-language statement of an identification strategy** and the shape to copy: name the alternative, list what was held fixed, hedge the verb. What it does not say is what remains — within-cell differences in difficulty, and the dependence between an expertise rating read from a transcript and an outcome read from the same transcript — and neither the paragraph nor anything else in the main text names that second problem.
- **The subject of every finding sentence in this section is a session, and it is the paper's most disciplined stretch.** "A novice-rated session reaches…", "A session rated intermediate or up reaches…", "sessions where the user appears to be a novice", "for novice-rated sessions", "for expert-rated ones". The rating is never asserted of a person inside §4. It is asserted of a person in the Key findings, the Introduction and the close.
- **Two measures are given for each group, so the reader sees that the gradient depends on the measure.** 15% and 77% for novice; "28-33%" and "91-92%" for intermediate-and-up. The strictest definition separates the two groups sharply and the loosest one barely does, and putting both in one sentence is what makes that visible. **Report a rate on the strictest and the loosest definition in the same breath, or the reader cannot tell whether the finding is about the world or about the threshold.**
- **Grouped levels get ranges, and the ranges are the honest form.** "28-33%", "91-92%", "80-81%", "5-7%": four ranges rather than four averages over levels 3–5. The reader sees the spread inside the group and cannot be told a precision the pooling does not support.
- **The shape of the relationship is itself a finding and it is stated without numbers.** "In each measure, most of the gain comes from moving between novice to intermediate; between intermediate and expert, the slope decreases." Concavity, asserted across the paper's success measures, in one sentence, with no test for the difference in slopes. It is also the sentence that becomes the close's "competence, not mastery" — so the paper's most quoted conclusion rests on an eyeballed curvature. **A claim about the shape of a gradient needs the two slopes and their difference**, and ours would carry them.
- **The controls are restated inside the sentence that reports the number.** "rises from 4% for novice-rated sessions to 15% for expert-rated ones, **accounting for all the controls described above**". A reader who lands on this sentence alone is told the estimate is adjusted and where the adjustment is described. Cheap, and it travels with the quote.
- **The strongest caveat in the paper is a footnote attached to the number it undermines.** Footnote 9, on the recovery gradient: "Conditioning on trouble selects different sessions for different users. Experts hit trouble less often overall, so the troubled sessions they do have are likely to be on harder problems—using the price estimate of the session as a proxy for the complexity of the session, we see that the average estimated value of a troubled session roughly doubles from the bottom of the expertise scale to the top. Part of the gap in recovery rates may therefore reflect that novices get stuck on routine problems while experts get stuck on challenging hard problems." Selection named, direction named, *evidence for the direction supplied* (the value of a troubled session roughly doubles across the scale), and the consequence for the headline stated with a modal. **A caveat that brings its own evidence is worth more than the finding it qualifies** — this footnote is the best-written thing in the document, and it is in eight-point type at the back.
- **An interpretation is marked twice and a definition is inlined at the point of use.** "We say a troubled session is abandoned if it is judged as failed and zero lines of code are written: 19%…" — definition, colon, number. Then "In other words, the least experienced users are more likely to give up…" (restatement) and "Part of the value of expertise **appears to be** the ability to steer the agent in the right direction" (conjecture, marked). The comparison group in that sentence, though, is "everyone else", which is the one group in §4 that is never defined.
- **What the abandonment measure conflates, and the paper does not say.** "Abandoned" is judged failure plus zero lines of code added. Three of the paper's own nine work modes — analyzing data, communicating, planning — need not touch code at all, so a failed session in those modes meets the definition mechanically. Figure 5's points control for work mode, which mitigates it; the construct is never flagged. Recorded as a definitional-writing fault: **a composite measure built from an outcome and a by-product of the task type has to say which tasks can produce the by-product.**

### Finding 7 — occupation against expertise (§4.2)

> Software engineers and users in other "computer and mathematical occupations" reach verified success in about 30% of their sessions overall, where users from other professions reach verified success about 26% of the time. Among sessions that produce code (i.e., sessions that add or modify at least one line of code), those numbers are 34% and 29% respectively. That five-point gap is small, and it has neither widened nor narrowed over seven months, even as the success rates in both groups increased. In code-producing sessions, every one of the ten largest occupations in our dataset lands within seven points of software engineers in terms of their success. Management occupations are highest on verified success, slightly above the software engineering occupations. Their higher verified success rates may reflect management skills that transfer to directing an agent. But they may also partly reflect our measurement: verification rests partially on explicit confirmation in the transcript, and managers may be more likely to communicate when they get what they ask for.10

**Annotation.**

- **Both levels, for both samples, before the gap is named.** 30 against 26 overall; 34 against 29 among code-producing sessions; and only then "That five-point gap is small". The restriction is defined inline where it is introduced ("sessions that produce code (i.e., sessions that add or modify at least one line of code)"). **A gap is published with both of its rates and with the sample each rate is computed on** — the discipline the May 2026 post's "twice as many" lacked, met here twice in two sentences.
- **The gap is characterised before it is defended, and it is never tested.** "small" is an interpretation; no interval, no test, no n. The whole argument of the section — that occupation matters less than expertise — is a comparison between this five-point gap and the expertise gradient reported two pages earlier, and that comparison is never made numerically in one place. **When a post's claim is that one difference is smaller than another, the two differences belong in the same sentence, in the same units.** Ours would put them in one row of a table.
- **A null about a trend, with nothing behind it.** "it has neither widened nor narrowed over seven months, even as the success rates in both groups increased." Two over-time claims — a null and a rise — in one clause, with no figure, no coefficient, no interval and no minimum detectable effect. This is the first thing a referee raises, and the paper has already built the monthly panel that would answer it (month is a control in both adjusted specifications). **A null stated in the same clause as a positive trend, with neither quantified, is the weakest sentence shape in the corpus.**
- **"within seven points" is a claim about an exhibit, and it depends on which measure you read.** The sentence says "within seven points … in terms of their success" without naming a success measure, while Figure 6 plots three. The caption repeats the claim in the same unspecified form. **A tolerance claim names the measure it is a tolerance on.**
- **The anomaly is given two explanations, the second of which indicts the paper's own instrument.** "may reflect management skills that transfer to directing an agent. But they may also partly reflect our measurement: verification rests partially on explicit confirmation in the transcript, and managers may be more likely to communicate when they get what they ask for." Substantive reading first, instrument reading second, neither chosen, both modal. Then footnote 10 turns the artefact into a candidate finding: "Even if the model misclassifies managers, the signals relied upon to determine that the user is a likely manager—perhaps in how tasks are delegated and specified—tend to be associated with greater success. In other words, perhaps acting like a manager confers greater success." **The best-handled surprise in the corpus: explain it, then say how your own measurement could have manufactured it, then say what would still be true if it had.** Copy all three steps.

### Naming discipline

The house rule is that the question says AI and every finding says Claude. This paper is the corpus's cleanest instance of it, and it goes further: **"AI" appears exactly twice in eighteen pages** — "human-AI collaboration" in the first bullet and "interactive AI coding-assistant usage" on p. 3 — while "Claude" appears about fifty-six times and "session" about a hundred and ten. The generalising vocabulary is not "AI" but a three-step ladder:

- **"Claude" / "Claude Code"** for everything measured: "Claude rates the user's apparent expertise", "Claude takes fewer actions per turn", "~400,000 Claude Code sessions", "the estimated value of the average session".
- **"coding agents" / "agentic coding" / "the agent"** for the class the finding is about: "Agentic coding has taken off", "there remains a clear division of labor in agentic coding", "Coding agents are not substituting for domain expertise", "the agent decides how to build it".
- **"knowledge work"** for the extrapolation: "a preview of where knowledge work is headed", "as agentic tools take on other forms of knowledge work".

Each rung is a wider claim on thinner evidence, and the paper never uses a wider word for a measured sentence. **The ladder is the device to take: measured in the product's name, argued in the class's name, extrapolated in the economy's name, and never the reverse.** The one slip is in the other dimension — the unit, not the technology — where session-level ratings become person-level traits in the bullets, the introduction and the close.

## Comparisons

Nine comparisons carry the paper. Two are the findings; five are internal contrasts that make a number legible; one sets the paper's own instrument against machine-recorded telemetry; one sets the subject against outside evidence.

### Expertise level against expertise level — the spine

> A novice-rated session reaches our strictest measure, verified success, 15% of the time and at least partial success 77% of the time. A session rated intermediate or up reaches verified success 28-33% of the time and partial success 91-92% of the time (Figure 5).

> In each measure, most of the gain comes from moving between novice to intermediate; between intermediate and expert, the slope decreases.

**Annotation.** A five-point ordinal compared three ways in the same section: bottom against the rest (the sentence above), adjacent steps (the caption's "as is each adjacent-level step"), and shape (concavity). One construct, three comparisons, and the third is the one the close keeps. **One claim tested at more than one level beats six claims at one level** — this is the corpus's cleanest illustration of the rule, since the same gradient is then re-run on a second sample (troubled sessions) and on six outcome measures. What is missing is the comparison the argument needs most: the gradient over time, which the paper's own closing agenda names as the thing to watch, and which seven months of data and a month control would support.

### Novice against "everyone else"

> 19% of sessions where the user appears to be a novice end abandoned, against 5-7% for everyone else.

**Annotation.** A four-level group compressed into "everyone else" and given as a range rather than a rate, which is the honest form for a heterogeneous residual. But the residual group is never defined — levels 2–5, or 3–5? — and the paper's other groupings use "intermediate or up" (levels 3–5). **A comparison against a residual category defines the category where the comparison is made**; this one does not, and the sentence is the one the close reuses ("novices abandon the session at several times the rate of everyone else"), where the ratio replaces both rates.

### Software occupations against everyone else — the comparison that names the section

> Software engineers and users in other "computer and mathematical occupations" reach verified success in about 30% of their sessions overall, where users from other professions reach verified success about 26% of the time. Among sessions that produce code … those numbers are 34% and 29% respectively.

**Annotation.** The comparison is stated twice on two samples, which is what makes it convincing: restricting to code-producing sessions raises both rates and narrows nothing much, so the reader can see the gap is not an artefact of non-software users doing non-code work. **Running the same two-group comparison on the sample that most favours the other side is the cheapest robustness a post can offer, and it goes in the same sentence.** The web version adds a third pair on the loosest measure (89 and 88), which the PDF omits — so the document of record is the one missing the comparison that most supports its own heading.

### Ten occupations against one benchmark

> In code-producing sessions, every one of the ten largest occupations in our dataset lands within seven points of software engineers in terms of their success.

**Annotation.** A many-group comparison reduced to a tolerance around a reference group, with the reference group chosen because it is the one the reader expects to win. The exhibit does the same thing visually: Figure 6 colours the software group differently from the other nine and draws benchmark lines across the chart. **When the finding is "no group is far from the benchmark", the benchmark is named, the tolerance is stated, and the exhibit draws the benchmark as a line** — the shape is right; the missing pieces are the measure the tolerance applies to and the intervals, which the caption promises and the sentence does not use.

### October against April — the only time comparison

> The clearest change is that the share of sessions spent fixing broken code fell from 33% to 19% (Figure 4).

> That five-point gap is small, and it has neither widened nor narrowed over seven months, even as the success rates in both groups increased.

**Annotation.** Two uses of the same seven months, written to opposite standards. The composition changes are given as endpoint pairs with a figure behind them. The occupation-gap null is given as a phrase with nothing behind it. **The same window, the same panel, the same paper: one comparison gets both levels and an exhibit, the other gets six words.** The lesson is not that the null is wrong; it is that a null needs more apparatus than a positive result, not less.

### The user against Claude — the comparison inside the unit

> On average, people make about 70% of the planning decisions but only 20% of the execution decisions (Figure 2).

**Annotation.** The only comparison in the paper taken within the unit of observation: the same session yields both shares, so the contrast is invariant to which sessions are in the sample. That is what makes it the paper's most robust claim and its slogan. **A comparison taken inside the unit survives selection into the sample** — the May 2026 post reached the same conclusion from the opposite direction, building its belief finding from a within-respondent contrast after giving up on the level. Two very different pieces, one rule.

### All sessions against troubled sessions

> A similar gradient appears in sessions that run into challenges along the way. We say a session hits trouble when the failure signal records verified evidence of failure.

**Annotation.** A conditional sample introduced as a replication of the main comparison ("A similar gradient appears"), defined in the next sentence, and then — in footnote 9 — conceded to be a different comparison for different groups, because who ends up in the conditional sample depends on the variable being studied. **Conditioning on a mid-process outcome makes the comparison group depend on the treatment; the paper says so, in a footnote, with evidence.** In our posts that paragraph goes under the finding, and the sample sizes for each cell go in the drawer.

### Classifier against telemetry

> The two sources have high agreement for instance, more than 90% of sessions our classifier labeled as creating or modifying code showed code changes in the telemetry.

**Annotation.** A comparison of two instruments rather than two groups, and the only one in the main text. The independent source is named, it is machine-recorded rather than model-read, and the claim's direction is inside the sentence. It is also the only validation number the paper publishes: the five other classifiers are validated in the appendix and characterised in the close as agreeing "with a strong reference model on the majority of sessions", which is an agreement claim with no statistic. **"The majority of sessions" is not an agreement rate**, and a sentence of that shape cannot be checked.

### Claude Code against the outside world

> The share of Github projects with coding agent activity has more than doubled since late 2025,1 and Claude Code users now spend an average of 20 hours per week using the tool.2

**Annotation.** The paper's only external comparison, and it is a premise rather than a finding: one third-party measure of adoption and one internal intensity measure, placed side by side in the first paragraph to establish that the subject is worth measuring. Neither is revisited. Both carry footnotes that narrow them. **The comparison that justifies the paper is made once, in the opening, against outside evidence, and never asked to carry an argument** — the same discipline the April 2025 report used for its inherited premise.

**Annotation.** Both tables are built the same way and it is the paper's best structural idea. Two details of register in Table 1. ### Alt text

Per `room/director-2026-09-16-alt-text-ruling.md`, alt text is recorded and marked, never quoted as prose or caption. **All eight exhibits on the web page carry empty `alt` attributes** — each renders as an image with no alternative text. The only alt strings on the page are the hero image's, which repeats the title ("Agentic coding and persistent returns to expertise"), and the share card's, which describes an illustration ("Large hand-shaped network diagram with abacus-like nodes and interconnected beads representing data processing"). **Alt-text numbers: none**, so nothing in this file is marked `(alt text)`. It improves on the corpus's earlier practice in two ways: the estimator's matching set is in the caption, and sample restrictions are given operationally. It falls short in three ways, consistently: **no figure states its n**, the clustering unit changes between figures without explanation, and the in-image title and the caption title disagree on four figures out of six.

## Limitations

There is no Limitations section and no heading containing the word. The qualification is substantial and it is distributed across four layers.

**Layer 1 — the preliminary-findings paragraph, third of five in "Looking ahead", verbatim:**

> These findings are preliminary. As in most of our research, we cannot measure real-world outcomes, like whether code written in a session is actually used or discarded thereafter, or whether it produces an economically valuable artifact. In addition, the non-interactive usage this report excludes is a substantial share of activity. Developing a framework to measure it is a priority for future work. And all of our classifications of sessions depend on a model's reading of the transcript. In the Appendix, we show that our classifiers track independent telemetry in expected directions, and agree with a strong reference model on the majority of sessions. But classifiers remain challenging to validate at scale, and Claude Code sessions add further difficulty, as they may be too long and complex for human labels to serve as ground truth.

**Layer 2 — the identification paragraph, at the head of §4.1, verbatim:**

> One might worry that expertise isn't the real driver—perhaps experts simply pick different tasks, or differ in other ways. Throughout this section, we partially address this worry by comparing sessions doing the same kind of work, at the same estimated value, in the same month, on the same subject, from people in the same broad occupation group, and ask how outcomes differ by the person's rated expertise.

**Layer 3 — fences inside the sentences that report findings, verbatim, in order of appearance:**

> While we don't have full answers to these questions yet, we look to Claude Code usage data for early signals. *(§1)*

> If these patterns hold across the economy, it suggests that… *(§1)*

> Note that expertise is capturing something quite different from job title or general ability, and, crucially, it is task-specific. *(§2.3)*

> The session is left unclassified when there is no signal about the user's occupation. *(§3.1)*

> These price estimates are coarse, so we use them primarily to compare tasks to one another over time, not as dollar values to be read literally. *(§3.2)*

> We do not observe users' real-world outcomes, and we cannot ask them directly whether they got what they wanted out of Claude. *(§4)*

> For the following analysis, which is focused on the degree of success or failure in a session, we exclude sessions classified as having "no clear goal," which comprise about 7.7% of our full sample. *(§4)*

> But they may also partly reflect our measurement: verification rests partially on explicit confirmation in the transcript, and managers may be more likely to communicate when they get what they ask for. *(§4.2)*

**Layer 4 — the footnotes. Six of the ten are limitations, and each sits on the number it qualifies:**

> Detection of agentic coding activity relies on agent co-authorship tags and configuration files, which likely undercount actual usage. *(footnote 1, on the external adoption figure)*

> Note that this measures hours in which Claude Code was actively running, not the user's hands-on time typing to Claude. *(footnote 2, on the 20 hours per week)*

> Note that we exclude Claude Code usage that runs through third party integrated developer environments, and software development kits. We also therefore exclude sessions in "headless" mode where a user runs a single prompt in the CLI via claude -p "<prompt>" . We exclude this usage since it differs in two key ways—much of it is programmatic, with Claude Code embedded in automated tools and pipelines rather than conversing with a user, and even when a user is present, we do not see a user's session end-to-end the way we do on the surfaces we include. *(footnote 4, on the sample definition)*

> Like all measures in this report, these inferences are produced using our privacy-preserving analysis tool. No researcher reads individual transcripts, occupation labels are never linked to identifiable users, and we only observe aggregates over a minimum number of distinct users. *(footnote 7, on the occupation classifier)*

> The estimation approach we take here is intended to get at relative differences in the value of sessions, not absolute value. … Since the relative estimates will remove any consistent bias from these issues, we place more emphasis there. *(footnote 8, on the task-value estimator)*

> Conditioning on trouble selects different sessions for different users. … Part of the gap in recovery rates may therefore reflect that novices get stuck on routine problems while experts get stuck on challenging hard problems. *(footnote 9, on the recovery gradient)*

**Annotation.**

- **The allocation is right and the navigation is not.** Design-level limits are in the close; identification is at the head of the section it governs; measure-level limits are on the measures; number-level limits are in footnotes at the numbers. Nothing is in the wrong layer. But a reader skimming the six section headings never meets the word "limitation", the phrase "preliminary" appears once on page 15, and the paper's best caveat is footnote 9. **A caveat a skimmer cannot find is a caveat addressed to referees only** — the same verdict as the May 2026 post, and our template's promotion of Limitations to a top-level heading is the answer in both cases.
- **The framing sentence is three words.** "These findings are preliminary." No throat-clearing, no restatement of the contribution, and the next sentence is already a concession. The corpus's rule holds: **the shortest honest framing sentence is the best one, and the concession is the second sentence.**
- **Every concession names what is not measured, in the reader's units.** "whether code written in a session is actually used or discarded thereafter, or whether it produces an economically valuable artifact" — two concrete unobserved outcomes rather than an abstraction about external validity. "the non-interactive usage this report excludes is a substantial share of activity" — an excluded population, characterised in words. "all of our classifications of sessions depend on a model's reading of the transcript" — the single dependency the whole paper rests on, stated flatly. **A limitation is worth most when it names the sentence the paper is not entitled to write**, and the first of these names three.
- **A magnitude is missing from the concession that most needs one.** "a substantial share of activity" is the excluded non-interactive usage, and no number is given anywhere — even though the same telemetry that measures lines of code exists for those sessions by the paper's own account. The exclusion's *reasons* are given in full in footnote 4, and its *size* is given nowhere. **Every exclusion on this page has a reason; the largest one has no bound.**
- **The validation concession gives with one hand and takes with the other, in three sentences.** "In the Appendix, we show that our classifiers track independent telemetry in expected directions, and agree with a strong reference model on the majority of sessions. But classifiers remain challenging to validate at scale, and Claude Code sessions add further difficulty, as they may be too long and complex for human labels to serve as ground truth." The reassurance is unquantified ("in expected directions", "the majority of sessions") and the concession is specific (human labels cannot serve as ground truth on long sessions). **A reassurance stated more vaguely than the doubt it answers reads as a concession.** Ours states the agreement rate or does not claim agreement.
- **The limitation that discharges into a commitment.** "Developing a framework to measure it is a priority for future work." One limitation, one named remedy, in the sentence after the concession — the same join the May 2026 post made in its caveats paragraph, and the reason the passage reads as a plan rather than as a disclaimer.
- **What a referee would raise first, in order.** (1) **Shared-source dependence.** The expertise rating and the outcome judgment are read from the same transcript by the same family of classifiers, and one of the expertise signals is "whether the user tends to correct Claude or Claude tends to correct the user" while the failure signal scores "the user pushing back on the output". The matching set addresses task selection, not this; nothing in the main text names it. (2) **No MDE anywhere**, and in particular none behind "it has neither widened nor narrowed over seven months". (3) **No n**, at any level below the "~400,000 interactive sessions from ~235,000 people" of §1: not per expertise level, not per occupation group, not per panel, not per cell — while three captions assert intervals and significance. (4) **The panel is unused.** ~400,000 sessions from ~235,000 people supports a within-user specification, a test of whether the "task-specific" rating varies across one person's own sessions (the construct's own testable implication), and a learning curve; the only use made of it is clustering. (5) **The seven-month changes are not decomposed** into new users arriving and existing users changing what they do, though the paper itself says which occupation groups are growing fastest. (6) **The expertise gradient is never shown over time**, which is the one statistic the paper's own closing agenda says would change the conclusion. (7) **"Abandoned" conflates failure with task type** (zero lines of code is the normal state for three of the nine modes). None of these is concealed; all seven are consequences of what the paper chose not to report.
- **What it qualifies that a weaker piece would not.** The hours-per-week figure (footnote 2 says what the hours are not); the external adoption evidence (footnote 1 says the detection undercounts); its own dollar estimates (never printed as dollars); the recovery gradient (footnote 9 supplies the counter-evidence); and its own most flattering anomaly (management's lead, half-attributed to the instrument). Five qualifications that change what a reader may conclude, none of them under a heading.

## Close

"Looking ahead", verbatim, all five paragraphs:

> **Looking ahead**
>
> The results in this report offer an emerging picture of how agentic coding amplifies some forms of knowledge and skills, while substituting for others. In sessions that produce code, every major occupation succeeds at rates within a few points of those in software-related occupations. And the gains come mostly from competence, not mastery—a working grasp of the domain captures most of the benefit, while deep specialization adds only a bit more beyond that.

> *(the preliminary-findings paragraph — quoted in full under `## Limitations`)*

**Annotation.**

Every percentage stays in the sections. **The close states what was learned and leaves the numbers in the findings** — the corpus is unanimous across five very different pieces, and this one keeps the rule even for its headline.
- **The close's first sentence is the paper's thesis and it is a two-sided one.** "how agentic coding amplifies some forms of knowledge and skills, while substituting for others." Amplification and substitution in one clause, which is the frame the whole Economic Index line uses for automation and augmentation, imported here without the vocabulary. Then the two paragraphs discharge the two halves in order: substitution (coding background matters less), then amplification (domain expertise matters more). **A two-sided thesis sentence followed by one paragraph per side is the cleanest close structure in the corpus.**
- **The conjecture markers are in place on the substitution half and absent on the amplification half.** "**It appears that** coding agents are making a coding background less relevant to successful programming" — marked. "successful sessions are more likely to exhibit domain expertise" — a correlation stated as one, carefully, with sessions as the subject. But by the third sentence of that paragraph the subject has changed: "domain experts are able to direct Claude to do more work with each instruction they give", "the ability to steer Claude toward success comes more from command of a domain than from the ability to write code", "A person with such command, in any field, may now be able to do technical work they previously could not." Three sentences about persons, from a session-level rating produced by a classifier reading the same transcripts the outcomes came from, with the modal appearing only in the third. **The close is where fences get dropped, so the close is where they have to be checked** — the same verdict as the May 2026 post, reached by a different route. Our rule that every quantitative sentence maps to an entry in `results.json` has to cover the *unit* as well as the number: a session-level result closes as a session-level sentence.
- **The most quoted sentence in the paper is the one with the least support.** "And the gains come mostly from competence, not mastery—a working grasp of the domain captures most of the benefit, while deep specialization adds only a bit more beyond that." It rests entirely on the concavity sentence in §4.1, which is asserted without a slope comparison. It is also perfectly memorable, and it will be the sentence that survives. **The sentence a close makes quotable is the sentence the analysis has to be strongest on**, and here the ordering is reversed.
- **What comes next is written as two falsifiable predictions with their interpretations attached.** "if the returns to expertise begin to decrease over time, that would suggest that models are starting to supply the essential judgment that users currently bring"; "If the share of coding sessions completed successfully by users outside software occupations continues to grow, it could indicate that software production is becoming a part of ordinary work in every field". Two conditionals, each naming a measurable movement in one of the paper's own measures and what it would mean. **This is the best "what comes next" in the corpus**: not a promise to publish more, but a statement of which number would have to move, in which direction, for the conclusion to change. It is also what our pre-registration discipline asks for, written as prose. Copy it exactly.
- **Why it matters is the last sentence of the tracking paragraph, not the last sentence of the piece.** "These shifts would change who benefits from agentic coding, and by how much, and would have implications for what is most valued in the labor market." Distributional stakes, conditional, with no policy and no recommendation.
- **No recommendation to anyone, and no refusal to give one.** Nothing is addressed to firms, workers, educators or policymakers, and unlike the February 2025 report there is no explicit declining of the role. The occupational finding — that non-software workers succeed at nearly the software rate — is the most obviously actionable result in the corpus and nothing is drawn from it. Our template requires recommendations; the models available remain February 2025's explicit refusal and this paper's substitute, which is to name the measurements a future reader should watch.
- **Title against ending.** The title is "Agentic coding **and persistent returns to expertise**". The word "persistent" appears nowhere in the document except the title, the running foot that repeats the full title at the bottom of every page after the title page, and the BibTeX title — nineteen printed occurrences, and not one of them inside a sentence. The phrase "returns to expertise" appears in the body exactly twice: as §4.1's heading, and in the closing conditional "if the returns to expertise begin to decrease over time". So the title's noun phrase is delivered by the close ("successful sessions are more likely to exhibit domain expertise"), and the title's adjective is **not tested anywhere**: persistence is a claim about time, the paper's only time-varying result is the occupation-gap null, and the closing agenda names a falling gradient as something a *future* report would detect. Two readings are available — that the returns persist over the seven months, or that they persist despite the automation of coding — and the text never chooses. **A title may not carry a word the findings do not test**, and this is the corpus's clearest instance: one adjective, printed nineteen times, with no section behind it. The house rule that the title matches the ending is satisfied on the noun and failed on the modifier.
- **The last sentence is a generalisation with three hedges and it is one line long.** "And coding is a leading case—what happens in software is likely a preview of what may come as agentic tools take on other forms of knowledge work." "likely", "may come", "a leading case" — the widest claim in the paper, carrying more hedging per word than any other sentence in it, standing alone as its own paragraph. **The piece ends by naming what it is evidence *for*, not by restating what it found**, and it buys the right to that by hedging three times in twenty-three words.
- **First person, and what it is doing.** "We hope that these measures will allow us to track consequential shifts as they happen" is institutional commitment, the same use as the February 2025 report; there is no disciplinary "we" of the kind the May 2026 post used. So the close is translatable into our register without loss: "these measures are intended to track…" costs the sentence nothing. That is a difference worth recording — **this close, unlike May 2026's, contains no first-person construction that our style cannot reproduce.**
- **If the close had to stand as the summary, it would.** Remove the Key findings bullets and the reader still gets both findings, their shape, their preliminary status and the two tests that would overturn them. That is the test our template implies, and it also explains why the two drifts in the bullets (25% against 27%; "debugging" for "fixing broken code") cost the paper nothing structurally: the block was never load-bearing.
