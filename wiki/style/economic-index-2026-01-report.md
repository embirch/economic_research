**Where the findings sit: three times over, at three depths.** The Introduction's two "We find:" blocks carry six bullets with the headline numbers (24%, over half, 2–5 years) before the word "primitive" has been defined and before any sample is described. Each chapter then restates its findings in an *Overview* (Chapters 1, 3) or an unheaded opening (Chapters 2, 4) before earning them. The sample — 1M Claude.ai conversations and 1M 1P API transcripts, 13–20 November 2025 — is in footnote 1 of Chapter 1 and repeated in footnote 2 of Chapter 3. A reader again reaches every headline number before reaching the description of the data that produced it.

**Where the methods sit, and what changed.** Unlike the third report, this one has a methods chapter. Chapter 2 is nine pages on how five measures were designed, selected, validated, revised and bounded, and it is placed *before* the two chapters that use them. Everything else still follows the series' rule of *define at point of use, document in footnote*: the AUI is re-derived where it is re-used (p. 12), the convergence model is built in the section that estimates it (pp. 15–17), "effective AI coverage" is defined in the paragraph that first reports it (p. 43), the task-level education measure is constructed where it is first needed (p. 45), and the CES aggregator is set up two pages before Figure 4.6. **The transferable structure is the pair: a methods chapter for the instrument that is new, point-of-use definitions for everything derived from it.**

## Opening move

### The Introduction, verbatim and in full (PDF pp. 2–4)

> **Introduction**
>
> **How is AI reshaping the economy?**

> This report introduces new metrics of AI usage to provide a rich portrait of interactions with Claude in November 2025, just prior to the release of Opus 4.5. These "primitives"—simple, foundational measures of how Claude is used, which we generate by asking Claude specific questions about anonymized Claude.ai and first-party (1P) API transcripts—cover five dimensions relevant to AI's economic impact: user and AI skills, how complex tasks are, the degree of autonomy afforded to Claude, how successful Claude is, and whether Claude is used for personal, educational, or work purposes.

> The results reveal striking geographic variation, real-world estimates of AI task horizons, and a basis for revised assessments of Claude's macroeconomic impact.

> The data we release alongside this report are the most comprehensive to date, covering five new dimensions of AI use, consumer and firm use, and country and region breakdowns for Claude.ai

> **What has changed since our last report**
>
> In the first chapter, we revisit findings from our previous Economic Index report published in September 2025. We find:
>
> - **Claude usage remains concentrated among certain tasks, most of them related to coding**
>   While we see over 3,000 unique work tasks in Claude.ai, the top 10 most common tasks account for 24% of our sampled conversations, a slight increase since our last report. Augmentation patterns (conversations where the user learns, iterates on a task, or gets feedback from Claude) grew, rising to just over half of conversations on Claude.ai. In contrast, automated use remains dominant in 1P API traffic, reflecting its programmatic nature.
> - **Global usage remains persistently uneven while US states converge**
>   The US, India, Japan, the UK, and South Korea lead in overall Claude.ai use. Worldwide, uneven adoption remains well-explained by GDP per capita. Within the US, workforce composition plays a key role in shaping uneven adoption as states with more computer and mathematical professionals show systematically more Claude usage.

> While substantial concentration remains, since our last report Claude usage has become noticeably more evenly distributed across US states. If sustained, usage per capita would be equalized across the country in 2–5 years.

> **Introducing and analyzing our new economic primitives**
>
> In the second chapter, we discuss the motivation for and introduce our new economic primitives, including how they were selected and operationalized, and their limitations. We additionally present evidence that our primitives capture directionally accurate aspects of underlying usage patterns as compared to external benchmarks. In chapters three and four, we use these primitives to further investigate implications for adoption and productivity. We find:
>
> - **Claude use diversifies with higher adoption and income**
>   While the most common use of Claude is for work, coursework use is highest in countries with the lowest GDP per capita, while rich countries show the highest rates of personal use. This aligns with a simple adoption curve story: early adopters in less developed countries tend to be technical users with specific, high-value applications or use Claude for education, whereas mature markets see usage diversify toward casual and personal purposes.
> - **Claude succeeds on most tasks, but less so on the most complex ones**
>   We find that Claude generally succeeds at the tasks it is given, and that the education level of its responses tends to match the user's input. Claude struggles on more complex tasks: As the time it would take a human to do the task increases, Claude's success rate falls, much like prominent evals measuring the longest tasks that AIs can reliably perform.
> - **Job exposure to AI looks different when success rates are factored in**
>   We also use the success rate primitive to better understand job exposure to AI, calculating the share of each occupation that Claude can perform by weighting task coverage by both success rates and the importance of each task within the job. For some occupations, like data entry keyers and database architects, Claude shows proficiency in large swaths of the job.
> - **Claude is used for higher-skill tasks than those in the broader economy**
>   The tasks we observe in Claude usage tend to require more education than those in the broader economy. If we assume that AI-assisted tasks diminish as a share of worker responsibilities, removing them would leave behind less-skilled work. But this simple task displacement would not affect white-collar workers uniformly—for some occupations it removes the most skill-intensive tasks, for others the least.

> Without the tasks that we observe Claude performing, travel agents would experience deskilling as complex planning work gives way to routine ticket purchasing and payment collection. Property managers, by contrast, would experience upskilling as bookkeeping tasks give way to contract negotiations and stakeholder management.

> **A new window for understanding AI's impact on the economy**
>
> These results provide a new window into how AI is currently impacting the economy. Knowing the success rate of tasks gives a more accurate picture of which tasks might be automated, how impacted certain jobs might be, and how labor productivity will change. Measuring differential performance by user education sheds light on inequality effects.

> Indeed, the close relationship between education levels in inputs and outputs signals that countries with higher educational attainment may be better positioned to benefit from AI, independent of adoption rates alone.

> This data release aims to enable researchers and the public to better understand the economic implications of AI and investigate the ways in which this transformative technology is already having an effect.

### Annotation

- **What question is posed.** The H3 asks "How is AI reshaping the economy?" and the Introduction never answers it. That heading is scaffolding, not a thesis; nothing after it is an answer to it. The report's actual proposition is in the first sentence of the body — "This report introduces new metrics of AI usage" — and its actual claim is that a *measurement gap* is what has been holding the economics back. Compare September 2025, which asked "Why is this?" in three words and answered it in a bolded thesis two paragraphs later. **This is the weaker of the two openings and the reason is diagnosable: there is no puzzle in it.** A reader is told what is being built before being told what could not previously be answered. For our own posts the lesson is the one the September file already states — name the thing that could not be settled, then the instrument, then the result — and this report is the counter-example that shows what is lost when the order is reversed.
- **How a new instrument is introduced in the first sentence it appears in.** "These 'primitives'—simple, foundational measures of how Claude is used, which we generate by asking Claude specific questions about anonymized Claude.ai and first-party (1P) API transcripts—cover five dimensions…" Scare quotes on the coined term, a five-word gloss ("simple, foundational measures of how Claude is used"), the **provenance of the measurement inside the same em-dash clause** ("by asking Claude specific questions about anonymized … transcripts"), and then the five dimensions enumerated in plain words. The single most important thing about these measures — that Claude generates them — is not deferred to the methods chapter. It is in the defining sentence, unhedged, before any number. That is the model for introducing a constructed measure.
- **Who is said to be affected.** Not employees, not firms, not countries in the first instance — *researchers*. "The data we release alongside this report are the most comprehensive to date"; "This data release aims to enable researchers and the public…". The people whose work changes are named only in the last two bullets and the paragraph after them: white-collar workers, travel agents, property managers. Nobody is named as harmed; deskilling is stated as a conditional consequence of an assumption the report labels as an assumption ("If we assume that AI-assisted tasks diminish as a share of worker responsibilities…").
- **What the data is said uniquely to show.** One superlative, attached to the data and not to a result: "the most comprehensive to date". Later, in the close, "regional breakdowns globally for the first time". As in September, "first" and "most" modify a *cut of data*, never a finding.
- **How soon the first number appears.** Not until the first bullet, about 200 words in, and the first three numbers are all Anthropic's own: "over 3,000 unique work tasks", "24% of our sampled conversations", "just over half". This is a change from September, which opened on somebody else's survey to establish a puzzle. With no puzzle to establish there is no outside number, and the Introduction has no citations at all in the PDF. **The two facts are connected, and they are worth naming together: an opening with no external number is usually an opening with no puzzle.**
- **The two-beat bullet survives intact.** Every bullet is a bolded claim in words followed by the claim in numbers or in named cases. Six bullets, six hedges or scope restrictions inside them: "a slight increase", "remains well-explained by", "This aligns with a simple adoption curve story", "much like prominent evals…", "If we assume that…", "would not affect white-collar workers uniformly". No bullet asserts a mechanism without marking it as a story.
- **Precision varies with depth, again deliberately.** "just over half" in the Introduction is "52%" in Chapter 1. "2–5 years" in the Introduction is, in Chapter 1, three separate estimates (a Gini extrapolation giving "roughly two years", OLS/WLS giving "little more than two years", 2SLS giving "just four to five years") with their standard errors discussed. Coarse at the top, exact in the body, and the coarse form is the *union* of the body's range rather than its most favourable point — which is the honest way to round a range upward into a headline.
- **The Introduction picks its illustrations for recognisability, not for rank.** The deskilling bullet's worked pair is "travel agents" and "property managers". Chapter 4's text names neither: it works through *technical writers*, *travel agents*, teaching professions and *real estate managers*. "Property managers" is the reader-friendly half of an O\*NET title that the chapter renders in full. This is the same licence September took by naming Singapore and Canada while Israel led; here it is exercised on an occupation name rather than a rank, and it is less defensible, because a reader searching the chapter for "property managers" will not find them.
- **One claim in the Introduction that the body does not support in the form given.** "For some occupations, like data entry keyers and database architects, Claude shows proficiency in large swaths of the job." Chapter 4 discusses data entry workers at length; database architects are named nowhere else in the report. A bullet that names two examples and delivers one is a drafting tell worth catching in our own drafts: **every occupation named in a summary bullet should appear in the section that earns it.**
- **Register of the opening.** First person plural throughout for institutional and analytical acts ("we generate", "we release", "we revisit", "we discuss", "we present", "we use", "We find", "we see", "we observe"), never for a finding. The findings inside the bullets are agentless or take Claude as subject ("Claude usage remains concentrated", "coursework use is highest in countries with the lowest GDP per capita", "Claude's success rate falls"). Our house style forbids the first person; the substitution is the one September's file identifies — attribute the act to the analysis or the exhibit.
- **Two drafting tells in the front matter.** The third paragraph of the Introduction ends without a terminal period in the PDF ("…breakdowns for Claude.ai"). And the heading "How is AI reshaping the economy?" is a question the document does not take up. Both are small; both are the kind of thing a summary block would have caught, which is part of the argument for not having one.

## Findings and their caveats

### Chapter 1, Finding 1 — concentration rose on both surfaces

> Even though frontier LLMs have an impressive range of capabilities relevant to every facet of the modern economy, Claude usage remains very concentrated among a small number of tasks. As compared to nearly one year ago, consumer usage on Claude.ai is modestly more concentrated: The share of conversations assigned to the ten most prevalent O\*NET tasks was 24% in November 2025, 1pp higher than in August and up from 21% in January 2025. The most prevalent task in November 2025—modifying software to correct errors—alone represented 6% of usage.

> In our last Anthropic Economic Index report we began tracking business adoption patterns by studying Claude usage among 1P API customers. The ten most common tasks grew from 28% of API records in August to 32% in November. Rising concentration among a small set of tasks suggests the highest-value applications continue to generate outsized economic value even as models have become more capable at a wider range of tasks. As with Claude.ai the most common task among API customers was modifying software to correct errors, which accounted for one in ten records.

**Annotation.** The claim is made once and then tested at two levels — the same statistic on two samples — and the *difference between the two movements* is what carries the finding: +1pp on Claude.ai against +4pp on the API, so "concentration among tasks increased more notably" for enterprise use. Each number is given with two comparison points (November against August, and November against January) so a one-point move cannot be mistaken for a trend and a four-point move cannot be dismissed as noise. The opening concession is doing anti-overclaim work in the same breath as the claim: "Even though frontier LLMs have an impressive range of capabilities relevant to every facet of the modern economy". And the interpretation sentence separates itself with a hedge ("suggests") and carries an explicit counterweight inside it ("even as models have become more capable at a wider range of tasks") — the report states the fact that argues against its own reading, in the sentence that offers the reading.

The shared top task on both surfaces ("modifying software to correct errors", 6% of Claude.ai usage and "one in ten records" on the API) is reported as a coincidence of ranking, not as a similarity claim. Note the rounding discipline: 6% as a percentage where it sits beside other percentages, "one in ten" where it stands alone in a sentence about prevalence.

### Chapter 1, Finding 2 — the crossover reverses

> How AI will affect the economy depends not just on the tasks Claude is used for but the way that users access and engage underlying model capabilities. Since our first report, we have classified conversations into one of five interaction types, which we group into two broader categories: automation and augmentation.[5]

> Figure 1.3 plots how automated versus augmented use has evolved over time since we first started collecting this data one year ago. In January 2025, augmented use of Claude was dominant: 56% of conversations were classified as augmentation compared to 41% automated.[6] In August 2025, more conversations were classified as automated as compared to augmented.

> This was a notable development since it suggested that rapid improvements in model capabilities and platform functionality coincided with users increasingly delegating tasks entirely to Claude. This was evident in the "directive" collaboration mode, which is further grouped as automation. Directive conversations are those in which users give Claude a task and it completes it with minimal back-and-forth. From January 2025 to August 2025 the share of such directive conversations rose from 27% to 39%.[7]

> Three months later, the share of directive conversations had fallen 7pp to 32% in November 2025 as augmentation once again became more prevalent on Claude.ai than automation. Nevertheless, the automation share was still elevated as compared to nearly one year ago when we first began tracking this measure, suggesting that the underlying trend is still toward greater automation even as the August spike overstated how quickly it was materializing.

> While we see some evidence of a shift toward soft skill usage on Claude.ai with design, management, and education now higher, the shift back toward augmented use was broad-based in November (Figure 1.4). The rise in augmented use was driven mainly by users iterating with Claude to complete tasks ("task iteration") rather than asking Claude to explain concepts ("learning").

And the two footnotes that bound it:

> [2] The share of conversations on Claude.ai that were classified into neither automation nor augmentation categories fell from 3.9% to 3.0%.

> [7] In this report we use Sonnet 4.5 for classification whereas in our previous Economic Index report we used Sonnet 4. We previously found that different models can generate different classification outcomes, though these effects tend to be modest.

**Annotation.** This is the most instructive finding in the corpus on **how to revise your own published claim in public**, and it is worth reading twice. The sequence is: restate the prior finding in the prior report's own terms; restate the interpretation that was offered for it ("This was a notable development since it suggested…"); give the new number with its direction and magnitude ("had fallen 7pp to 32%"); and then — the move that does the work — **separate the level from the trend**: "Nevertheless, the automation share was still elevated as compared to nearly one year ago … suggesting that the underlying trend is still toward greater automation even as the August spike overstated how quickly it was materializing."

Nothing is retracted and nothing is defended. The August observation is re-described as a *spike around a trend* rather than as an error, and the word "overstated" is applied to the *pace* rather than to the direction. Compare September 2025's unhedged headline — "This is the first report where automation usage exceeds augmentation usage" — which is exactly the sentence this paragraph has to absorb. The absorbing is done in one clause. **For our own posts: when a later result cuts against an earlier one, name the earlier claim, distinguish what survives (direction) from what does not (rate), and say which of the two the earlier claim over-read.**

Two boundaries travel with the finding. Footnote 2 accounts for the residual that made the 52%/45% pair not sum to 100 — a question the third report left a reader unable to answer about its own 77%/12% split, so this is a direct repair of a named weakness in the prior wave. Footnote 7 discloses that the classifier changed again (Sonnet 4.5 for Sonnet 4) and, unlike September's footnote 4, **does not rerun the comparison**: it appeals instead to a previous finding that such effects "tend to be modest". That is weaker than the prior report and it is the caveat a referee would raise first here — the headline is a 7pp reversal measured across an instrument change, and the reader is given no re-run and no magnitude for the instrument effect. The prior wave reported "45% automation versus 49%"; this one reports nothing comparable.

### Chapter 1, Finding 3 — workforce composition, not income, inside the US

> What shapes patterns of usage within the US and around the world? In our previous report we emphasized the key role played by income differences globally: Variation in Claude usage across countries is largely accounted for by variation in GDP per capita. In Chapter 3 we revisit the importance of income in shaping not just usage intensity but also patterns of usage around the world.

> Within the US, income is less clearly a predictor of usage. Instead, what appears to matter most is the composition of each state's workforce and how well-matched the workforce is to Claude capabilities as reflected in task-level usage. States that have a higher share of workers in computer and mathematical occupations—like Washington D.C., Virginia, and Washington—tend to have higher usage per capita. Quantitatively, each 1% increase in the share of such tech workers in a state is associated with 0.36% higher usage per capita (Figure 1.7). This alone accounts for nearly two-thirds of the cross-state variation in AUI.

> While we would intuitively expect Claude usage to be higher in states with more tech workers, this pattern holds more generally: Usage per capita is higher in states with more workers in occupations where Claude usage is overrepresented as compared to the US workforce (e.g., Arts, Design, Entertainment, Sports and Media) or with relatively fewer workers in occupations where Claude usage is low as compared to the national economy (e.g., Transportation and Material Moving). This can be seen by calculating the Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence between the composition of each state's workforce and the global composition of Claude usage. States with a lower KL divergence—and thus with a workforce that looks more similar to Claude usage patterns—tend to have higher usage per capita.

**Annotation.** A question posed as a heading-free sentence ("What shapes patterns of usage within the US and around the world?"), the prior report's answer restated, and then the prior answer *narrowed by level*: income still explains the global variation, and does not explain the US variation. Two facts reported side by side with the elasticity attached to the one that holds ("each 1% increase in the share of such tech workers … is associated with 0.36% higher usage per capita") and the fit stated separately ("accounts for nearly two-thirds of the cross-state variation in AUI"). Slope and fit given as distinct quantities, exactly as September's file recommends.

The third paragraph is the *one claim at more than one level* pattern in its cleanest form in this report. The obvious version of the claim (tech workers) is conceded to be obvious — "While we would intuitively expect Claude usage to be higher in states with more tech workers" — and then generalised to a distributional statistic that does not depend on picking an occupation: the KL divergence between a state's workforce mix and Claude's usage mix. Two examples are given on opposite sides (Arts/Design overrepresented, Transportation underrepresented) so the generalisation is not a one-sided illustration. And the technical term is glossed by its consequence rather than by its formula: "States with a lower KL divergence—and thus with a workforce that looks more similar to Claude usage patterns—tend to have higher usage per capita." No coefficient is given for the KL version, and none is claimed.

### Chapter 1, Finding 4 — the convergence estimate, and how a fragile number is bounded

> By contrast, usage became more evenly distributed across US states from August to November 2025: the Gini coefficient, a standard measure of equality, fell from 0.37 to 0.32. While it is important to exercise caution in interpreting short-run changes, this is a relatively large change toward perfect equality in which the AUI is equal to 1 for all states with a Gini coefficient of 0. If the Gini coefficient for the US again falls by 0.05 every three months, then parity of usage would be reached in roughly two years.

> While differences in workforce composition appear to play a role in shaping regional adoption within the US, early evidence suggests Claude is diffusing considerably faster than historical precedent would predict. Economically consequential technologies have historically taken around half a century to achieve full diffusion across the US (Kalanyi et al., 2025). By contrast, comparing Claude adoption rates in November 2025 to three months prior, we estimate that parity in adoption per capita across US states—as measured by the AUI—could be reached within 2–5 years. This estimate comes with a high degree of uncertainty as the precision of our estimates cannot rule out much slower rates of diffusion.

> We generate this estimate through the lens of a simple model of diffusion, which we briefly describe here. We model diffusion as proportional convergence toward a common steady state of equalized usage per capita in which each state s has an AUI equal to 1:

> Under this model, the log deviation of AUI from steady state (AUI = 1) shrinks by a factor of β every three months, implying a half-life of *ln(.5)/ln(*β*)* quarters. For example, with quarterly data a value of β = 0.99 implies a half-life of about 17 years. To illustrate, starting from an initial AUI of 2, this means AUI would decline to around 1.4 after 17 years and to around 1.1 after 50 years. We take β = 0.99 as a sensible benchmark because it implies a pace of diffusion similar to economically consequential technologies in the 20th century.

> Naively estimating this equation by ordinary least squares (OLS) yields an estimate of β̂ ≈ 0.77. Weighted least squares (WLS) where we weight by each state's workforce yields an estimate of β̂ ≈ 0.76 (Figure 1.8). Both are statistically distinguishable from 1 at conventional levels. Taken at face value, these estimates imply that it would take little more than two years for each state's AUI to close most of the gap to 1.

> A concern with estimating convergence this way is that our AUI estimates are subject to sampling noise and other variation unrelated to diffusion. This can produce classical attenuation bias: even if AUI is not actually changing, our estimate of β could end up meaningfully below one.

> To address this, we estimate the model by two-stage least squares (2SLS), instrumenting the log of AUI in August 2025 with the composition of each state's workforce, measured by its proximity to overall Claude usage patterns. The logic behind this instrument is that workforce composition is a strong predictor of Claude usage (relevance) but being measured independently, is expected to be uncorrelated with sampling noise in our AUI estimates (validity). As noted above, states with more workers in high-Claude-usage roles do tend to have systematically higher usage per capita.

> The 2SLS estimates imply modestly slower convergence: β̂ ≈ 0.89 unweighted and β̂ ≈ 0.86 when weighting by each state's working-age population. However, these estimates are less precise, and only the former is statistically distinguishable from 1 at the 10% level. Despite implying a slower convergence than OLS, the 2SLS estimates still imply rapid diffusion: just four to five years for the log deviation of each state's AUI to shrink by 90%.

> That said, our estimates are based on just three months of data. And while the 2SLS specification may help address sampling noise, considerable uncertainty remains. We will revisit this question of the pace of diffusion in future reports.

**Annotation.** The best-written passage in the report, and the one closest to what our own posts are supposed to do.

Eight moves, in order: the benchmark from outside the data (half a century, cited); the headline with its uncertainty *in the adjacent sentence* ("This estimate comes with a high degree of uncertainty as the precision of our estimates cannot rule out much slower rates of diffusion"); the model, stated in one sentence of prose plus one equation; **the null value made interpretable before any estimate is quoted** (β = 0.99 → a 17-year half-life → an AUI of 2 falls to 1.4 in 17 years and 1.1 in 50, "similar to economically consequential technologies in the 20th century"); the naive estimates with the word "Naively" attached and "Taken at face value" as the qualifier on their reading; the threat, named as a named bias ("classical attenuation bias") with its mechanism spelled out in one clause; the fix, with relevance and validity each argued in half a sentence; the corrected estimates with **their weaker significance reported** ("only the former is statistically distinguishable from 1 at the 10% level"); and a three-sentence paragraph that declines to close the question.

Four things to copy exactly. (i) The benchmark value of the parameter is given an economic interpretation *before* the estimate, so the reader knows what would be unremarkable — the same device as September's dashed line at AUI = 1 and its Zipf −1. (ii) Both estimators are reported, in the order naive-then-corrected, with the correction moving the answer *against* the headline (0.77 → 0.89, two years → four to five years) and the headline then quoted as the union of the two ("2–5 years"). (iii) The significance is reported at the level it actually attains, including the specification that fails ("only the former"). (iv) The sample size is never stated as a number, but its consequence is stated three times: "based on just three months of data", "a change observed over a three month period", "considerable uncertainty remains".

One tension the report does not resolve: the Gini extrapolation gives "roughly two years", the regression gives "2–5 years", and the two live four pages apart in the same chapter with no sentence reconciling them. September's report caught its own 40%-versus-9.7% discrepancy in a footnote; this one lets a similar mismatch stand. **In our own posts, two methods that answer the same question get one sentence saying which is preferred and why.**

### Chapter 2, how a new measure is introduced and bounded

The definitional paragraph:

> In this edition of the Anthropic Economic Index, we expand the breadth of data available to external researchers by providing insights on five economic "primitives", by which we mean simple, foundational measures of the ways that Claude is used, which we generate by asking Claude to answer specific questions about the anonymized transcripts in our sample. Some of our primitives encompass several such questions, and others use a single indicator.

The five definitions, each a bold-lead paragraph (pp. 21):

> ***Task complexity*** captures that tasks can vary in their complexity, including how long they take to complete and how difficult they are. A "debugging" task in O\*NET could refer to Claude fixing a small error in a function or comprehensively refactoring a codebase—with very different implications for labor demand. We measure complexity through estimated human time to complete tasks without AI, time spent completing tasks with AI, and whether users handle multiple tasks within a single conversation.

> ***Human and AI skills*** address how automation interacts with skill levels. If AI disproportionately substitutes for tasks requiring less expertise while complementing higher-skilled work, it could be another form of skill-biased technical change—increasing demand for highly skilled workers while displacing lower skilled workers. We measure whether users could have completed tasks without Claude, and the years of education needed to understand both user prompts and Claude's responses.

> ***Use case*** distinguishes professional, educational, and personal use. Labor market effects most directly follow from workplace use, while educational use may signal where the future workforce is building AI-complementary skills.

> ***AI autonomy*** measures the degree to which users delegate decision-making to Claude. Our latest report documented rising "directive" use where users delegate tasks entirely. Tracking autonomy levels—from active collaboration to full delegation—helps forecast the pace of automation.

> ***Task success*** measures Claude's assessment of whether Claude completes tasks successfully. Task success helps assess whether tasks can be automated effectively (can a task be automated at all?) and efficiently (how many attempts would it take to automate a task?). That is, task success matters for both the feasibility and the cost of automation labor tasks.

And the distinction from the existing facet:

> AI autonomy captures something different from our existing automation/augmentation distinction. For example, "Translate this paragraph into French" is high automation (directive, minimal back-and-forth) but low AI autonomy (the task requires little decision-making from Claude).

**Annotation.** Each definition is a three-part unit and the parts are always in the same order: **what the dimension is about · why an economist should care · how it is operationalised.** "We measure complexity through estimated human time to complete tasks without AI, time spent completing tasks with AI, and whether users handle multiple tasks within a single conversation" is the whole construct in one sentence with three components named. Two of the five carry a worked example instead of a middle clause, and both examples are chosen to show that the *existing* taxonomy cannot separate what the new one can: the debugging example ("a small error in a function or comprehensively refactoring a codebase") is the argument that O\*NET task labels are too coarse, and the translation example is the argument that autonomy is not automation. **Defining a new measure by naming the case the old measure gets wrong is the strongest available form and the one to copy.**

The one definition to read twice is task success: "measures **Claude's assessment of** whether Claude completes tasks successfully." The self-assessment is in the defining clause, not in a caveat. Every subsequent success number in the report inherits that clause whether or not it repeats it — and mostly it does repeat it ("Claude assesses personal tasks as successfully completed 78% of the time", "Claude estimates that users would be able to complete…"). The attribution travels with the number. Against that, the closing sentence of the same paragraph is garbled — "the feasibility and the cost of automation labor tasks" — which is the second typo in a defining passage.

The bounding section is the report's structural contribution and is quoted here in full:

> Our goal was to create classifiers that are straightforward to implement and in combination provide potentially important economic signals. While we are very confident in the directional accuracy of the new measures (e.g., tasks with higher average years of education needed to understand the human prompt are likely more complex), none of the measures should be taken as exact or definitive (e.g., Claude.ai may somewhat underestimate the human education years needed for many tasks).

> Even so, the primitives enrich our understanding of how people use AI. Systematic relationships emerge across primitives, regions, and tasks—patterns we explore in depth in Chapters 3 and 4. That these relationships are intuitive and consistent suggests the primitives capture relevant aspects of how people and businesses use Claude.

> External benchmarks reinforce this. In our productivity work, Claude's time estimates correlate with actual time spent on software engineering tasks. Figure 2.1 shows that our human education measure correlates with actual worker education levels across occupations. These validations suggest individual primitives are directionally correct—and combining them may provide additional analytical value, such as enriching productivity estimates with task success rates or constructing new measures of occupational exposure.

> Ultimately, the strongest validation will come from the primitives' ability to capture meaningful variation in labor market outcomes. The data we release enable external researchers to analyze economic shifts in new ways. Early work has been encouraging—the automation/augmentation distinction from prior reports has already been used by external researchers to analyze labor market shifts (Brynjolfsson, Chandar & Chen, 2025).

And the procedure, which is where a referee would go first:

> We propose that multiple simple primitives, even if somewhat noisy and not perfectly accurate by themselves, can together provide important signals on how AI is being used. We therefore mainly tested for directional accuracy.

> For classifying task duration with and without AI, we used minimally modified versions of our prior productivity work. For net new classifiers,[1] implemented via our privacy-preserving tooling, our validation process was as follows. We designed multiple potential measures to capture concepts such as task complexity. For Claude.ai, we evaluated the classifier performance compared to a human researcher on a small set of transcripts in which users gave feedback to Claude.ai and for which we thus have permission to look at underlying transcripts. For first-party API (1P API) data, we validate the classifiers using a mix of internal and synthetic data. Neither data sources are fully representative of Claude.ai or 1P API traffic, but they allow us to check that the classifiers are working on data that resembles real usage data, while ensuring privacy.

> Based on initial performance, we revised the classifiers that needed tweaking or discarded classifiers that did not perform well. Interestingly, we find that in some instances (e.g., to measure task success), a simple classifier performed better than a nuanced, complex classifier when compared to human ratings. We then compared performance of classifier versions with vs. without chain of thought prompting, and decided to keep chain of thought prompting only for three facets (human time estimate, human with AI time estimate, and AI autonomy) where we found that it substantially improved performance. We selected a final set of nine new classifiers for the five primitives, all of which are directionally accurate even if they may deviate somewhat from human ratings.

**Annotation.** The claim made for the instrument is **exactly as strong as the validation supports and no stronger**, and the report says so in the same sentence twice over, each time with a concrete instance on both sides: "While we are very confident in the directional accuracy of the new measures (e.g., tasks with higher average years of education needed to understand the human prompt are likely more complex), none of the measures should be taken as exact or definitive (e.g., Claude.ai may somewhat underestimate the human education years needed for many tasks)." Confidence and its limit, each with an example. That construction — *confident in X, not in Y, here is an instance of each* — is the single most transferable sentence pattern in this file.

The claim is then argued at three escalating levels of evidence, in ascending order of what they would prove and descending order of what has been done: internal coherence ("That these relationships are intuitive and consistent suggests…" — the weakest, and marked as a suggestion); external correlation (two named benchmarks, one from prior Anthropic work, one from BLS/ACS in Figure 2.1); and predictive validity, which is named as the standard and explicitly deferred ("Ultimately, the strongest validation will come from the primitives' ability to capture meaningful variation in labor market outcomes"). **Naming the validation you have not done, and calling it the strongest one, is how to introduce a measure without overclaiming it.**

The procedure paragraph is unusually candid in four places and each is quotable: the validation set is small and non-representative, and both facts are stated ("a small set of transcripts", "Neither data sources are fully representative"); classifiers were revised and *discarded* against that set, which means the reported set is selected on the validation data, and the report says it plainly; a negative result is reported ("a simple classifier performed better than a nuanced, complex classifier"); and a design choice is reported with the reason and the scope ("keep chain of thought prompting only for three facets … where we found that it substantially improved performance"). Nine classifiers for five primitives, counted. What is missing, and what a referee raises first, is any number: no accuracy, no agreement rate, no correlation with the human researcher, no N for the validation set. The honesty is entirely qualitative.

### Chapter 2, Finding — two clusters profiled across all five primitives

> Task complexity. Claude estimates that software development requests would take a competent professional approximately 3.3 hours to complete without AI—close to the global average of 3.1 hours. Personal life management tasks are estimated to be simpler, averaging 1.8 hours. Estimated human-AI collaboration time is similar across both (~15 minutes), showing this primitive varies less than other primitives for these two tasks.

> Human and AI skills. Software development requests draw on more specialized knowledge: both human prompts and AI responses are estimated to require approximately 13.8 years of education to understand, compared to 9.1–9.4 years for personal life management requests. Claude estimates that users would be able to complete personal life management requests by themselves 96% of the time, versus 82% for software development requests—indicating that Claude provides more essential support for technical work.

> Use case. Claude classifies 64% of software development requests as work-related, compared to just 17% for personal life management. This illustrates that Claude can be used for very different purposes. Overall, Claude.ai use is 46% work, 19% coursework, and 35% personal.

> AI autonomy. Both clusters show similar estimated autonomy levels (~3.5 on a 1 to 5 scale), near the global average. This means that both software development and personal life management tasks, on average, afford Claude a similar autonomy to make decisions on how to complete the task.

> Task success. Claude assesses personal tasks as successfully completed 78% of the time, versus 61% for software development. Harder tasks—those requiring more specialized knowledge and where users could not easily complete them alone—show lower estimated success rates.

**Annotation.** Five paragraphs, one per primitive, each in the same shape: the two cluster values, the global average as the third reference point, and one sentence of reading. The two clusters were chosen to be maximally unlike ("software development" against "personal life management"), so the exhibit doubles as a demonstration that the measures discriminate — and the report is explicit that the demonstration is the point: "To illustrate how the primitives distinguish between different types of AI use…".

The device to steal is what happens when a primitive **does not** discriminate. Two of the five come out flat, and both are reported as flat, with the flatness named as a property of the measure rather than glossed over: "Estimated human-AI collaboration time is similar across both (~15 minutes), showing this primitive varies less than other primitives for these two tasks"; "Both clusters show similar estimated autonomy levels (~3.5 on a 1 to 5 scale), near the global average." A five-measure profile in which all five separated would be less credible than this one.

Note the verbs. Every number is attributed to the classifier, never to the world: "Claude estimates that", "are estimated to require", "Claude estimates that users would be able to", "Claude classifies", "Claude assesses", "show lower **estimated** success rates". Nine attributions in five short paragraphs. And the precision convention from September holds: "approximately 3.3 hours", "~15 minutes", "~3.5" for quantities being characterised; "96%", "82%", "64%", "17%", "78%", "61%", "46% work, 19% coursework, and 35% personal" for quantities being compared.

The exhibit itself is a typeset three-column table — global average, software development, personal life management — with ten primitive rows and **one N per column**, which is the minimum an exhibit of this kind needs and more than the prose reports. Its interior values are not recorded here, per `room/director-2026-09-16-figure-values-ruling.md` ("in `wiki/style/` no chart-read numbers are recorded at all"); every number quoted above comes from the body prose. Two structural points survive without them: the exhibit carries rows the prose never discusses (multitasking, and the global column of several primitives), so the prose is a reading of the exhibit rather than a transcription of it; and where prose and exhibit round differently — the prose's "~15 minutes" and "~3.5" against the exhibit's finer values — the prose rounds *away* from the exhibit rather than toward it. That is the kind of small drift a verification pass exists to catch, and in our own posts the exhibit and the sentence take the same rounding.

### Chapter 3, Finding 1 — use case by income

> Our data, relying on a privacy-preserving[1] analysis of 1 million Claude.ai conversations,[2] reveals striking geographic differences in how Claude is adopted. Claude is predominantly used for work, across the globe and across the United States. However, there is geographic variation in use cases. At the global level, the Balkans and Brazil have the highest relative share of work use (see Figure 3.1), and Indonesia stands out with the highest share of coursework. At the US state level, New York stands out as the state using Claude relatively the most for work.

> Use case differences are related to a country's per capita income, which, in turn, is related to per capita AI adoption. We observe that work use cases and personal use cases of Claude are more common in higher income countries, while coursework use cases are more common in lower income countries (see Figure 3.2). Interestingly, these findings converge with recent work by Microsoft showing that AI use for school is associated with lower per capita income, whereas AI use for leisure is associated with higher per capita income.

> Multiple factors could contribute to these patterns:
>
> - Personal use cases may be more common as AI adoption increases and more diverse users use AI, or existing users explore wider applications of AI. In contrast, countries with lower per capita adoption (which is correlated with lower per capita income) may be focused on specific use cases such as coding or as coursework.
> - Countries differ in their ability to pay for Claude, and coursework use cases may be better suited to free Claude usage than complex use cases in work areas such as software engineering.
> - Users in higher-income countries may have more other resources, such as free time and continuous Internet access, that enable non-essential personal use cases.

**Annotation.** The dominant category is conceded before the variation is described ("Claude is predominantly used for work, across the globe and across the United States. However, there is geographic variation in use cases"), which is the same guard the concentration finding used and stops a share difference from reading as a level difference. Every geographic claim is relative and says so: "the highest **relative** share of work use", "using Claude **relatively** the most for work". An unqualified "highest share of work use" would have been a claim about levels that the sample floor cannot support.

The mechanism list is September's five-bullet **confluence** device with three bullets instead of five: candidate channels, none tested, none ranked, prefaced by "Multiple factors could contribute to these patterns". Two of the three are about ability to pay — a channel entirely outside the data and named as such. The external agreement is reported as convergence rather than as confirmation ("these findings converge with recent work by Microsoft"), which is the right verb for two studies that share neither sample nor method.

### Chapter 3, Finding 2 — one relationship, two levels, opposite signs

> However, the relationship between AUI and the primitives often differs between country and US state level. For example, at the country level, the AUI correlates negatively with the time it would take a human to complete a task without AI, and with how much decision-making autonomy AI is given. At the US state level, these relationships are not statistically significant–likely also due to the smaller sample size for US states. Additionally, we observe a positive correlation between the AUI and Claude.ai use for work at the US state, but not at the country level.

> Importantly, the primitives themselves are not necessarily causal factors—we don't know if income or education are truly driving adoption, or if they're proxies for other underlying conditions. Many of these factors are highly correlated with one another. For example, at the US state level, human education years show a strong association with the Anthropic AI Usage Index in isolation, but this relationship disappears once we control for GDP and other primitives—suggesting education may be capturing variation that's better explained by economic development and other factors.

> Economic and institutional context—such as how education levels vary within a geography—are related to how AI is being used. Interestingly, we observe that task success is negatively associated with human education at the country level, but positively related at the US state level. However, the positive relationship at the state level becomes insignificant when controlling for other primitives (see Figure 3.5). This means the relationship pattern at one level of observation (country) contradicts the relationship pattern at another level (US state). Cross-country, educated populations may attempt harder tasks and therefore see lower success rates. Within homogeneous contexts, education may not improve task success.

**Annotation.** A sign reversal across levels of aggregation, reported as a finding rather than smoothed into one. Four things are done in the right order and all four are transferable. (i) The null is explained, not just recorded: "these relationships are not statistically significant–likely also due to the smaller sample size for US states" — the report names its own power problem in the clause that reports the null. (ii) The identification limit is stated in its own paragraph, opening on "Importantly", before any interpretation: "the primitives themselves are not necessarily causal factors—we don't know if income or education are truly driving adoption, or if they're proxies for other underlying conditions." (iii) A bivariate result is shown to be fragile to controls, and the fragility is reported as the result: "human education years show a strong association … in isolation, but this relationship disappears once we control for GDP and other primitives". (iv) The contradiction is named in plain words rather than left for the reader to notice: "This means the relationship pattern at one level of observation (country) contradicts the relationship pattern at another level (US state)."

Then two candidate explanations, one sentence each, neither chosen, both phrased as conjecture with the subject being the population rather than the data: "Cross-country, educated populations may attempt harder tasks and therefore see lower success rates. Within homogeneous contexts, education may not improve task success." This is September's *fork left open on the page*, in miniature. What is missing is the power calculation that would distinguish "no relationship" from "not enough states"; the report gestures at it ("likely also due to the smaller sample size") and does not do it. **In our own posts an MDE goes beside every null, which is exactly the gap this passage leaves.**

### Chapter 3, Finding 3 — the correlation that carries the chapter

> We find a very high correlation between human and AI education, i.e. the number of years of education required to understand a human prompt or the AI's response (countries: *r* = 0.925, *p* < 0.001, *N* = 117; US states: *r* = 0.928, *p* < 0.001, *N* = 50). This highlights the importance of skills and suggests that how humans prompt the AI determines how effective it can be. This also highlights the importance of model design and training. While Claude is able to respond in a highly sophisticated manner, it tends to do so only when users input sophisticated prompts.

> How models are trained, fine-tuned and instructed affects how they respond to users. For example, one AI model could have a system prompt that instructs it to always use simple language that a middle school student could understand, whereas another AI model may only respond in complex language that would require a PhD education to understand. For Claude, we observe a more dynamic pattern where how the user prompts Claude relates to how Claude responds.

**Annotation.** The only place in the report where a correlation is reported with its coefficient, its p-value and its N, twice — and it is the one finding that replicates cleanly at both levels. The statistical furniture appears exactly where the claim is strongest, which is the right way round.

The interpretation is then walked back within four sentences, and the walk-back is the interesting part. "suggests that how humans prompt the AI determines how effective it can be" is a causal reading of a correlation; the next two sentences supply the rival reading — that the *model's* training determines the mapping — and make it concrete with a two-model thought experiment (a middle-school system prompt against a PhD one). The finding is then restated in the weakest form both readings support: "For Claude, we observe a more dynamic pattern where how the user prompts Claude relates to how Claude responds." Note the verb downgrade from "determines" to "relates to" across one paragraph break, and note the naming: the causal sentence says AI, the restatement says Claude. **The general claim takes AI; the claim the data supports takes Claude.**

The caveat a referee raises first is not raised at all: both education measures come from the same classifier reading the same transcript, so a correlation of 0.93 between them is partly a property of the instrument. The report's own Chapter 2 warning ("Claude.ai may somewhat underestimate the human education years needed for many tasks") applies to both series in the same direction, and nothing in Chapter 3 says so.

### Chapter 4, Finding 1 — the speedup–reliability tradeoff

> Our estimates suggest that, in general, the more complex tasks in our data yield a greater time savings (or "speedup") from AI. We derive this by having Claude estimate both how long a task would take a human working alone and the duration when human and AI work together, which we validated in previous work. Speedup is then the human-alone time divided by the human-with-AI time. So reducing a 1 hour task to 10 minutes would give a 6x speedup.

> The left panel of Figure 4.1 below gives the average speedup against our core measure of task complexity, the human years of schooling required to understand the inputs, all at the O\*NET task level.[1] It shows that in Claude.ai conversations, for example, prompts requiring 12 years of schooling (a high school education) enjoy a speedup of 9x, while those requiring 16 years of schooling (a college degree) attain a 12x speedup. This implies that productivity gains are more pronounced for use cases requiring higher human capital, consistent with evidence that white collar workers are far more likely to adopt AI (e.g., Bick et al 2025).

> Throughout the range of task complexity, the speedup is higher for API users. This could reflect the nature of the API data, which is restricted to single-turn interactions, and that API tasks have been specifically selected for automation.

> The results also capture a tradeoff, however. More complex tasks have a lower task success rate, as shown in the panel on the right. On Claude.ai, for example, tasks requiring less than a high school education (e.g., answering basic questions about products) attain a 70% success rate, but this drops to 66% for college-level conversations like developing analysis plans. Still, accounting for the difference in success rates—by either excluding low-success tasks or discounting speedups by success probability—does not eliminate the education gradient: complex tasks still show greater net productivity gains.

> One way to examine the implications of the education gradient is to look at the share of automation across the education levels required to understand the inputs. If high-education tasks show relatively more automation, it could signal more exposure for white collar workers. Here, though, the message is unclear: the automation share is essentially unrelated to the human levels of education required to write the prompt (Appendix Figure A.1).[2] On both Claude.ai and 1P API, tasks across education levels show automation patterns in roughly equal shares.

**Annotation.** A derived quantity is defined and then immediately made concrete with an arithmetic example in units nobody can misread: "Speedup is then the human-alone time divided by the human-with-AI time. So reducing a 1 hour task to 10 minutes would give a 6x speedup." Two sentences, formula and instance. The gradient is then read off the exhibit at two named points on the x-axis with their plain-English glosses attached ("12 years of schooling (a high school education) … 9x", "16 years of schooling (a college degree) … 12x"), which is how to quote a fitted relationship without quoting a coefficient.

Three caveats sit inside the finding rather than after it. The cross-platform difference is reported and then *explained away twice* in the same sentence ("This could reflect the nature of the API data, which is restricted to single-turn interactions, and that API tasks have been specifically selected for automation") — two competing explanations for one gap, neither chosen. The tradeoff is introduced with a paragraph-opening self-correction ("The results also capture a tradeoff, however") of the kind September used for "But not all API usage is for coding". And then the robustness check is reported with its result: two alternative treatments of success rates, both leaving the gradient intact ("does not eliminate the education gradient"). Reporting *which* alternatives were tried, in the sentence that reports they did not change the answer, is what makes a robustness sentence worth reading.

The null in the last paragraph is the model of how to report one. The question is posed as a design ("One way to examine the implications … is to look at the share of automation across the education levels"), the stake is stated ("If high-education tasks show relatively more automation, it could signal more exposure for white collar workers"), the answer is given as a null in plain words ("the message is unclear: the automation share is essentially unrelated to…"), and the null is then stated once more at the level it holds ("On both Claude.ai and 1P API, tasks across education levels show automation patterns in roughly equal shares"). No MDE. And the exhibit that would let a reader check it is Appendix Figure A.1, which is not in the document.

### Chapter 4, Finding 2 — the effective task horizon

> Recent work on AI "task horizons" (Kwa et al., 2025) finds that AI success rates decline with task duration: longer tasks are harder for models to complete. With each successive model generation, however, this decline has become shallower as models succeed on increasingly long tasks. METR operationalizes task horizon primarily as the maximum duration at which a model achieves at least 50% success, and growth in this metric has become a key indicator of AI progress.

> Figure 4.3 shows a similar measure using our primitives. The plot shows task-level success rates against the human time required, all at the O\*NET task level. In the API data, success rates drop from around 60% for sub-hour tasks to roughly 45% for tasks estimated to take humans 5+ hours. The fitted line crosses the horizontal 50% success line at 3.5 hours, suggesting that API calls attain a 50% success rate for tasks that are 3.5 hours. The analogous time estimate in METR's software engineering benchmark is 2 hours for Sonnet 4.5 and about 5 hours for Opus 4.5. (The data in this report predates the release of Opus 4.5.)

> Claude.ai data tells a different story. Success rates decline far slower as a function of task length. Extrapolating using the linear fit, Claude.ai would hit a 50% success rate at about 19 hours. This may reflect how multi-turn conversation effectively breaks complex tasks into smaller steps, with each turn providing a feedback loop that allows users to correct course.

> It's worth noting that a fundamental difference from the METR setting is selection. METR constructs a benchmark where a fixed set of tasks is assigned to models. In our data, users choose which tasks to bring to Claude. This means observed success rates reflect not just model capability but also user judgment about what will work, the cost of setting up the problem for Claude, and the expected time savings if the task succeeds.

> If users avoid tasks they expect to fail, for example, observed success rates will overstate true capability on the full distribution of potential tasks. This selection likely operates on both platforms, but in different ways: API customers select for tasks amenable to automation, while Claude.ai users select for tasks that could benefit from iteration. Also due to this selection effect, there's no guarantee that more performant models would show improvement in this plot, because users may respond to new models by providing more challenging presentations of otherwise similar O\*NET tasks.

> Controlled benchmarks like METR's measure the frontier of autonomous capability. Our real-world data can measure the *effective* task horizon, reflecting a mix of model capabilities and user behavior, and expanding beyond coding tasks. Both approaches find that AI can be effective for tasks requiring hours of human work.

**Annotation.** The passage to point at whenever someone asks how to compare your own measure to an established benchmark. Six paragraphs, and the order is the argument: the outside measure is described on its own terms first, including how it is operationalised ("the maximum duration at which a model achieves at least 50% success"); the report's analogue is constructed against the same threshold so the two are commensurable; both values are given (3.5 hours for the API, about 19 hours for Claude.ai) alongside both of METR's (2 hours for Sonnet 4.5, about 5 hours for Opus 4.5); the model-generation mismatch is disclosed in a parenthesis ("The data in this report predates the release of Opus 4.5"); the divergence between the two Anthropic numbers is given a mechanism, hedged ("This may reflect how multi-turn conversation effectively breaks complex tasks into smaller steps"); and then **two full paragraphs are spent on the reason the comparison is not apples to apples.**

Those two paragraphs are the best limitation writing in the report and they are in the body, beside the number, not in a footnote. Selection is named, its direction is given ("observed success rates will overstate true capability on the full distribution of potential tasks"), its presence on both platforms is asserted with the two mechanisms distinguished ("API customers select for tasks amenable to automation, while Claude.ai users select for tasks that could benefit from iteration"), and then the consequence that most authors would have left out is stated: the measure may not improve when the models do, because users will respond by bringing harder problems. A measure whose own designers tell you it may be insensitive to the thing it appears to track is a measure you can trust further than one that does not.

The closing paragraph does the naming work: METR measures "the frontier of autonomous capability", this measures "the *effective* task horizon", and the italic on *effective* is the whole distinction. The two are then reconciled on the one point where they agree ("Both approaches find that AI can be effective for tasks requiring hours of human work") rather than ranked. Note also that the 19-hour figure is explicitly labelled an extrapolation ("Extrapolating using the linear fit"), which matters because it lies far outside the range of the binned data.

### Chapter 4, Finding 3 — effective AI coverage

> Our earlier work found that 36% of jobs had AI usage for at least a quarter of their tasks, with about 4% reaching 75% task coverage. This measure was based only on the appearance of a task in our data, however. The primitives introduced in this report can help better characterize how AI is changing the work content of occupations.[3]

> First, we find that task coverage is increasing. Combining across reports, 49% of jobs have seen AI usage for at least a quarter of their tasks. But incorporating that task's share of the job, and Claude's average success rate, suggests a different set of affected occupations.

> We define effective AI coverage as the percent of a worker's day that can be performed successfully by Claude. It's calculated as the weighted sum of task success rates, where each task's weight is its share of the worker's time adjusted by how frequently the task occurs. The success rate comes from our primitives, the hours estimate from our previous work on productivity effects, and the frequency estimate from O\*NET data, where surveyed workers indicate how often they perform the task.

> The plot below shows how the effective AI coverage (y-axis) differs from task coverage alone (x-axis). The two are highly correlated, but with key differences. On the right side of the plot, occupations with high coverage—where almost all tasks appear with some frequency in Claude data—generally fall below the 45-degree line. This suggests that even 90% task coverage does not necessarily indicate large job impacts, since Claude may fail on key covered tasks or miss the most time-intensive ones.

> Zooming in, several occupations show large differences in effective AI coverage compared to task coverage. For example, data entry workers have one of the highest effective AI coverage. This is because although only two of their nine tasks are covered, their largest task—reading and entering data from source documents—has high success rates with Claude. AI excels at what they spend most of their time doing.

> Medical transcriptionists and radiologists also move up because their covered tasks happen to be their most time-intensive and highest-frequency work. For radiologists, their top two tasks—interpreting diagnostic images and preparing interpretive reports—have high success rates. These occupations have low task coverage because AI can't do the hands-on or administrative work in their job profiles, but it succeeds on the core knowledge work that dominates their workday.

> Microbiologists fall below the 45-degree line, suggesting lower effective AI coverage than would be predicted by task coverage alone. Claude covers half of their tasks, but not their most time-intensive: hands-on research using specialized lab equipment.

> This measure arguably gives a more realistic picture of job-level AI penetration. However, its implications depend on how often these Claude conversations actually displace or augment work that would otherwise be done by humans. For data entry clerks, AI likely does substitute for tasks previously performed manually. But when a Claude conversation maps to a teacher performing a lecture, it is less clear how this translates to reduced lecture time on the job. In future work, we could leverage our 1P API data to understand which of these tasks are being integrated into production workflows.

**Annotation.** The construction is given in three sentences and the *provenance of each input is named separately*: "The success rate comes from our primitives, the hours estimate from our previous work on productivity effects, and the frequency estimate from O\*NET data, where surveyed workers indicate how often they perform the task." Three sources, three clauses, one sentence. A composite measure whose components come from three places needs exactly this sentence, and most papers put it in an appendix.

The prior number is updated before the new measure is introduced, cleanly and with the reason for the change attached: 36% of jobs at quarter-coverage in the earlier work, 49% "combining across reports", and the update is attributed to accumulated coverage rather than to a change in method. Then the new measure is compared against the old one *on the same plot* with the 45-degree line as the null — the same "state the value that would be unremarkable" device as β = 0.99 and AUI = 1.

The worked cases are the finding. Four occupations, each with its own arithmetic stated in words: data entry workers ("although only two of their nine tasks are covered, their largest task … has high success rates"), medical transcriptionists and radiologists ("their covered tasks happen to be their most time-intensive and highest-frequency work"; "their top two tasks—interpreting diagnostic images and preparing interpretive reports—have high success rates"), microbiologists ("Claude covers half of their tasks, but not their most time-intensive: hands-on research using specialized lab equipment"). Two cases above the line and one below, so the measure is shown to move in both directions. This is how a measure is demonstrated rather than asserted.

The closing paragraph is a limitation of the kind a referee would raise first and it is placed at the end of the section, unhedged about what it concedes: coverage is not displacement. "when a Claude conversation maps to a teacher performing a lecture, it is less clear how this translates to reduced lecture time on the job" — one concrete counter-case is worth more than the abstract statement, and the report gives both. The next step is then named as a specific analysis rather than as "future research": use the 1P API data to see which tasks are in production workflows.

The naming discipline slips here and it is worth marking, because this section is where it slips worst. The measure is "effective **AI** coverage" but "the percent of a worker's day that can be performed successfully by **Claude**" — that pairing is correct and matches September's AUI. But then: "**AI** excels at what they spend most of their time doing"; "these occupations have low task coverage because **AI** can't do the hands-on or administrative work"; "For data entry clerks, **AI** likely does substitute for tasks previously performed manually." Three sentences in which the subject of a capability claim measured on Claude.ai data is AI. **Our posts say Claude in all three.**

### Chapter 4, Finding 4 — the task-removal exercise

> Beyond how much of a worker's day AI can successfully perform, a separate question is which tasks get covered, and whether those tend to be the high-skill or low-skill components of the job. Recent research has studied changes in the task mix within jobs to understand AI's impact on wages and employment (Autor and Thompson 2025; Hampole et al 2025). A key insight is that automation's effects depend not just on how many tasks are covered, but on which tasks.

> To see how jobs change when we remove the tasks AI can perform, we first construct a measure of the level of skill required for each task. O\*NET doesn't provide task-level education requirements, so we train a model that predicts years of schooling from task embeddings, using the BLS's occupation-level education as the target.[4] This way, a low-education occupation may still have a high-skill task if it looks like those that tend to exist in high-education occupations. For example, Legal Secretaries is a 12-year education occupation, but the task "Review legal publications and perform database searches to identify laws and court decisions relevant to pending cases" is predicted to require 17.7 years because it resembles tasks typically performed by lawyers and paralegals.

> The data shows that Claude tends to cover tasks that require higher levels of education. The mean predicted education for tasks in the economy is 13.2 years. For tasks that we see in our data, the mean prediction is about a year higher, 14.4 years (corresponding to an Associate's degree). This aligns with the occupation-level results from earlier reports, showing more Claude usage among white collar occupations.

> We next calculate how removing AI-covered tasks shifts the average education level of what remains. Overall, the net first-order impact is to deskill jobs, since AI removes tasks that require relatively higher levels of education. One job that experiences such deskilling is technical writers, which loses tasks like "Analyze developments in specific field to determine need for revisions" (18.7 years) and "Review published materials and recommend revisions or changes in scope, format" (16.4 years), leaving tasks like "Draw sketches to illustrate specified materials" (13.6 years) and "Observe production, developmental, and experimental activities" (13.5 years).

> These patterns illustrate how jobs may evolve over the coming years as their task content adjusts in response to AI. If the education level can be interpreted like expertise in Autor and Thompson's analysis, their framework might predict that wages will fall and employment will increase for technical writers and travel agents; conversely, real estate managers will specialize in complex negotiations and stakeholder management, shrinking employment while increasing wages.[5]

> However, our education-based measure differs from Autor and Thompson's expertise concept: their framework would label some tasks as high expertise where ours specifies low education—for example, the Electrician task "Connect wires to circuit breakers, transformers, or other components." And these predictions are based on current Claude usage patterns, which will shift as models are trained on new capabilities and users discover new applications—potentially changing which tasks are covered and whether the net effect is deskilling or upskilling.

And the footnote that puts a contrary literature beside the prediction:

> [5] On the other hand, some historical evidence suggests that when technologies automating job tasks appear in patent data, employment and wages subsequently fall for exposed occupations (Webb 2020).

**Annotation.** A constructed variable, built because the off-the-shelf one does not exist, with the construction, the estimator and the validating instance all given: O\*NET has no task-level education, so schooling is predicted from task embeddings against BLS occupation-level education (footnote 4: "a pretrained sentence transformer (all-mpnet-base-v2)" and "Ridge regression"), and then the measure is demonstrated on a case where it does something a cruder measure could not — a 12-year occupation containing a 17.7-year task. **When a measure is built rather than taken, show it working on one case where the naive alternative fails.**

The finding is then given as a difference of means with the baseline first: 13.2 years across the economy, 14.4 years for tasks seen in the data, and the gap characterised before it is quantified ("about a year higher"). The educational unit is glossed ("corresponding to an Associate's degree") so the gap means something to a reader who does not think in years of schooling.

The deskilling result is shown rather than stated: for each occupation, the tasks that leave and the tasks that remain, each with its predicted years, so the reader can see the mechanism arithmetic. And it runs in both directions — technical writers, travel agents and teaching professions down; real estate managers up.

Then three separate brakes are applied to the same claim, in three consecutive sentences. The prediction is conditional on borrowing someone else's framework ("**If** the education level can be interpreted like expertise in Autor and Thompson's analysis, their framework **might** predict…"). The borrowing is then explicitly limited with a counter-example that shows where the two concepts come apart ("their framework would label some tasks as high expertise where ours specifies low education—for example, the Electrician task 'Connect wires to circuit breakers, transformers, or other components.'"). And the whole exercise is scoped to the present ("these predictions are based on current Claude usage patterns, which will shift … potentially changing which tasks are covered and whether the net effect is deskilling or upskilling") — a sentence that concedes the *sign* of the headline result could reverse. Footnote 5 then cites evidence pointing the other way, unresolved. **Four brakes on one prediction, each of a different kind: conditional antecedent, concept mismatch with an instance, temporal scope, contrary literature. That is the anatomy of a well-caveated projection.**

### Chapter 4, Finding 5 — the productivity estimate revised downward

> In earlier work, we estimated that widespread adoption of AI could increase US labor productivity growth by 1.8 percentage points annually over the next decade. Here we revisit that analysis, incorporating the task success primitive introduced in this report and a richer treatment of task complementarity.

> Based on the speedups associated with tasks with at least 200 observations in our sample of 1M Claude.ai conversations,[6] we replicate our previous finding that current-generation AI models and current usage patterns imply a productivity effect of 1.8 percentage points per year over the next decade.[7]

> With the inclusion of 1P API data, we can assess whether implied labor productivity effects differ based on enterprise Claude deployment patterns. Two countervailing forces are at play: API usage is more concentrated in a narrower set of tasks and occupations (particularly coding-related work), which would tend to reduce implied effects; but task-level speedups are higher on average among API tasks, as implied by Figure 4.1. These forces largely offset: the API sample likewise implies a 1.8 percentage point increase in labor productivity over the next decade.

> A salient critique of this analysis is that it fails to account for model reliability. If workers must validate AI output, the productivity benefits will be smaller than raw speedups suggest. To assess how quantitatively important this channel might be, we incorporate the task success primitive introduced in this report, multiplying task-level time savings by task-specific success rates before aggregating.[8]

> This adjustment has a meaningful effect: implied productivity growth falls from 1.8 to 1.2 percentage points per year for the next decade based on Claude.ai usage, and to 1.0 percentage points for API traffic. Yet, even after accounting for reliability, the implied impact remains economically significant—a sustained increase of 1.0 percentage point per year for the next ten years would return US productivity growth to rates that prevailed in the late 1990s and early 2000s.

> A second critique concerns task complementarity. If some tasks are essential and cannot easily be substituted, then overall productivity effects will be constrained regardless of speedups on other tasks. Teachers may prepare lesson plans more efficiently with AI while having no impact on time spent with students in the classroom.

> The key parameter is the elasticity of substitution across tasks, σ. When the elasticity of substitution is less than one, tasks are complements and those tasks that are not sped up by AI become bottlenecks for broader productivity gains. Alternatively, when the elasticity of substitution is greater than one, then workers can allocate toward the more productive tasks—thereby amplifying the overall time savings at the occupational level. An elasticity of substitution equal to one is a special case that replicates the main analysis above.

> When tasks are complements, however, the implied aggregate labor productivity impact declines sharply as the economic effects are bottlenecked by tasks that AI speeds up the least. For example, at σ =0.5 the implied overall labor productivity effect is 0.7–0.9 percentage points per year—around half the size as implied by our baseline estimates. Additionally adjusting for task success further reduces the implied productivity effects to 0.8pp for Claude.ai and 0.6pp for API.

> On the other hand, when the elasticity of substitution is greater than one, the implied labor productivity based on pre-Opus 4.5 usage patterns is materially higher. For example, at σ =1.5 the implied labor productivity effect rises to 2.2–2.6 percentage points per year, consistent with greater specialization in tasks where AI provides the largest speedups.

> In both cases the implied productivity impact based on API traffic is more responsive to the degree of task substitutability. This is consistent with the fact that there is a larger share of API traffic concentrated in fewer tasks and associated occupations as compared to Claude.ai: When tasks are complements, this concentration amplifies the bottleneck problem; when they are substitutes, it amplifies productivity gains from task specialization.

> What this analysis shows is that the productivity effects of automation may ultimately be constrained by bottleneck tasks that elude AI automation for the time being. And the labor market implications of increasingly capable AI could be similarly affected by such forces. For example, Gans and Goldfarb (2026) argue that the presence of bottleneck tasks within jobs means that partial AI automation can lead to an increase in labor income as such tasks increase in economic value (at least until a job is *entirely* automated).

And the footnote that makes the replication checkable, including what happens when the threshold is dropped:

> [6] When we first assessed the aggregate productivity implications of Claude usage, we relied on a sample of 100k Claude.ai conversations from Fall 2025. Based on the set of tasks for which we observed speedups, we estimated that labor productivity could be 1.8 percentage points higher per year over the next decade. Expanding the sample to 1M observations means that we need to take a stand on how to handle very infrequently occurring tasks—which are very common given that usage follows a power law, as we documented in our past report. We choose a threshold of 0.02% because it replicates our previous results for our sample of Claude.ai conversations. For privacy-preserving reasons, we only ever analyze tasks with at least 15 observations, or an implied threshold of 0.015% for a 100k sample. And so our results are internally consistent across samples. If we do not impose a restriction on our 1M sample and assume that efficiency gains for any task in our sample, even those with just 15 observations out of one million, the implied aggregate labor productivity growth over the next decade would be roughly 5% percentage points per year—a mechanical increase based on a the much larger set of tasks included.

**Annotation.** The structure is *replicate, then let the critiques move the number*, and it is the report's strongest sequence.

The prior estimate is restated, reproduced on the new sample before anything is changed ("we replicate our previous finding … 1.8 percentage points per year"), and reproduced a second time on an independent sample with the two offsetting reasons named in advance ("Two countervailing forces are at play … These forces largely offset"). Only then are the critiques admitted. Each is introduced in the voice of the critic — "A salient critique of this analysis is that it fails to account for model reliability"; "A second critique concerns task complementarity" — stated in one sentence of mechanism, and then *answered with a number that is worse for the report*: 1.8 → 1.2 (Claude.ai) and 1.0 (API) for reliability, and 1.8 → 0.7–0.9 at σ = 0.5, or 0.8pp and 0.6pp with both adjustments. The revised number is then made meaningful in units a reader already has ("a sustained increase of 1.0 percentage point per year for the next ten years would return US productivity growth to rates that prevailed in the late 1990s and early 2000s") rather than being defended as still large.

The parameter sweep is the right way to handle an unidentified structural parameter: the key parameter is named, its two regimes are explained in plain words before any value is quoted, the value that reproduces the baseline is identified (σ = 1), and results are reported on both sides of it — 0.7–0.9pp at σ = 0.5, 2.2–2.6pp at σ = 1.5. The report does not pick a σ. It reports the sensitivity and leaves the choice with the reader, and then points to the one structural asymmetry the sweep reveals ("the implied productivity impact based on API traffic is more responsive to the degree of task substitutability"), with the reason given.

Footnote 6 is the most credibility-earning paragraph in the report and is worth reading in full for one sentence: "If we do not impose a restriction on our 1M sample … the implied aggregate labor productivity growth over the next decade would be roughly 5% percentage points per year—a mechanical increase based on a the much larger set of tasks included." The authors disclose the specification that would have tripled their own headline, name it as mechanical, and say why the threshold they chose was chosen ("because it replicates our previous results"). That last clause is also the footnote's vulnerability, and a referee would say so: a threshold selected to reproduce a prior result is not an independent replication of it, and the footnote's own phrase "internally consistent across samples" is the honest description. (Two typos survive in it: "5% percentage points" and "based on a the much larger set".)

### The report's hedge vocabulary, graded

Collected from the passages above, in descending confidence: *we find* · *we document* · *the data shows* · *we replicate* · *we observe* · *reveals* · *indicating that* · *implies that* · *suggests* · *appears to* · *tends to* · *is associated with* · *may reflect* · *could reflect* · *might predict* · *likely* · *perhaps* · *we don't know if* · *the message is unclear* · *considerable uncertainty remains* · *we will revisit this question … in future reports*. As in the third report, two rungs are almost never used for one claim, and neither *shows* nor *proves* is used of a causal claim. New in this wave, and specific to a report whose measures are model-generated: a whole register of **attribution** verbs that sit outside the confidence ladder — *Claude estimates*, *Claude assesses*, *Claude classifies*, *are estimated to*, *predicted to require*, *Claude's assessment of*. These are not hedges. They name the instrument. A sentence can be fully confident and still take one.

### Claude versus AI across the whole report

- **Title:** "…Economic Primitives" — neither word. **Chapter titles:** "What has changed since our last report" (neither), "Introducing economic primitives" (neither), "How **Claude** is used varies by geography", "Tasks and productivity" (neither).
- **Introduction:** mixed by design. The framing says AI ("This report introduces new metrics of **AI** usage", "five dimensions relevant to **AI**'s economic impact", "how **AI** is currently impacting the economy"); every measured quantity says Claude ("**Claude** usage remains concentrated", "the top 10 most common tasks account for 24% of our sampled conversations", "**Claude**'s success rate falls").
- **Inside a single bullet:** "The tasks we observe in **Claude** usage tend to require more education than those in the broader economy. If we assume that **AI**-assisted tasks diminish as a share of worker responsibilities, removing them would leave behind less-skilled work." Observation takes Claude; the counterfactual takes AI.
- **The primitives:** one of the five is named "**AI** autonomy" and is defined as "the degree to which users delegate decision-making to **Claude**". "**AI** education" is the years of education needed to understand "**Claude**'s responses". Same construction as the AUI: the name says AI, the measurement says Claude.
- **Chapter 3** holds the line almost perfectly. Every AUI value, every use-case share, every correlation is attached to Claude; the two sentences that generalise say AI ("higher income countries use **AI** more as an assistant and collaborator", "Economic and institutional context … are related to how **AI** is being used").
- **Chapter 4 breaks it.** "**AI** excels at what they spend most of their time doing"; "these occupations have low task coverage because **AI** can't do the hands-on or administrative work"; "For data entry clerks, **AI** likely does substitute for tasks previously performed manually"; "**AI** removes tasks that require relatively higher levels of education"; "**AI** covers tasks like 'Plan, describe, arrange, and sell itinerary tour packages'"; "the tasks **AI** handles are often the more skilled components of a job"; "49% of jobs have seen **AI** usage for at least a quarter of their tasks". Every one of these is a statement about what was observed in Claude.ai and 1P API data, and every one says AI.
- **Captions:** Claude in 1.1–1.8, 2.1, 2.2, 3.1–3.5; "**AI** autonomy" in 4.2 (the primitive's name); "Effective **AI** coverage tracks the share of a worker's time-weighted duties that **AI** could successfully perform, based on **Claude.ai** data" in 4.4 (name says AI, sample says Claude — the correct form); "Implied labor productivity effect from **AI**" in 4.6, where the underlying speedups are Claude's.
- **Concluding Remarks:** "how **AI** will reshape economies and labor markets" (phenomenon) beside "**Claude** tends to be used more … on tasks that require higher education" (measurement). Correct.

The rule the third report kept throughout, this one keeps in three chapters out of four. **The corpus finding stands and is sharpened by the exception: the question, the title, the stakes and the policy sentence say AI; the sample, the index, the numbers and the captions say Claude — and Chapter 4 of the fourth report is the counter-example to hold a draft against.**

### Across waves of the same series

> As compared to nearly one year ago, consumer usage on Claude.ai is modestly more concentrated: The share of conversations assigned to the ten most prevalent O\*NET tasks was 24% in November 2025, 1pp higher than in August and up from 21% in January 2025.

> The ten most common tasks grew from 28% of API records in August to 32% in November.

> Such usage has risen steadily since our first report, up from 9% of conversations on Claude.ai in January 2025 to 15% in November.

> Such dominance has subsided on Claude.ai: the share of conversations on Claude.ai assigned to such (mostly) coding-related tasks is down from a peak of 40% in March 2025 to 34% in November 2025. At the same time, the share of transcripts assigned to computer and mathematical tasks among 1P API traffic edged higher from 44% in August to 46% in November 2025 (Figure 1.2).

> This jump in the prevalence of design- and writing-related tasks reversed a steady decline across earlier reports.

> Perhaps the most notable development for API customers was the increase in the share of transcripts associated with Office and Administrative Support related tasks, which rose 3pp in August to 13% in November 2025.

**Annotation.** The wave labels of the third report (V1/V2/V3) have been almost entirely replaced by **calendar dates** in the body prose — "January 2025", "March 2025", "August 2025", "November 2025" — and survive only in the Figure 1.8 caption ("August 2025 (V3) and November 2025 (V4)"). That is the right direction of travel for a series that now has four waves and irregular gaps: a reader cannot infer from "V2 to V3" that eight months elapsed, but "August to November" carries its own denominator. Every magnitude here is quoted against at least two reference points, and where a series is not monotone the report says which point is the extremum ("down from a **peak** of 40% in March 2025"), so a two-point comparison cannot be mistaken for a trend.

Two forms to copy. "This jump … reversed a steady decline across earlier reports" — a direction change stated as a change in the *series*, which is a claim only a fourth wave can make and is the compounding return on keeping definitions stable. And "1pp higher than in August and up from 21% in January 2025" — the same number given against the previous wave and against the first wave in one clause, so the reader sees both the noise scale and the trend in the same breath. One drafting tell: "rose 3pp in August to 13% in November 2025" should read "from its August level"; as written the preposition puts the rise in the wrong month.

### The passage that revises an earlier hypothesis in public

> In our previous report we noted that automated use had risen to exceed augmented use on Claude.ai, perhaps capturing both improving capabilities and greater familiarity among users with LLMs. Data from November 2025 points to a broad-based shift back toward augmented use on Claude.ai: The share of conversations classified as augmented jumped 5pp to 52% and the share deemed automated fell 4pp to 45%.

> Nevertheless, the automation share was still elevated as compared to nearly one year ago when we first began tracking this measure, suggesting that the underlying trend is still toward greater automation even as the August spike overstated how quickly it was materializing.

> Globally, Claude usage per capita—as captured by the Anthropic AI Usage Index (AUI)—remains highly uneven and strongly correlated with GDP. These gaps are stable: we see no evidence that low-use countries are catching up or that high-use countries are pulling away.

> This mirrors a finding from our 3rd Economic Index report where countries with higher Anthropic AI Usage Index tend to use Claude in a more collaborative manner (augmentation), rather than letting it operate independently (automation).

> Our earlier work found that 36% of jobs had AI usage for at least a quarter of their tasks, with about 4% reaching 75% task coverage. This measure was based only on the appearance of a task in our data, however.

> In earlier work, we estimated that widespread adoption of AI could increase US labor productivity growth by 1.8 percentage points annually over the next decade. Here we revisit that analysis, incorporating the task success primitive introduced in this report and a richer treatment of task complementarity.

**Annotation.** Four distinct relationships to the report's own past, and the grammar differs for each — which is the pattern worth naming, because a series that only ever "confirms" or "corrects" has two moves and this one has four.

1. **Revised.** The automation crossover. The prior claim is restated *with its prior interpretation attached* ("perhaps capturing both improving capabilities and greater familiarity"), the new number is given, and the revision is made by separating trend from level rather than by withdrawing anything. "the August spike overstated how quickly it was materializing" is the whole retraction, and it retracts a rate, not a direction.
2. **Replicated.** The AUI–GDP relationship ("we replicate the finding from our prior report that GDP is strongly correlated with the AUI"; "a 1% increase in GDP per capita is associated with a 0.7% increase in Claude usage per capita" — the identical elasticity to September's 0.7) and the augmentation-with-adoption gradient ("This mirrors a finding from our 3rd Economic Index report"). Note that a replication is reported as a replication, in one clause, without being re-argued.
3. **Superseded by a better measure.** Task coverage: the old number is given, then the reason it was inadequate ("This measure was based only on the appearance of a task in our data, however"), then the replacement. The old measure is not called wrong; it is called incomplete, and the sentence that says so specifies in what respect.
4. **Re-estimated under a critique.** The 1.8pp productivity figure, reproduced first and then moved down by adjustments the report imports from its critics.
5. **And one thing explicitly *not* found to have changed.** "These gaps are stable: we see no evidence that low-use countries are catching up or that high-use countries are pulling away." A null across waves, stated in the Overview as one of four headline observations. A series that reports its stable quantities as findings is a series whose moving quantities can be believed.

### Countries against US states

> Within the US, income is less clearly a predictor of usage. Instead, what appears to matter most is the composition of each state's workforce and how well-matched the workforce is to Claude capabilities as reflected in task-level usage.

> Indeed, the left panel of Figure 1.6 shows that the AUI concentration across countries was essentially unchanged between our last report and this report.

> By contrast, usage became more evenly distributed across US states from August to November 2025: the Gini coefficient, a standard measure of equality, fell from 0.37 to 0.32.

> However, the relationship between AUI and the primitives often differs between country and US state level. For example, at the country level, the AUI correlates negatively with the time it would take a human to complete a task without AI, and with how much decision-making autonomy AI is given. At the US state level, these relationships are not statistically significant–likely also due to the smaller sample size for US states.

> Interestingly, we observe that task success is negatively associated with human education at the country level, but positively related at the US state level.

> This relationship is not significant at the US state level, perhaps because income variation and use case diversity are more limited within the United States than globally.

> Other relationships depend on context. At the country level, higher usage correlates with shorter tasks and less AI autonomy; within the US, these patterns do not hold. Task success and human education show opposite relationships globally versus within the US.

**Annotation.** The comparison of two levels is the finding, in three different ways, and the report is careful to give each its own explanation rather than one blanket excuse. Concentration is stable globally and falling within the US, on the same statistic (the AUI) at two geographies — and the contrast is carried by a single "By contrast" with two Gini values against a Lorenz curve that "was essentially unchanged". Income predicts globally and does not predict within the US, with a substitute predictor supplied ("the composition of each state's workforce"). And several primitive relationships hold globally and vanish within the US, with two candidate reasons offered at different points and neither asserted: statistical power ("likely also due to the smaller sample size for US states") and restricted variation ("perhaps because income variation and use case diversity are more limited within the United States than globally").

The task-success–education reversal is the strongest version because both signs are significant somewhere and the report refuses to pick: "the relationship pattern at one level of observation (country) contradicts the relationship pattern at another level (US state)". **One claim tested at two levels, with the disagreement between the levels reported as the result rather than resolved in favour of the convenient level, is the house standard, and this is the corpus's best example of it.** The chapter's Conclusion then restates all three contrasts in three sentences without adding a number — which is how a multi-level finding is compressed without being softened.

### Claude.ai against the 1P API

> Overall, API usage is overwhelmingly work-related (74% vs. 46%) and directive (64% vs. 32%), with three-quarters of interactions classified as automation compared to less than half on Claude.ai (see Figure 1.3).

> Claude.ai users, by contrast, engage in more back-and-forth: task iteration and learning modes are far more common, and tasks tend to be more lengthy—both in terms of human time with AI (15 minutes vs. 5 minutes) and the estimated time a human would need to complete the task alone (3.1 hours vs. 1.7 hours). Claude.ai also shows higher task success rates (67% vs. 49%), which may reflect the benefits of multi-turn conversation, where users can clarify, correct course, and iterate toward a solution. Claude.ai users also give the AI more autonomy on average, and are more likely to bring tasks they couldn't complete alone.

> These differences are also reflected in the occupational distribution of tasks. API usage is heavily concentrated in Computer & Mathematical tasks (52% vs. 36%), consistent with its use for programmatic, automation-friendly workflows like code generation and data processing. Office & Administrative tasks are also more prevalent in the API (15% vs. 8%), reflecting routine business operations suited to delegation. Claude.ai, by contrast, sees substantially more Educational Instruction tasks (16% vs. 4%)—coursework help, tutoring, and instructional material development—as well as more Arts, Design, and Entertainment tasks (11% vs. 6%). Claude.ai also has a longer tail of human-facing categories like Community & Social Service and Healthcare Practitioners, where users seek advice, counseling, or information on personal matters.

> These patterns suggest that 1P API deployments concentrate on tasks amenable to systematic automation, while Claude.ai serves a broader range of use cases including learning, creative work, and personal assistance.

> Part of this reflects the nature of the interaction: Claude.ai transcripts can include multi-turn conversations, while the API data we analyze is limited to single input-output pairs. This is because API requests arrive independently, with no metadata linking them to prior exchanges. This means we can only analyze them as isolated user-assistant pairs rather than full conversation trajectories.

> Throughout the range of task complexity, the speedup is higher for API users. This could reflect the nature of the API data, which is restricted to single-turn interactions, and that API tasks have been specifically selected for automation.

> In the API data, success rates drop from around 60% for sub-hour tasks to roughly 45% for tasks estimated to take humans 5+ hours. … Extrapolating using the linear fit, Claude.ai would hit a 50% success rate at about 19 hours.

> In both cases the implied productivity impact based on API traffic is more responsive to the degree of task substitutability.

**Annotation.** The spine of the report's cross-sample work, and the phrasing template is fixed: the two values in an "(X vs. Y)" parenthesis, API first when the section is about the API, Claude.ai first when it is not, with the direction word in the prose. Eight dimensions compared — use case, collaboration mode, task duration with and without AI, success rate, autonomy, occupational mix, task horizon, productivity sensitivity — and the discipline about what each comparison licenses is as tight as September's: mode differences support "amenable to systematic automation"; success-rate differences support the multi-turn mechanism; horizon differences support the *effective* horizon concept; substitutability differences support the concentration argument.

The move that makes the whole comparison trustworthy is placed before it, not after: **the confound is stated as a property of the data before any number is quoted.** "Claude.ai transcripts can include multi-turn conversations, while the API data we analyze is limited to single input-output pairs. This is because API requests arrive independently, with no metadata linking them to prior exchanges." Three sentences: the asymmetry, its cause, and its consequence for what can be analysed. Every subsequent Claude.ai-versus-API gap can then be read against it, and the report invokes it again twice where it matters most ("which may reflect the benefits of multi-turn conversation"; "This could reflect the nature of the API data, which is restricted to single-turn interactions"). A comparison between two samples that differ structurally needs exactly this paragraph, once, early.

One number to treat with care: "with three-quarters of interactions classified as automation compared to less than half on Claude.ai" cites Figure 1.3 rather than giving both values, and "directive (64% vs. 32%)" is the only place the API directive share appears. And the API's human-with-AI time is given as "5 minutes" against Claude.ai's "15 minutes" while Figure 2.2's exhibit gives Claude.ai's as 15.4 minutes — rounding, not disagreement, but the kind of thing a verification pass catches.

### Against external benchmarks

> Economically consequential technologies have historically taken around half a century to achieve full diffusion across the US (Kalanyi et al., 2025). By contrast, comparing Claude adoption rates in November 2025 to three months prior, we estimate that parity in adoption per capita across US states—as measured by the AUI—could be reached within 2–5 years.

> [3] See, for example, Kalanyi et al (2025): "Second, as the technologies mature and the number of related jobs grows, hiring spreads geographically. This process is very slow, taking around 50 years to disperse fully."

> METR operationalizes task horizon primarily as the maximum duration at which a model achieves at least 50% success, and growth in this metric has become a key indicator of AI progress. … The analogous time estimate in METR's software engineering benchmark is 2 hours for Sonnet 4.5 and about 5 hours for Opus 4.5.

> Interestingly, these findings converge with recent work by Microsoft showing that AI use for school is associated with lower per capita income, whereas AI use for leisure is associated with higher per capita income.

> Figure 2.1 shows that our human education measure correlates with actual worker education levels across occupations.

> This implies that productivity gains are more pronounced for use cases requiring higher human capital, consistent with evidence that white collar workers are far more likely to adopt AI (e.g., Bick et al 2025).

> A key insight is that automation's effects depend not just on how many tasks are covered, but on which tasks.

> For example, Gans and Goldfarb (2026) argue that the presence of bottleneck tasks within jobs means that partial AI automation can lead to an increase in labor income as such tasks increase in economic value (at least until a job is *entirely* automated).

**Annotation.** External work enters in five distinct roles and each has its own grammar, which is worth cataloguing because our own posts need all five.

(i) **As the benchmark a headline is measured against.** Kalyani et al.'s fifty years is what makes "2–5 years" a claim rather than a number, and the footnote quotes the source sentence verbatim so the reader can check that "full diffusion" means what the comparison needs it to mean. The comparison is then given a ratio in the Introduction ("roughly 10x faster"). (ii) **As the operational definition the report borrows to make its own measure commensurable** — METR's 50%-success threshold, adopted explicitly so that 3.5 hours and 19 hours can be set beside 2 and 5 hours. (iii) **As independent agreement**, reported as convergence and not as confirmation ("these findings converge with recent work by Microsoft"). (iv) **As validation of an instrument** — BLS/ACS occupational education against the human-education primitive, which is the only external check on any primitive in the report. (v) **As the framework whose predictions are borrowed under an explicit condition** — Autor and Thompson, with the borrowing limited by a counter-example, and Gans and Goldfarb, whose argument points the other way from the report's own deskilling result and is quoted for that reason.

The report's own reliance on external data is disclosed where the derived measure needs it: the frequency weights in effective AI coverage come "from O\*NET data, where surveyed workers indicate how often they perform the task", and the task-level education target is "the BLS's occupation-level education". Note that the citation practice in the PDF is bare author–year with no reference list anywhere in the 55 pages; a reader who wants Kalyani et al. must find it themselves, and the PDF spells the name wrong. **A post of ours cites the source; the PDF's practice here is below the standard the text otherwise sets.**

### Small vivid comparisons

**Annotation.** Nine devices, and unlike September's set — which mostly converted abstract quantities into holdable comparisons — most of these do a different job: they make a *definition* or a *limitation* concrete. The one that converts a number into meaning is the productivity sentence, and it does so by naming a period the reader remembers rather than by giving a multiple. **The transferable rule: a worked example is cheapest and most valuable where a definition or a caveat is doing the work, not where a headline is.**

### The caption template, distilled

For any exhibit in this series, as of the fourth report:

1. **Bold, numbered, sentence case.** Terminal period on the title: use one, and use it everywhere. This report does not, and the inconsistency is visible enough to be worth a rule.
2. **Title is a declarative finding where there is one to state** (3.2, 4.4), **descriptive where there is not** (1.1, 4.1, 4.3, 4.6). The report never puts a hedge in the title, which September's Figure 2.7 did to good effect; where a finding is noisy here it is simply left out of the title.
3. **Gloss, plain roman, one sentence per job**, in this order where applicable: exhibit type · what is plotted against what, with units · the unit of analysis, named explicitly ("at the O\*NET task level", "at the occupation level") · how any index, share or predicted variable was constructed, restated in full rather than cross-referenced · the sample, its inclusion floor **and the reason for the floor** · the coverage boundary, in the series' fixed words · panel logic · what colour encodes · the weighting scheme **and its purpose** · the full control set, enumerated, for any conditional estimate · what any line is — fit, null or threshold · the null or benchmark value the reader should compare against, with the familiar number attached · the coefficient's meaning in plain words, with "associated with" · where the full version lives, if the exhibit is an excerpt.
4. **Disclose what the exhibit is not** where its form invites a wrong inference (1.5's "not based on the words used in the underlying transcripts").
5. **No separate source line.** External provenance goes in a clause, with the series named by its published title (2.1).
6. **Say Claude for the sample, AI only in a measure's name** — and when both appear, pair them as Figure 4.4 does.
7. **Repeat verbatim rather than paraphrase.** The floor-with-its-reason sentence appears five times across Figures 3.1–3.5 — three times in its 200-observation form, once in its 100-observation form, once combined — and the coverage boundary "The underlying data includes Claude.ai Free, Pro and Max usage" appears five times, word for word; the diff between "Free and Pro" and "Free, Pro and Max" is the only notice the reader gets that the sample changed.
8. **Point to the text for a derivation** rather than compressing two pages into a caption (1.8, 4.6).

## Limitations

**There is no limitations section**, for the second wave running: no "Caveats", no "Limitations", no "Threats to validity" in 55 pages. But the dispersal is markedly better organised than in the third report, and in one respect the architecture has changed for the better and is worth copying.

### 1. A named sub-section on the limits of the new instrument, promised in the Introduction

> In the second chapter, we discuss the motivation for and introduce our new economic primitives, including how they were selected and operationalized, **and their limitations**.

> While we are very confident in the directional accuracy of the new measures (e.g., tasks with higher average years of education needed to understand the human prompt are likely more complex), none of the measures should be taken as exact or definitive (e.g., Claude.ai may somewhat underestimate the human education years needed for many tasks).

> We propose that multiple simple primitives, even if somewhat noisy and not perfectly accurate by themselves, can together provide important signals on how AI is being used. We therefore mainly tested for directional accuracy.

> Neither data sources are fully representative of Claude.ai or 1P API traffic, but they allow us to check that the classifiers are working on data that resembles real usage data, while ensuring privacy.

> We selected a final set of nine new classifiers for the five primitives, all of which are directionally accurate even if they may deviate somewhat from human ratings.

> Ultimately, the strongest validation will come from the primitives' ability to capture meaningful variation in labor market outcomes.

**Annotation.** The Introduction *promises* limitations and a named sub-section delivers them. That is the structural improvement over the third report, where a reader could not answer "what are the limitations of this work?" without reading all twenty-eight footnotes. Here the limits of the central new instrument are in one findable place, stated as bounds on use rather than as apologies, and every one is specific: directional not exact; a named direction of likely bias (education years underestimated); a non-representative validation set; deviation from human ratings conceded for all nine classifiers; and the validation that matters named as not yet done. **This is the form our posts should use for a constructed measure.** What it still lacks is any quantity — no agreement rate, no N, no accuracy — so the bound is qualitative throughout.

### 2. In the body, beside the finding

> This estimate comes with a high degree of uncertainty as the precision of our estimates cannot rule out much slower rates of diffusion.

> While this is consistent with rapid AI adoption and diffusion, this estimate comes with uncertainty given that it is based on a change observed over a three month period. Diffusion may ultimately proceed more slowly in the months and years to come.

> While it is important to exercise caution in interpreting short-run changes, this is a relatively large change toward perfect equality…

> A concern with estimating convergence this way is that our AUI estimates are subject to sampling noise and other variation unrelated to diffusion. This can produce classical attenuation bias: even if AUI is not actually changing, our estimate of β could end up meaningfully below one.

> However, these estimates are less precise, and only the former is statistically distinguishable from 1 at the 10% level.

> That said, our estimates are based on just three months of data. And while the 2SLS specification may help address sampling noise, considerable uncertainty remains. We will revisit this question of the pace of diffusion in future reports.

> Part of this reflects the nature of the interaction: Claude.ai transcripts can include multi-turn conversations, while the API data we analyze is limited to single input-output pairs.

> At the US state level, these relationships are not statistically significant–likely also due to the smaller sample size for US states.

> Importantly, the primitives themselves are not necessarily causal factors—we don't know if income or education are truly driving adoption, or if they're proxies for other underlying conditions. Many of these factors are highly correlated with one another.

> This relationship is not significant at the US state level, perhaps because income variation and use case diversity are more limited within the United States than globally.

> Here, though, the message is unclear: the automation share is essentially unrelated to the human levels of education required to write the prompt (Appendix Figure A.1).

> It's worth noting that a fundamental difference from the METR setting is selection. … If users avoid tasks they expect to fail, for example, observed success rates will overstate true capability on the full distribution of potential tasks.

> Also due to this selection effect, there's no guarantee that more performant models would show improvement in this plot, because users may respond to new models by providing more challenging presentations of otherwise similar O\*NET tasks.

> However, its implications depend on how often these Claude conversations actually displace or augment work that would otherwise be done by humans. … But when a Claude conversation maps to a teacher performing a lecture, it is less clear how this translates to reduced lecture time on the job.

> However, our education-based measure differs from Autor and Thompson's expertise concept: their framework would label some tasks as high expertise where ours specifies low education…

> And these predictions are based on current Claude usage patterns, which will shift as models are trained on new capabilities and users discover new applications—potentially changing which tasks are covered and whether the net effect is deskilling or upskilling.

> However, these estimates reflect current model capabilities, and all signs suggest that reliability over increasingly long-running tasks will improve.

**Annotation.** Eighteen caveats in the body, beside their findings — which is the placement the third report got wrong and this one gets right. Three categories, and all three are in the body rather than the footnotes: statistical precision (three months of data, said four times; the 10% significance level; the state-level power problem), identification (attenuation bias named as a named bias; "not necessarily causal factors"; the single-turn/multi-turn asymmetry; selection into the sample), and construct validity (the proxy mismatch with Autor and Thompson; coverage is not displacement; current usage patterns will shift and may reverse the sign).

The selection paragraph is the best of them because it states a consequence most authors would suppress: the measure may fail to improve when the models improve. And the repetition of "three months" is worth noticing as a technique — a sample limitation restated in the Overview, in the body, at the end of the section and in the Introduction is a limitation a reader cannot fail to carry.

The unit of observation named with its consequence and the check that addresses it cited. Geolocation standards with version numbers. Privacy thresholds given twice at two granularities. Task-observation floors given separately for the two analyses that use different ones, with the reason for the tighter one ("to reduce measurement error"). The embedding model and the estimator named. There is not one sentence of the form "as with all studies, there are limitations".

Footnotes 5 and 6 of Chapter 3 drop the Seychelles and Wyoming outright for abusive traffic, where September kept Utah in the headline bullet with the abuse finding in a footnote and a stated belief that it was not driving the results. **Excluding the contaminated geography and saying so beats keeping it and asserting robustness, and the series has moved from the second to the first.** Footnote 8's "There are certainly other ways to adjust based on task reliability" is the same class of honesty: the chosen adjustment is defended by naming the assumption under which it is the right one, not by claiming it is the only one.

### Annotation

- **Placement, and the improvement.** Dispersed, as before, but no longer hidden: the instrument's limits are in a named sub-section, and the eighteen body caveats sit beside the findings they qualify rather than in footnotes. A reader who reads only the body now meets every major caveat. That is the third report's main editorial failure repaired, and it happened because the new measure forced a methods chapter. **The form our posts use — the specificity of these footnotes, in a section a referee can find — is now closer to what the series itself does.**
- **The three caveats a referee would raise first, and where they are.** (i) *Every primitive is Claude's estimate, validated only for direction, on a small non-representative set, with no reported accuracy.* Chapter 2 says all of this except the last, and it is the report's best-handled limitation. But note what it means downstream: a success rate that is Claude's assessment of Claude's own work is the input to effective AI coverage, to the task-horizon estimate and to the productivity adjustment, so a single unquantified instrument carries three of the four headline results in Chapter 4. No sensitivity analysis to classifier error appears anywhere. (ii) *The convergence headline rests on one three-month difference across 50 states, measured with an instrument that changed between the two periods.* The three-month limitation is stated four times; the attenuation bias is instrumented; but the classifier change (Sonnet 4 → Sonnet 4.5, footnote 7 of Chapter 1) is disposed of by appeal to a previous finding rather than by a re-run, and the same footnote governs the 7pp augmentation reversal. September re-ran its comparison on the old classifier and reported the 4pp discrepancy; this report does not. **This is the passage to point at when explaining why an instrument change between waves must be re-run, not asserted.** (iii) *Inferred geography*, unchanged from September: every AUI value rests on IP geolocation of conversations with VPN, anycast and hosting traffic excluded, so the index measures where conversations originate, not where users live or work, and the exclusion is selective in a way that could bias the technically sophisticated populations being ranked. Stated factually in footnote 2 of Chapter 3, never discussed.
- **Where a threat is answered, the answer is reported with its cost.** The attenuation-bias sequence is the model: threat named, instrument proposed with relevance and validity argued, corrected estimate reported, **the correction moves the answer away from the headline**, the loss of precision disclosed, and the specification that fails the significance test identified. Reporting that only one of two 2SLS estimates clears 10% is what makes the rest of the passage trustworthy.
- **Where a threat is not answered, the report says so in the same breath.** "considerable uncertainty remains"; "the message is unclear"; "we don't know if income or education are truly driving adoption"; "there's no guarantee that more performant models would show improvement in this plot"; "it is less clear how this translates to reduced lecture time on the job". No "we believe this is not driving the results" appears anywhere in the report — the third report's weakest formula is gone.
- **What is missing throughout: an MDE beside a null.** Four nulls are reported (state-level primitive relationships; the automation–education relationship; the augmentation relationship at state level; global convergence). Each is given a candidate explanation and none is given a detectable-effect size. Our standard requires one, and this report is the corpus's clearest illustration of what its absence costs: a reader cannot tell whether "not statistically significant" at the state level means "no relationship" or "50 states".
- **Drafting tells.** The `wiki/INDEX.md` description of an inline appendix is wrong, and the reason is that the report references "Appendix Figure A.1" as though it were in the document. "Kalanyi et al." for Kalyani, twice (body p. 15 and footnote 3). "ISO-3611" for ISO-3166 in footnote 4 of Chapter 3. "roughly 5% percentage points per year" and "based on a the much larger set" in footnote 6 of Chapter 4. "the feasibility and the cost of automation labor tasks". "rose 3pp in August to 13%". "may have more other resources". A missing terminal period at the end of the Introduction's third paragraph. Figure 4.6's missing colon and its ungrammatical second caption sentence. "First Author Block" and "Second Author Block" as published headings on the web page. Individually trivial; collectively they are the signature of a 55-page document with no summary block to force a final read-through, and they are the argument for our own verification pass running over captions and footnotes as well as body prose.

### Annotation

- **What was learned.** Stated as an instrument first and a finding second, which is unusual and is the right choice for this report: "This fourth Anthropic Economic Index report introduces economic primitives—foundational characteristics of AI use—that show how Claude is used by both consumers and firms." The second sentence then re-states the bound on the instrument, in the close, without being asked to: "these measures are directionally accurate and, taken together, provide important signals even if individual classifications are imperfect." **A close that repeats the limitation of its own central measure, in its opening paragraph, is doing something almost no paper does and is the single most copyable move in this section.** Only then do the findings arrive, as a shape rather than a list: usage concentrates on high-education tasks, income mediates how the technology is used, diffusion is fast within the US and slow between countries. No number survives into the Concluding Remarks — not one figure in six paragraphs, exactly as in September.
- **Why it matters.** Three conditionals, each with an antecedent the report did not measure, and each with its dependency named: "**If** these tasks shrink for US workers, the net effect could be to deskill jobs. But these impacts depend crucially on complementarity across tasks, and whether increased productivity at a certain task may increase the demand for it." The stake is then raised to the international level and left as an open question rather than a prediction: "this raises questions about whether AI will narrow or widen international economic gaps." Compare September's "These patterns risk creating divergence", which asserted a direction of risk. This close declines even that, and the reason is visible in the evidence: within-US convergence and between-country stability point opposite ways, so the honest statement is a question. **A why-it-matters that ends in a question is legitimate when the findings point both ways; it is evasion only when they do not.**
- **What comes next.** Committal about method and data, and — new in this wave — **specific about what would falsify the report's own reading**: "As AI capabilities advance, Claude's success rate may increase, usage patterns may show greater autonomy, users may tackle new and more complex tasks, and tasks that prove automatable may graduate from interactive chat to API deployment." Four named directions of change, each of which would move a number in this report, and none of them predicted to happen. Chapter 1's close names its specific next measurement ("We will revisit this question of the pace of diffusion in future reports"), Chapter 4's names the mechanism that could reverse its own conclusion ("the continuing growth in model capabilities suggests that both task coverage and task success may increase"), and Chapter 2 names the validation not yet done. Nothing predicts which way a future number will move.
- **Recommendations, and who receives them.** Two, both in the final paragraph, and neither addressed to a named instrument. *Policymakers*: "whether policymakers create a regulatory context that advances both safety and innovation, will shape how AI transforms economies" — the least directive policy sentence in the corpus so far; it does not tell policymakers to do anything, it tells them that what they do matters. *Everyone, about access*: "For AI to benefit users globally, expanding access alone will not suffice—developing the human capital that enables effective use, particularly in lower-income economies, is essential." This is the report's one genuine recommendation, it is the only sentence in the close containing the word "essential", and it is traceable to a specific finding — the 0.93 correlation between human and AI education years, restated in the Introduction as "countries with higher educational attainment may be better positioned to benefit from AI, independent of adoption rates alone". **A single recommendation that follows from a single coefficient beats a list, and this one is scoped ("particularly in lower-income economies") and negatively framed ("expanding access alone will not suffice") so that it corrects a specific prior rather than proposing a programme.** *Researchers* get the data and the invitation, as always: "We publish this data to enable researchers, journalists, and the public to investigate novel questions about AI's economic impacts that can form the empirical foundation for policy responses." Note "journalists" is new to the list.
- **How it avoids a template summary.** Five devices. (i) **No numbers.** (ii) **The close re-states the instrument's limitation rather than its achievements**, in paragraph one. (iii) **Every substantive paragraph turns on itself**: "But these impacts depend crucially on…"; "Geographic diffusion patterns reinforce this picture. Within the US, per capita usage has converged slightly; globally, diffusion is slower"; the whole of paragraph four, which is about how this report's numbers will stop being true. (iv) **The one-sentence contribution claim is about scope and transparency, not results** — "this edition significantly expands both the scope and transparency of usage data we share" — the same move as September's and February's. (v) **It ends on human capital, not on AI**: the last clause of the report is "developing the human capital that enables effective use, particularly in lower-income economies, is essential", which relocates the subject of the whole document from the technology to the people using it.
- **Where the close is weaker than September's.** Two things. Paragraphs open on the report more often than on the world — four of six begin with "This fourth…", "Our findings…", "Equally important to the patterns documented here…", "Building on prior releases…" — against September's two of nine. And there is no equivalent of September's single bolded thesis sentence ("Ultimately, the economic effects of transformative AI will be shaped as much by technical capabilities as by the policy choices societies make"), so nothing in this close is quotable as the report's statement about its own role. **A close with no sentence about what measurement can and cannot settle is a close a reader will not remember.**
- **Title against ending.** Title: "Economic Primitives". Ending: "developing the human capital that enables effective use … is essential." **They do not match.** The title names the instrument; the close names a policy conclusion about human capital. The word "primitives" appears in the close's first sentence and then never again, and nothing in the last two paragraphs requires the title's subject. Test the two against each other as September's file instructs — if the ending does not contain the title's key word doing work, one of the two is wrong — and this report fails it. The close that *would* have matched is Chapter 2's: the primitives are directionally accurate, they are not exact, and their value is in what they can predict. The close that was written is about who benefits from AI. Both are good; they belong to different documents. **This is the corpus's clearest example of a title–ending mismatch, and it is the specific failure our rule "the title matches the ending" exists to prevent.**
- **First person.** Used throughout for institutional and analytical acts ("We use Claude to estimate", "we find", "we replicate", "we observe", "We will track", "We publish this data"). Our house style forbids it. The substitutions this report supplies, used about half the time already, are the ones to borrow: attribute to the exhibit ("Figure 2.1 shows that…", "The data shows that…", "The plot shows…"), to the analysis ("Our estimates suggest…", "these estimates imply…", "What this analysis shows is that…"), or to the classifier ("Claude estimates that…", "Claude assesses…"). The third of these has no equivalent in the earlier reports and is specific to model-generated measures; it is also the most natural way to write such a sentence without the first person.
- **Two chapter closes plus one report close, and one chapter with none.** Chapters 3 and 4 have Conclusions; Chapters 1 and 2 end on a forward pointer instead ("We will revisit this question … in future reports"; "Chapter 4 explores task-level variation in greater depth"). The asymmetry reads as unfinished rather than as a choice. Each of the two closes that exist ends on its own hedge — Chapter 3's "may help predict", Chapter 4's "On the other hand, the continuing growth in model capabilities … could increase productivity impacts" — so neither section ends on its strongest possible reading, which is the series' settled and best habit.
