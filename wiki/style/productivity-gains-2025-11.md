# productivity-gains-2025-11 — style annotation

## Source

- **Title, set over four lines on the PDF cover:** "Estimating AI productivity gains from Claude conversations". The web page carries the same string as its H1; the BibTeX block in both versions gives `title = {Estimating AI productivity gains from Claude conversations}`. The title is the corpus's cleanest instance of the house naming rule — **the quantity is AI, the source is Claude, in one line** — and it is one of the few titles in the corpus to name an act of measurement ("Estimating") rather than a finding or a series; compare "Tracking AI's role in the US and global economy" and "Labor market impacts of AI: A new measure and early evidence".
- **Date:** the PDF cover sets its fields as two labelled columns, "Published:" over "November 5, 2025"; the web page header reads "Nov 25, 2025"; both BibTeX blocks give `date = {2025-11-05}`. The PDF's `/CreationDate` is 2025-11-24 19:03 UTC. `wiki/INDEX.md` carries 2025-11-25 with the discrepancy recorded in its Standing notes; this file follows that and flags it again under `## Verification`.
- **Authors, as set on the cover:** the right-hand column, "Author:" over "Alex Tamkin and Peter McCrory", then below both columns "Acknowledgements:" and fifteen names (Kevin Troy, Ruth Appel, Keir Bradwell, Sarah Heck, Jack Clark, Jared Mueller, Jennifer Martinez, Sarah Pollack, Francesco Mosconi, Stuart Ritchie, Jared Powell, Carl De Torres, Lara Karadogan, David Saunders, Ben Merkel). Two authors and no affiliation line; the acknowledgements are on the cover rather than at the end, so the institutional weight is declared before the first sentence.
- **Primary URL fetched:** https://www.anthropic.com/research/estimating-productivity-gains
- **PDF fetched (document of record):** https://www-cdn.anthropic.com/e5645986a7ce8fbcc48fa6d2fc67753c87642c30.pdf — 25 pages, PDF 1.4, 3,584,111 bytes, producer "Adobe PDF Library 18.0", creator "Adobe InDesign 21.0 (Macintosh)". The typographic consequence matters for reading the quotations below: **the document has no italic font at all.** Emphasis is set in the Medium weight of Tiempos Text (body) and Styrene (captions), so where the web page distinguishes `<strong>` from `<em>`, the PDF renders both as bold, and several words the web italicises carry no emphasis in the PDF.
- **Document type:** a designed research note, not a preprint and not a blog post. It calls itself both: "this research note" (p. 3) and "This report" (p. 5). No abstract, no keywords, no reference list, no author-contribution statement, no appendix of robustness tables. What it has instead: a three-page "Overview" (pp. 2–4) that functions as an executive summary, ten numbered figures, one unnumbered table, five footnotes carrying the theory, a BibTeX block, and an appendix of four prompts reproduced in full.
- **Length:** 4,091 words of body prose (pp. 2–21), plus ~750 words of figure and table captions, ~310 words of footnotes and ~360 words of prompts. Pages 2–4 are the Overview; pp. 4–5 the Introduction; pp. 5–8 method and validation; pp. 9–13 task-level results; pp. 13–17 the aggregation; pp. 17–18 the bottleneck conjecture; pp. 19–20 Limitations; pp. 20–21 Conclusion; pp. 21–22 BibTeX and footnotes; pp. 22–25 the appendix. Counted as body prose only (the PDF sets body at 11 pt Tiempos Text and captions at 8 pt Styrene, so the two are separable by font), **the method-and-validation block runs ~650 words against ~700 for the whole task-level results section, and the Validation section alone (438 words) is longer than any single findings subsection (the longest is 321)** — which is the structural fact that defines this piece.
- **Citations, and what a PDF reader can do with them.** There is no bibliography. Every citation is either a hyperlink on the anchor text or a bare author–year in prose or footnote: "Acemoglu (2024)" (p. 14), "Baqaee and Farhi (2019)" (footnote 2), "Filippucci, Gal, and Schief, 2024" (p. 16 and the Figure 10 caption), "Aghion, Jones, and Jones" (p. 18). In the PDF the anchor text is coloured but **carries no link annotation at all** (zero in 25 pages), and the only URL printed as text in the body is the FRBSF series in footnote 5 (the BibTeX block on p. 21 prints one more). So the document of record shows its citations and withholds their targets. The sharpest case: the five past-trial results on p. 13 are cited by hyperlinking the numbers themselves — "56%, 40%, 26%, 14% and even negative" are five separate anchors — and in the PDF they are five coloured numerals with no sources. The disconfirming trial in the Limitations ("a recent randomized controlled trial", p. 19) is cited the same way, and on the web it resolves to the same study as the "negative" in that list.
- **Audience, two of them, segregated by section.** The Overview is written for a general reader: "90 minutes", "roughly twice the run rate in recent years", no notation. The aggregation section is written for macroeconomists: Hulten's theorem, Domar weights, the labour share of income, TFP against labour productivity, and a footnote distinguishing the two ("The increase in TFP is more primitive than the increase in labor productivity", footnote 3). The registers do not blend; footnote 2 carries the entire formal statement of the method so the body never has to.
- **Companion:** `wiki/reports/productivity-gains-2025-11.md` has the claims, the verbatim definitions, the prompts, the internal arithmetic inconsistencies and the "what it did not test" list. This file annotates only how the piece is built and phrased.

**Register difference from the rest of the corpus, in one paragraph.** Every other piece in the corpus measures something Claude's users did; this one measures something Claude *said* about what its users did, and then multiplies it by the US wage bill. The entire rhetorical architecture follows from that one fact. (1) **Validation precedes results** — a section headed "Validation", with two exercises and two figures, sits between the method and the first finding, which no other piece in the corpus does in its main text (the closest is the Claude Code expertise appendix, which validates its estimator in an appendix sub-section). (2) **The estimator is named as the grammatical subject of the findings**: "Claude's estimates imply", "Claude thinks", "Based on Claude's estimates", "Claude estimates that". The note almost never says a thing is true; it says what Claude's estimates imply. `estimat*` appears 104 times in pp. 2–21, roughly one word in forty. (3) **The headline is fenced by assumption rather than by interval.** There is not one confidence interval, standard error or sensitivity run attached to the 1.8%; what fences it instead is a stack of named assumptions, each stated in the same sentence or the next, and the noun "exercise" used twice for the estimate itself. Where the February 2025 paper hedges with a tilde, this one hedges with a conditional clause.

## Section order

Headings in order, with the PDF's typographic tier, what each does, and roughly how long it runs. The third tier is the same size as body text and is distinguished from it by face alone, which is why several of its headings read as sentences rather than labels.

**Where the findings appear.** Three times, at three depths, and the depths are unequal: the Overview prose (the two task-level numbers and the aggregate, no figure references, no sample window), the Overview bullets (every headline number plus its principal caveat), and the body sections (the number, its figure, its method and its caveat in the same subsection). The Conclusion restates only the aggregate. Unlike the February 2025 paper, no *task-level* finding is restated four times; the aggregate is the exception, and it is a large one — "1.8%" appears seven times as the estimate (Overview prose, twice in bullet three, the Figure 7 caption, the Findings paragraph on p. 15, the Conclusion, and the Figure 10 caption) and an eighth time as the 2019 comparison base. Unlike the launch posts, the summary is not a teaser but a full parallel text — a reader who stops after p. 4 has every number in the document and none of the validation.

**Where the limitations appear.** More than twenty places, of which the Limitations section is the last. Three are in the Overview (a dedicated three-sentence paragraph, the overstatement clause in bullet two and the omission clause in bullet three); four are in the findings subsections, one each; two are inside the method (the "implicitly assumes" sentences on p. 14); three are in the Findings paragraphs on pp. 16–17; two are in captions (Figures 6 and 7); six are the Limitations bullets; one is in the Conclusion. **No finding in this document is more than three sentences from a caveat**, which is the single most imitable structural property of the piece.

**Where the method appears.** Split in two and separated by the results, which is unusual and deliberate: the task-level method is on pp. 5–6, before the validation; the aggregation method is on p. 14, after the task-level results and immediately before the aggregate. Each method sits directly above the numbers it produces, so neither is read in the abstract. The formal apparatus is pushed into footnote 2; the operational apparatus — the prompts, which *are* the instrument — is pushed to the appendix.

**A hierarchy divergence between the two published versions.** The PDF's three typographic tiers and the web page's heading levels do not agree. On the web, "Estimating task length and time savings" is an `<h2>` (top tier) and "Validation" an `<h3>` beneath it, while the PDF sets both at 15 pt; and "Findings" is an `<h3>` sibling of "From task-level efficiency gains…" on the web but an 11 pt child of it in the PDF, which changes whether the aggregate reads as a section of its own. Per `room/director-2026-09-16-pdf-web-ruling.md` both are recorded and neither treated as governing; the PDF's tiering is the one described above.

## Opening move

### The Overview, prose paragraphs, verbatim (pp. 2–3)

> What do real conversations with Claude tell us about the effects of AI on labor productivity? Using our privacy-preserving analysis method, we sample one hundred thousand real conversations from Claude.ai, estimate how long the tasks in these conversations would take with and without AI assistance, and study the productivity implications across the broader economy. Based on Claude's estimates, these tasks would take on average about 90 minutes to complete without AI assistance, and Claude speeds up individual tasks by about 80%.

> Extrapolating these estimates out suggests current-generation AI models could increase annual US labor productivity growth by 1.8% over the next decade – roughly twice the run rate in recent years. But this isn't a prediction of the future, since we don't take into account the rate of adoption or what might happen once AI models improve further.

> Our analysis has limits. Most notably, we can't account for additional time humans spend on tasks outside of their conversations with Claude, including validating the quality or accuracy of Claude's work. But as AI models get better at time estimation, we think our methods in this research note could become increasingly useful for understanding how AI is shaping real work.

### Two of the five summary bullets, verbatim (bold as the PDF sets it, p. 3; the list runs pp. 3–4)

> - **Across one hundred thousand real world conversations, Claude estimates that AI reduces task completion time by 80%.** We use Claude to evaluate anonymized Claude.ai transcripts to estimate the productivity impact of AI. According to Claude's estimates, people typically use AI for complex tasks that would, on average, take people 1.4 hours to complete. By matching tasks to O\*NET occupations and BLS wage data, we estimate these tasks would otherwise cost $55 in human labor.

> - **Extrapolating these results to the economy, current generation AI models could increase annual US labor productivity growth by 1.8% over the next decade. This would double the annual growth the US has seen since 2019, and places our estimate towards the upper end of recent estimates.** Taking as given Claude's estimates of task-level efficiency gains, we use standard methods to calculate a 1.8% implied annual increase in US labor productivity over the next ten years. However, this estimate does not account for future improvements in AI models (or more sophisticated uses of current technology), which could significantly magnify AI's economic impact.

### The Introduction, first three paragraphs, verbatim (p. 4)

> As part of the Anthropic Economic Index, we have documented how people use Claude across different tasks, industries, and places. We've captured the **breadth** of uses—how people use Claude for legal, scientific, and programming tasks—but not their **depth**. How substantial are the tasks for which people use Claude, and how much time does Claude save them?

> The current version of the Economic Index can't capture this **within-task heterogeneity**—for instance, it can't distinguish report-writing tasks that take five minutes from those that take five days, or financial modeling tasks that take an afternoon from those that take a few weeks. This makes it difficult to assess AI's economic effects: a software developer might use Claude to write ten pull requests in a day, but if nine are minor documentation updates and one is a critical infrastructure change, simply counting the number of these tasks performed with Claude misses the point.

> Not only that, but as model capabilities improve, we want to understand whether they do higher-value work. To understand how AI is reshaping work and productivity, we need to know not just which tasks Claude handles, but how substantial those tasks and time savings are.

### The disqualification of the alternatives, and the roadmap, verbatim (pp. 4–5)

> Several groups have begun conducting randomized controlled trials to measure productivity gains in narrow domains, including software engineering tasks, writing, and customer service. METR's work on measuring AI's ability to complete long tasks has demonstrated that AI systems can independently tackle extended, multi-step challenges. But these evaluations consider a narrow set of problems, rather than broad real-world use. To assess AI's overall impact on the economy, we need a way to analyze hundreds or thousands of real-world AI applications.

> This report takes a first step toward that goal. It uses Claude to estimate how much time it would take a human to complete the tasks that Claude handles, compares that to how long Claude and the human took together, and thereby calculates how much time the AI has saved. While AI models lack context about users' expertise, workflows, and constraints, we find that model-estimated times show promising accuracy for a dataset of software engineering tasks, relative to both human-estimated completion times and time-tracked outcomes.

> In what follows, we present our methodology for estimating task-level time savings, validate our approach against ground-truth data, and then use these estimates to assess which tasks and occupations show the largest productivity gains from AI. We then explore what our task-level estimates imply about aggregate productivity as AI begins to be adopted throughout the economy.

**Annotation.**

- **The question is the first sentence, in the interrogative, and it names both nouns in the right places.** "What do real conversations with Claude tell us about the effects of AI on labor productivity?" The evidence is Claude; the quantity is AI; the outcome is a national statistic. That is the house rule (question says AI, evidence says Claude) executed in nineteen words, and it is the best single sentence in the corpus to keep beside a draft opening. Note what it does *not* do: it makes no forecast, names no stake, and cites nobody.
- **The method arrives in sentence two, as three verbs in series.** "we sample one hundred thousand real conversations from Claude.ai, estimate how long the tasks in these conversations would take with and without AI assistance, and study the productivity implications across the broader economy." Sample, estimate, study — the whole paper, in the order the sections come, with the aggregation admitted as "study the productivity implications" rather than as a prediction. When a post's method is three steps, this is the construction: one sentence, three verbs, no subordination.
- **How soon the first number appears, and which number.** "one hundred thousand" at word 24, "90 minutes" at word 66 and "80%" as the last word of the paragraph (81 words) — all three inside the first paragraph, and the sample size first. The economy-wide number waits until the second paragraph, which is the right order: the reader is told the size of the evidence before the size of the claim.
- **The large number and its disclaimer share a paragraph, and the disclaimer is the next sentence.** "…could increase annual US labor productivity growth by 1.8% over the next decade – roughly twice the run rate in recent years. But this isn't a prediction of the future, since we don't take into account the rate of adoption or what might happen once AI models improve further." Four fences in two sentences: "Extrapolating these estimates out suggests" (the verb of the sentence is *suggests*, and its subject is the extrapolation, not the world); "current-generation" (temporal); "could"; and then the explicit denial of the genre — *this is not a forecast* — with two named omissions. **This is the corpus's model for hedging an economy-wide number: name the operation, attach the comparison that makes it legible, then deny the genre the number invites, and say what is missing by name.** Note that the comparison ("roughly twice the run rate in recent years") arrives before the disclaimer, so the number is made meaningful before it is made provisional.
- **Limits before results, in a paragraph of their own, third from the top.** "Our analysis has limits. Most notably, we can't account for additional time humans spend on tasks outside of their conversations with Claude, including validating the quality or accuracy of Claude's work." Three sentences: the flat concession, the single most damaging omission named concretely, then a recovery that is about the *method's future* rather than about the current result ("as AI models get better at time estimation, we think our methods in this research note could become increasingly useful"). Down, then up, and the up is a claim about the instrument. The February 2025 paper does this in the Introduction's last two paragraphs; here it is on page three, above every finding.
- **The gap is in Anthropic's own instrument, not in the literature.** "We've captured the **breadth** of uses … but not their **depth**." Two bolded nouns carry the whole positioning, and the thing being criticised is the Economic Index. Then the criticism is made specific twice: once as a definitional failure ("can't distinguish report-writing tasks that take five minutes from those that take five days") and once as a worked counter-example ("a software developer might use Claude to write ten pull requests in a day, but if nine are minor documentation updates and one is a critical infrastructure change, simply counting the number of these tasks performed with Claude misses the point"). **This is the construction to copy for "the puzzle Anthropic left open": name the published instrument, name the distinction it cannot draw, then give one case where the distinction decides the answer.** The pull-request example costs one sentence and makes the rest of the paper necessary.
- **The alternatives are disqualified by scope, not by result.** RCTs "in narrow domains" and METR's long-task work are described by what they achieved, credited in full ("has demonstrated that AI systems can independently tackle extended, multi-step challenges"), and then set aside by one shared property: "these evaluations consider a narrow set of problems, rather than broad real-world use." One clause disqualifies a literature, and the requirement it implies is stated immediately as a need rather than as a claim ("we need a way to analyze hundreds or thousands of real-world AI applications"). Same move as the February 2025 paper's em-dash aside, in three sentences instead of one, and with the credit given first.
- **The downgrade is in the opening.** "This report takes a first step toward that goal." Five words, placed at the head of the paragraph that describes the design, before any result. Compare the February paper's "We offer this initial framework … as a starting point", which is in the last paragraph of its §4.2. Putting the downgrade first costs nothing and inoculates the whole document.
- **The roadmap sentence puts validation second of four.** "we present our methodology …, validate our approach against ground-truth data, and then use these estimates to assess which tasks and occupations show the largest productivity gains … We then explore what our task-level estimates imply about aggregate productivity". The order of the sentence is the order of the sections, and the promise of validation is made before the reader can ask for it.
- **What the summary block costs, since our template forbids one.** Two drifts, both visible only by reading the Overview against the body. (1) Bullet one gives "80%" as the headline saving; p. 12 gives "an estimated 84% time savings" for the median conversation and the Figure 6 caption gives "The overall median savings is 81%" across tasks. Three statistics of one construct, each correct for its own unit, none reconciled anywhere — and the summary's version is the one that travels. (2) Bullet three says the gain "would double the annual growth the US has seen since 2019" where p. 15 says it "would nearly double the current long-term growth rate, which has averaged 2.1% per year since 1947 and 1.8% since 2019": the bullet drops "nearly" and silently changes the base. **A summary written after the body drifts toward the sharper number**, exactly as the coding-agents post's summary does. One entry per number, quoted identically everywhere, is the only defence.
- **The one place the naming discipline inverts.** Bullet one reads "Claude estimates that AI reduces task completion time by 80%": the *estimator* is named Claude and the *agent of the effect* is named AI. The Overview prose has it the other way round — "Claude speeds up individual tasks by about 80%" — which is the form our house rule wants for a finding. Both sentences are about the same number; the second is the one to imitate, and the pair is worth keeping as the cleanest illustration in the corpus of why the rule exists.
- **First person, and what it is doing.** "we" 79 times and "our" 42 times in pp. 2–21, and unlike the launch posts the first person carries the *findings* ("we find", "we calculate", "we estimate", "we observe"), not only the commitments. House style forbids it, and the substitution is nearly free where the estimate is the subject: "we calculate that Claude's estimates imply an annual increase in US labor productivity of 1.8%" → "Claude's estimates imply an annual increase in US labor productivity of 1.8%"; "we find that model-estimated times show promising accuracy" → "model-estimated times show promising accuracy". The one construction that does not survive deletion is "we think" (used four times to mark opinion, e.g. "we think these estimates might overstate current productivity effects to at least some degree"), and the replacement is to state the direction of the bias without a believer, which is stronger and is what the note itself does elsewhere.

## Findings and their caveats

The note's order is: the instrument, then the task level, then the economy, then a conjecture. Every finding is quoted in the order it appears.

### The instrument as a finding — validation before results (pp. 6–8)

> Estimating task duration is notoriously difficult for humans. AI models have an even more difficult job, since they lack crucial context about the broader context of tasks (though we expect this context to increase over time as features like memory and external integrations become more comprehensive). To assess whether Claude's estimates are informative, we conducted two validation analyses.

> **Self-consistency testing:** First, we assess whether Claude produces stable estimates of task lengths across different conversation samples, or across variations in our prompts.

> We create multiple prompt variations—for example, asking about an "employee with appropriate skills" versus a "skilled professional"—to assess how sensitive estimates are to the way the prompt is phrased. We analyze 1,800 conversations with each variant, where users consented to share these conversations with us, and computed correlations across prompt variants. The results showed strong self-agreement, with log-scale correlations of r=0.89–0.93 across variants.

> **External benchmarking:** Self-agreement doesn't matter much if a model's predictions don't correspond well to reality. To check this, we tested Claude's time estimation capabilities against a dataset of thousands of real-world software development tasks gathered from JIRA tickets for open-source repositories, with both developer estimates and actual tracked completion times.

> This is a very challenging task for Claude, given that Claude receives only the title and description of the JIRA tickets, while the human developers have full context on the codebase and the ticket, and have seen how long similar tasks take to complete. On a subset of 1000 tasks from this benchmark:
>
> - Human developers themselves achieved ρ=0.50 Spearman correlation with actual times, and a Pearson correlation of r_log=0.67 on the log values, indicating a moderate-strength correlation (higher is better for both values).
> - Claude Sonnet 4.5 achieved ρ=0.44 and r_log=0.46
> - Claude Sonnet 4.5 with ten examples of tasks and their ground-truth time lengths showed a worse ρ=0.39, but improved r_log=0.48

> This analysis suggests that Claude's estimates provide directional information that is only slightly worse than software developers' own estimates. However, we observe that Claude's estimates are much more compressed than humans—predicting comparatively long times for shorter tasks, and vice versa—and are overall more prone to overestimates. This suggests that the actual differences in task lengths across tasks may be larger than we report, and that actual task lengths may be slightly shorter. Overall, these findings demonstrate that model predictions have meaningful correlation with real-world outcomes, at least in this domain, making them useful for comparing one task to another or tracking changes over time. We also observe higher correlation from Claude Sonnet 4.5 compared to Claude Sonnet 4, suggesting that these estimates may continue to improve with model capabilities.

**Annotation.** The section opens by lowering the bar in two sentences and it lowers it for *humans first*: "Estimating task duration is notoriously difficult for humans. AI models have an even more difficult job". The benchmark is therefore not truth but a human estimator, and the note says so before reporting anything. Then the information asymmetry is spelled out in one sentence with both sides named — Claude gets "only the title and description", the developers "have full context on the codebase and the ticket, and have seen how long similar tasks take" — so the reader knows the comparison is unfair in a stated direction before the numbers arrive. **Naming which side of a comparison is handicapped, and how, is what converts a losing number into an informative one.**

The three bullets report two correlation families for each estimator, with the human row first, the reading glossed for a non-specialist ("indicating a moderate-strength correlation (higher is better for both values)") and the one inconvenient result stated as a split verdict rather than dropped: ten-shot prompting "showed a worse ρ=0.39, but improved r_log=0.48". A result that moves two metrics in opposite directions is reported with both, in one clause, and not adjudicated.

The verdict sentence is graded twice and both gradings are conservative: "provide directional information that is only slightly worse than software developers' own estimates" — *directional*, not accurate, and *only slightly worse*, not comparable. Then the caveat, and this is the passage to re-read before writing any measurement-error paragraph: "Claude's estimates are much more compressed than humans—predicting comparatively long times for shorter tasks, and vice versa—and are overall more prone to overestimates. This suggests that the actual differences in task lengths across tasks may be larger than we report, and that actual task lengths may be slightly shorter." The bias is **named, mechanised, signed and translated into two corrections to the reader's reading of the paper** — one to the spread, one to the level — in two sentences. Nothing in the corpus does this better. What it does not do is propagate the correction: the 1.8% is never recomputed under the measured compression, and that is the first thing a referee asks.

The section then closes on a boundary statement — "at least in this domain, making them useful for comparing one task to another or tracking changes over time" — which withdraws the instrument's licence for levels and keeps it for comparisons and trends. Since the paper's headline *is* a level, the boundary sentence quietly concedes more than the Limitations section does. And the last sentence converts the benchmark into a trend claim ("higher correlation from Claude Sonnet 4.5 compared to Claude Sonnet 4, suggesting that these estimates may continue to improve with model capabilities") with no number for either model — a one-clause extrapolation offered as reassurance.

### Finding 1 — the example tasks (p. 10)

> Looking at individual tasks within occupations provides concrete examples of where and how AI might be delivering time savings. At the most extreme end, we see users complete curriculum development tasks that Claude thinks would take 4.5 hours in just 11 minutes. Such tasks have an implied labor cost of $115 based on the average hourly wage of teachers.

> People also use AI to save 87% of the time it would take to write invoices, memos, and other documents (at least for the type of documents Claude is asked to handle). Finally, AI saves 80% of time on financial analyst tasks like interpreting financial data for tasks that would ordinarily cost $31 in wages.

**Annotation.** The findings section opens on a single case, and the case is the extreme one, labelled as extreme before it is given: "At the most extreme end". The hedge is two words inside the sentence — "that Claude thinks would take 4.5 hours" — which is the cheapest attribution device in the document and the one most worth stealing: *Claude thinks* where a paper would write *we estimate*. The pairing is concrete on both sides (4.5 hours against 11 minutes), so the reader computes the saving without being given a percentage.

The second paragraph shows the cost of writing a findings paragraph without a claims list. Its hedge is a good one — "(at least for the type of documents Claude is asked to handle)", a parenthetical that fences the population rather than the number — but the two dollar figures in this passage cannot be reconciled with the Figure 4 caption's stated formula (task cost = task time × hourly wage) without reading values off the figure image, and the sibling report records both numbers as unexplained against it. Per `room/director-2026-09-16-figure-values-ruling.md` no figure values are recorded here; the style point stands on its own. **Two of the six numbers in the note's first findings passage do not follow from the note's own published definition.** That is what a claims list and a claims map exist to prevent, and it is the strongest argument in the corpus for building one.

### Finding 2 — task length by occupation (p. 10)

> Human time estimates show that Claude handles tasks of very different lengths depending on the occupation. In the below plots, we show averages for each occupation category among the subset of tasks that Claude is used for. The average management task where Claude is used (e.g. selecting investments) is estimated to take humans 2.0 hours to complete, followed by legal (1.8 hours), education (1.7), and arts/media tasks (1.6). At the other end of the spectrum, food preparation tasks (e.g. planning or pricing menu items), installation/maintenance, and transportation tasks all take 0.3-0.5hrs on average, suggesting more circumscribed tasks, or tasks with less waiting time. Given that Claude's time estimates tend to underestimate long tasks and overestimate short tasks, it is possible that these differences might be even greater in practice.

**Annotation.** Four moves in five sentences, in the right order. (1) The denominator is stated before the numbers, and it is a restriction, not a population: "averages for each occupation category **among the subset of tasks that Claude is used for**". Every occupation number in this note is conditional on Claude's coverage, and the sentence that introduces them says so. (2) The ranking walks down one end and then names the other end, with a worked exemplar in parentheses at each end — "(e.g. selecting investments)", "(e.g. planning or pricing menu items)" — so the reader knows what a two-hour management task and a half-hour food task actually are. (3) The mechanism is offered as a two-way disjunction and left unchosen: "suggesting more circumscribed tasks, or tasks with less waiting time". (4) The closing sentence applies the *validation section's measured bias* to this specific reading: "Given that Claude's time estimates tend to underestimate long tasks and overestimate short tasks, it is possible that these differences might be even greater in practice."

That last move is rare and worth naming: **a caveat that makes the finding stronger.** It is credible precisely because the same paragraph could have used it the other way, and it is the only place in the corpus where a measured instrument bias is carried forward into the interpretation of a later result. Copy the shape — *given the bias we measured, the reading moves in this direction* — and, unlike the note, put a number on how far.

### Finding 3 — cost, and the wage gradient (pp. 10–11)

> Cost estimates amplify this variation in the impact of AI: the tasks with the longest time estimates also tend to be the tasks with the highest labor costs. We compute these cost estimates by multiplying the median time for each task by the associated occupation's average wage in the OEWS May 2024 data. The average management task would cost $133 for a professional compared to $119 for legal tasks and $8 for tasks relating to food preparation and serving. Business and financial tasks average $69 while computer and mathematical tasks average $82.

> Across all tasks we observe, we estimate Claude handles work that would cost a median of $54 in professional labor to hire an expert to perform the work in each conversation. Of course, the actual performance of current models will likely be worse than a human expert for many tasks, though recent research suggests the gap is closing across a wide range of different applications.

> Across major occupational groups, we observe a positive correlation between average hourly wage among tasks/occupations in our sample and the human-time-equivalent duration of the tasks Claude is asked to handle. For example, the Management and Legal occupational categories rank at the top of the classification in terms of average hourly wage—aligning with Claude's strengths in complex knowledge work.

**Annotation.** The construction rule is given in the sentence before the numbers ("multiplying the median time for each task by the associated occupation's average wage in the OEWS May 2024 data"), with the vintage of the external data in the same clause — the reader can reconstruct any cost figure from two published quantities. The spread is then carried by three occupations at the top, middle and bottom rather than by a range statistic, which is the corpus's standard device.

Two things to keep from the second paragraph. The unit of the median is spelled out to the point of redundancy — "a median of $54 in professional labor to hire an expert to perform the work in each conversation" — thirteen words defining what a dollar figure is a figure *of*, and worth every word, because the natural misreading (what the conversation cost) is expensive. And the quality caveat is attached to the cost finding rather than to the time finding: "Of course, the actual performance of current models will likely be worse than a human expert for many tasks". This is the counterfactual's weakest joint — the whole note prices AI output as if it were expert output — and it is conceded in a subordinate clause and then immediately softened by an uncited "recent research suggests the gap is closing" (a hyperlink on the web, an unresolvable coloured phrase in the PDF). A referee would ask for the bound, and there is none anywhere in the document.

The wage gradient is stated in prose without its coefficient — "we observe a positive correlation between average hourly wage … and the human-time-equivalent duration" — and the coefficient lives only in the Figure 5 caption ("r=0.8"). Direction in the body, magnitude in the caption: the same division of labour the February 2025 paper uses for its Job Zone ratios, and the same cost, which is that the sentence a reader carries away has no magnitude in it. The interpretive clause is marked as conjecture by one participle: "aligning with Claude's strengths in complex knowledge work" — nine words asserting something the data cannot show, fenced by *aligning with*.

### Finding 4 — the distribution of time savings (pp. 12–13)

> Our human time and cost estimates capture the magnitude of tasks people tackle with AI. But the time savings—Claude's estimate for how much faster work gets done with AI—reflects the productivity gains that might come from using AI for those tasks.

> The median conversation experienced an estimated 84% time savings, though we see considerable variation across tasks and categories. For example, the task of checking diagnostic images only shows 20% time savings, likely because this is already a task that can be done quickly by experts without AI assistance. By contrast, the task of compiling information from reports sees approximately 95% time savings, likely because AI systems can read, extract, and cite information much more quickly than people. Overall, the distribution of time saved by task is concentrated within the 50-95% range, peaking between 80-90%.

> These large time savings align with Claude's abilities to read and write far faster than people can. However, our approach doesn't take into account the additional work people need to do to refine Claude's outputs to a finished state, or whether they continue iterating on the work product across multiple sessions—both of which would result in smaller time savings. Past randomized controlled trials have typically found smaller time savings, including 56%, 40%, 26%, 14% and even negative time savings across different applications—perhaps due to these effects or because these studies examined earlier generations of models.

**Annotation.** The construct is re-defined in an em-dash apposition at the moment it becomes load-bearing — "the time savings—Claude's estimate for how much faster work gets done with AI—" — with the estimator inside the definition, and the outcome it stands for is hedged in the same sentence ("reflects the productivity gains that **might** come from"). The distribution is then reported by its middle, its two tails and its mode, with each tail carrying a mechanism marked as conjecture by the same two words ("likely because … likely because"), and the mechanisms are of different kinds: the low tail is explained by the *counterfactual* being already fast, the high tail by the *model* being fast. Two explanations, two levels, one word of hedge each.

The third paragraph is the most consequential in the document and it is built in three steps: a reason to believe the result ("align with Claude's abilities to read and write far faster than people can"), the two omissions that would shrink it, each named and both signed ("both of which would result in smaller time savings"), and then the external evidence that contradicts the note — five past-trial results in a single list, the note's own estimate being higher than all of them — followed by two candidate reconciliations offered as alternatives ("perhaps due to these effects or because these studies examined earlier generations of models"). **The strongest disconfirming evidence in the document sits in the same paragraph as the finding, and the note declines to adjudicate.** That placement is right and the refusal is honest; what is missing is any attempt to reconcile, e.g. re-estimating on the subset of conversations resembling the trial tasks. Note also what the Overview does with this paragraph: nothing. The five trial numbers appear only here and in one Limitations bullet.

### Finding 5 — the economy-wide estimate (pp. 14–17)

> To estimate economy-wide productivity effects, we use Hulten's theorem, a standard method that allows us to aggregate efficiency gains at the task-level to the broader US economy. As in Acemoglu (2024)'s "baseline" approach, we model the implied increase in labor productivity as a weighted average over task-level productivity gains—a modeling choice that implicitly assumes that capital investment will increase as a result of an increase in total factor productivity (TFP) associated with AI adoption. In this framework, the implied increase TFP is the gain in labor productivity multiplied by the labor share of income.

> **Task-level productivity improvements:** In the previous section, we provided estimates we can use to compute how much more quickly each task is completed with AI assistance. We take the log difference between time without AI and the time with AI to generate a productivity improvement value, and conservatively assign tasks not observed in our sample a null improvement.

> **Economy-wide estimate:** We weight each task's implied productivity gains by its economic importance using two factors: (i) the fraction of time that Claude estimates the occupation spends on that task (as above), and (ii) the occupation's share of the total US wage bill (the number of people employed in that occupational category multiplied by the average wage, then divided by total wage bill across all occupations). For the total wage bill, we use May 2024 OEWS data. This approach implicitly assumes the time estimates that Claude produces represent reliable averages across all instances of each task, and that Claude or similar AI systems will be adopted across the entire US economy.

> Assuming 10 years for AI to reach universal adoption across the US economy—and using current models—we calculate that Claude's estimates imply an annual increase in US labor productivity of 1.8%. This would nearly double the current long-term growth rate, which has averaged 2.1% per year since 1947 and 1.8% since 2019. Assuming that labor's share of total factor productivity is 0.6, this implies an overall total factor productivity increase of 1.1% per year. Given that TFP growth has tended to be less than 1% since the early 2000s, these estimates suggest that, even broad deployment of current AI systems could cause growth to double: achieving the rates of the late 1990s, and of the 1960s and 1970s.

> This estimated increase in aggregate labor productivity implied by task-level efficiency gains is within the range of recent estimates of AI's potential impact on productivity, though it lies towards the upper end (Filippucci, Gal, and Schief, 2024).

> Importantly, this exercise assumes that AI capabilities (and humans' effectiveness in using AI) remain the same over the next 10 years as when we took our sample. This, though, seems unlikely to hold: we think that AI will continue to improve rapidly over the coming years.

> Therefore, this estimate should be taken as an exercise exploring what might happen based on current usage patterns, not a prediction of the impact on productivity that is actually most likely to happen. As we have written about in other work, we remain extremely alert to the possibility that AI causes significant labor market disruptions, which would likely be associated with larger increases in productivity due to AI. As models progress, this could represent an approximate lower bound on the productivity effects of AI, although our estimate does not account for unevenness in adoption, which might reduce real-world productivity gains in the short term.

**Annotation.** This is the passage the whole note exists to write, and the hedging is architectural rather than lexical. Six devices, in the order they operate.

1. **The method is borrowed by name and the borrowing is attributed.** "we use Hulten's theorem, a standard method" — named, glossed in six words, and its formal statement exported to footnote 2. "As in Acemoglu (2024)'s 'baseline' approach" puts the modelling choice on an external authority, and the quotation marks around *baseline* mark it as that author's term. When a post takes a method off the shelf, this is the form: name it, gloss it, cite whose implementation, footnote the algebra.
2. **The assumption a method carries is stated as the method's own, not the authors'.** "a modeling choice that implicitly assumes that capital investment will increase as a result of an increase in total factor productivity". The word "implicitly" appears twice in two paragraphs, both times introducing an assumption that a reader would not otherwise see — the other being "This approach implicitly assumes the time estimates that Claude produces represent reliable averages … and that Claude or similar AI systems will be adopted across the entire US economy." **Writing your own hidden assumptions out in a sentence beginning "this approach implicitly assumes" is the single most transferable sentence pattern in this document**, and it is what the assumptions sweep in our standards is for.
3. **The conservative choice is labelled as it is made.** "conservatively assign tasks not observed in our sample a null improvement." One adverb, in the clause that makes the choice. The note never returns to bound what the choice costs, which is the referee's objection; but the labelling is correct practice and cheap.
4. **The assumption precedes the number in the same sentence.** "Assuming 10 years for AI to reach universal adoption across the US economy—and using current models—we calculate that Claude's estimates imply an annual increase in US labor productivity of 1.8%." The reader meets the horizon and the model vintage before the digit, and the chain of custody is three links long — *Claude's estimates* → *imply* → *an annual increase*. The note does not say productivity will rise 1.8%; it says an estimate implies it.
5. **The comparison is the finding.** "This would nearly double the current long-term growth rate, which has averaged 2.1% per year since 1947 and 1.8% since 2019." Two bases, both dated, so the reader can check the doubling themselves — and the TFP figure gets the same treatment ("TFP growth has tended to be less than 1% since the early 2000s", with footnote 5 supplying 0.7% for 2015–2024 and 1.6% for 1995–2004). Without these two clauses the 1.8% is a number; with them it is a claim about the post-war record. **A macro number needs its historical base in the same sentence, with the window stated.**
6. **The derived quantity carries its parameter, and the parameter carries its provenance.** "Assuming that labor's share of total factor productivity is 0.6, this implies an overall total factor productivity increase of 1.1% per year", with footnote 4: "Acemoglu 2024 calculates the labor share in AI-exposed industries as 0.57; we use the economy-wide share of 0.6 for simplicity given how close it is." The deviation from the source is disclosed, quantified and justified in one sentence. That is what a deviation log looks like in published prose.

Then three paragraphs of downgrade, which is the part to study hardest. The first names the assumption that the note itself believes is false, and says so: "this exercise assumes that AI capabilities … remain the same over the next 10 years as when we took our sample. This, though, seems unlikely to hold". The second re-labels the whole result by genre — "this estimate should be taken as an exercise exploring what might happen based on current usage patterns, not a prediction of the impact on productivity that is actually most likely to happen" — the second time the note calls its headline an *exercise* rather than an estimate. The third fences in both directions in one sentence: "this could represent an approximate lower bound on the productivity effects of AI, although our estimate does not account for unevenness in adoption, which might reduce real-world productivity gains in the short term." Bound, hedged bound ("approximate"), and the counter-consideration in the same sentence. Nothing here is an interval, and a referee's first demand is one; but as *prose* this is the corpus's best answer to the question of how to publish a very large number you do not believe to be a forecast.

### Finding 6 — who the gain comes from (p. 17)

> Reflecting the fact that some tasks and occupations appear much more frequently in our data than others, we observe similar phenomena in occupations' contributions to labor productivity as well. Software developers contribute most (19%) to the total labor productivity gain attributable to AI. General and Operations Managers (about 6%), Market Research Analysts and Marketing Specialists (5%), Customer Service Representatives (4%) and Secondary School Teachers (3%) round out the top five.

> In contrast, restaurants, healthcare delivery, construction, and retail contribute much less to the overall productivity effect. This is mostly because few of their tasks appear in our data—largely because these occupations have few associated tasks in our sample.

**Annotation.** The finding is introduced by its own artefact. The sentence that reports the composition begins "Reflecting the fact that some tasks and occupations appear much more frequently in our data than others" — the distribution is attributed to the sample before a single share is given. And the low end is explained the same way, twice in one sentence: "This is mostly because few of their tasks appear in our data—largely because these occupations have few associated tasks in our sample." **A composition finding whose first and last words are about coverage rather than about the economy.** This is the right way to report a ranking produced by an instrument with known blind spots, and it is the opposite of what a reader tempted by the ranking would write. Precision to keep: "contribute most (19%) to the total labor productivity gain attributable to AI" states what the percentage is a share of, in the same clause, so 19% cannot be misread as a productivity gain.

One drift to note, recorded in the sibling report's arithmetic checks: the ordering of the fourth and fifth entries in this sentence does not match the ordering in the Figure 7 table. The style point is that a top-five list written in prose and a table of the same quantities are two claims, and something has to reconcile them.

### Finding 7 — the bottleneck conjecture, kept out of the findings (pp. 17–18)

> If workers are able to accelerate a subset of their occupational tasks with AI, the tasks where AI provides less speedup may come to represent a larger and thus more important share of those occupations' work. For example, AI might help a home inspector prepare reports, but if the inspector still has to spend the same amount of time physically traveling to the property to perform the inspection in person, this could make inspections a greater fraction of the job overall.

> Figure 9 illustrates this for a few occupations. For software developers, AI speeds up the process of software development, testing, documentation, and manipulating data. But we do not currently see meaningful AI use for coordinating system installation or supervising the work of other technologists or engineers. For teachers, we see that AI assists with lesson and activity planning, but not with sponsoring extracurricular clubs or enforcing rules in the classroom.

> From a growth perspective, these observations align well with a recent observation from Aghion, Jones, and Jones: "growth may be constrained not by what we are good at but rather by what is essential and yet hard to improve."

**Annotation.** The section is fenced by its heading. "How might AI change how workers spend their time?" is the only interrogative heading in the document, and it does the work a paragraph of hedging would otherwise do: **nothing under a question mark reads as a result.** Inside, the argument is stated as a conditional with three modals ("If workers are able to … may come to represent … this could make"), and it is made concrete by a worked example from outside the data entirely — the home inspector, who does not appear in the sample — so no reader can mistake the illustration for evidence. The observational content is stated in the negative and dated: "we do not **currently** see meaningful AI use for coordinating system installation or supervising the work of other technologists or engineers", which is an absence in the sample being reported as an absence in the sample.

The theoretical payoff is delegated to a twenty-two-word quotation from someone else, and the note claims only that its observations "align well with" it. **Where a post has a mechanism it cannot test, borrow the sentence that states it and claim only alignment.** Compare the February 2025 paper's "human barriers to entry may be significantly different than barriers to language models", which makes its own version of this point in its own voice and pays for it with a hedge.

### The hedge inventory, graded

Read as a set, the note's hedges are five devices, from weakest commitment to strongest.

1. **Attribution to the estimator.** "Based on Claude's estimates", "Claude's estimates imply", "Claude thinks", "Claude estimates that", "Human time estimates show", "Claude's estimate for how much faster work gets done". This is the note's default and it is applied to *every* headline. The count tells the story: `estimat*` 104 times in pp. 2–21 (109 if *overestimate* and *underestimate* are counted in).
2. **Attribution to the sample.** "in our sample" (nine occurrences, prose and captions), "in our data", "among the subset of tasks that Claude is used for", "across all tasks we observe", "for the type of documents Claude is asked to handle".
3. **The genre denial.** "this isn't a prediction of the future", "should be taken as an exercise exploring what might happen based on current usage patterns, not a prediction", "this exercise assumes". Twice the note refuses the category its number belongs to, and both refusals are in the same paragraph as the number.
4. **Modals for mechanism and for extrapolation.** "could" 11 times, "might" 14, "may" 7, "likely" 7 — and the note's characteristic construction is *modal inside a participial clause after the number*: "suggesting more circumscribed tasks", "likely because this is already a task that can be done quickly by experts", "aligning with Claude's strengths in complex knowledge work".
5. **Temporal fences.** "current-generation AI models", "using current models", "current usage patterns", "we do not currently see", "at least in this domain, … or tracking changes over time". Fifteen instances of `current*`. The whole document is time-stamped in its adjectives, which is how a first-wave measurement keeps the right to be wrong later.

**And the device the note does not use: numeric uncertainty.** No interval, standard error, bootstrap or minimum detectable effect appears anywhere. The only error bars in the document are the per-bin 95% CIs in the validation figure, and they are mentioned only in that figure's caption. For our own posts this is the gap to close, not the model to copy: the prose hedging here is exemplary and is *not a substitute* for a stated precision.

### Claude against AI, counted

`AI` and `Claude` appear 87 and 86 times in pp. 2–21 — as close to parity as the corpus gets. The split is systematic and mostly the right way round: **Claude** names the source ("conversations from Claude.ai", "Claude.ai transcripts"), the estimator ("Claude estimates", "Claude's estimates imply", "Claude thinks"), the subject of the task-level findings ("Claude speeds up individual tasks by about 80%", "Claude handles tasks of widely varying complexity") and the object of the caveats ("validating the quality or accuracy of Claude's work", "refine Claude's outputs"); **AI** names the quantity and the counterfactual ("without AI assistance", "time savings", "AI's economic effects", "AI productivity gains"). Three places invert it and are worth marking, because they are the ones a reader quotes: "Claude estimates that AI reduces task completion time by 80%" (p. 3), "People also use AI to save 87% of the time…" and "AI saves 80% of time on financial analyst tasks" (p. 10). In all three the measurement is Claude's and the actor is written as AI. Our rule fixes this by substitution — *Claude saves 80% of time on financial analyst tasks in this sample* — and the note's own Overview shows the fix is free.

## Comparisons

Every comparison that carries a finding, in the order it appears.

### Against Anthropic's own instrument — the licence to exist

> We've captured the **breadth** of uses—how people use Claude for legal, scientific, and programming tasks—but not their **depth**.

**Annotation.** The comparison is to the Economic Index's own published capability, not to a literature, and it is made in one antithesis with the two terms bolded. Everything that follows is licensed by it. For our own posts, whose whole premise is a thread Anthropic left open, this is the form: **name the published instrument, state in two nouns what it has and lacks, then spend the next paragraph proving the lack matters.**

### Against experiments and evaluations — disqualified by scope

> But these evaluations consider a narrow set of problems, rather than broad real-world use. To assess AI's overall impact on the economy, we need a way to analyze hundreds or thousands of real-world AI applications.

**Annotation.** Credit first, one shared limitation second, the requirement third and stated as a need rather than a boast. Compare the February 2025 paper's single-sentence disqualification of three literatures; this version is gentler, which is appropriate when the disqualified work is more rigorous than yours.

### Claude against human developers — the validation comparison

> This analysis suggests that Claude's estimates provide directional information that is only slightly worse than software developers' own estimates.

**Annotation.** The benchmark is not truth but a second estimator, chosen because it is the estimator whose place Claude is taking. The verdict adjective is graded before the numbers are read ("only slightly worse"), the numbers are given for both sides in the same three bullets, and the handicap is stated in the preceding sentence. **When validating an instrument against a human baseline, report the baseline's own accuracy first — it is usually the most persuasive number you have.** Here it is: ρ=0.50 for developers with full context sets the ceiling against which ρ=0.44 reads well.

### Against the published trials — the comparison the note loses

> Past randomized controlled trials have typically found smaller time savings, including 56%, 40%, 26%, 14% and even negative time savings across different applications—perhaps due to these effects or because these studies examined earlier generations of models.

**Annotation.** Five external results in one list, descending, ending on a sign change; the note's own ~80% is above all of them and the sentence does not restate it, so the reader performs the comparison. Two candidate explanations follow, both hedged by one "perhaps", offered as alternatives and not ranked. The honesty is in the placement — same section as the finding, not in an appendix — and the weakness is that the comparison is never adjudicated: the note does not re-cut its own data to the trials' task types, which is exactly what the February 2025 paper does when it re-computes its depth measure at Eloundou et al.'s 10% threshold. **If a post's estimate disagrees with the experimental literature, the strong move is to re-cut to the other design's threshold and put both thresholds in the sentence.** The weaker move, taken here, is to list the disagreement and gesture at two reasons.

### Against the historical record — the comparison that makes the number mean something

> This would nearly double the current long-term growth rate, which has averaged 2.1% per year since 1947 and 1.8% since 2019.

> Given that TFP growth has tended to be less than 1% since the early 2000s, these estimates suggest that, even broad deployment of current AI systems could cause growth to double: achieving the rates of the late 1990s, and of the 1960s and 1970s.

**Annotation.** Two bases with their windows, then a second comparison on the derived quantity with a *period* rather than a number as the yardstick ("achieving the rates of the late 1990s, and of the 1960s and 1970s"), which is how a macro number is made intuitive to a non-specialist without adding a digit. Note the asymmetry with the Overview bullet, which states the same comparison as "This would double the annual growth the US has seen since 2019": the body says *nearly double* against a 1947 base, the summary says *double* against a 2019 base. The comparison carries the finding, so a comparison restated with a different base is a different finding.

### Against the published estimates — the whole literature in a borrowed chart

> This estimated increase in aggregate labor productivity implied by task-level efficiency gains is within the range of recent estimates of AI's potential impact on productivity, though it lies towards the upper end (Filippucci, Gal, and Schief, 2024).

> **Figure 10: Predicted increase in annual labor productivity growth over a 10-year horizon due to AI.** Figure reproduced from Filippucci, Gal, and Schief, 2024. The dotted line is 1.8%, derived from Claude's estimates.

**Annotation.** The placement in the literature is done in one body sentence with a split verdict ("within the range … though it lies towards the upper end") and then discharged entirely to an appendix figure that is *someone else's chart with the note's own estimate drawn on it as a dotted line*. That is the cheapest legitimate way to place an estimate among rivals, and the caption is scrupulous about provenance: whose figure, and which line is the note's. Two costs, both worth stating. The body prose contains no rival estimate at all, so a reader of pages 2–21 never learns who else has estimated this or what they found; and the comparison sits after the BibTeX and the footnotes, behind an "Appendix" heading, which is the last place a reader looks. Per the figure-values ruling, no value is recorded from that chart in this file.

### Within occupations — accelerated against bottleneck tasks

> **Figure 9: Four different occupations along with "accelerated" tasks that show large potential time savings, and potential "bottleneck" tasks that do not appear in our sample.** For example, software engineers see large estimated time savings in developing and debugging software, but not in supervising programmers. Weekly time fractions are estimated by Claude (see previous section).

**Annotation.** The comparison is inside an occupation rather than across occupations, which is what makes the bottleneck argument legible at all, and both categories are put in scare quotes in the caption because both are the note's own coinages. The second category is defined by an absence in the data — "tasks that do not appear in our sample" — and the caption says so rather than calling them tasks AI cannot do. **A comparison between what was observed and what was not observed must define the second term as an absence in the sample, in the caption, or it will be read as a finding about the world.**

### Small comparisons that do the work of paragraphs

> a software developer might use Claude to write ten pull requests in a day, but if nine are minor documentation updates and one is a critical infrastructure change, simply counting the number of these tasks performed with Claude misses the point

> report-writing tasks that take five minutes from those that take five days

> AI might help a home inspector prepare reports, but if the inspector still has to spend the same amount of time physically traveling to the property

> growth may be constrained not by what we are good at but rather by what is essential and yet hard to improve

> transformative productivity improvements—from electrification, computing, or the internet—came not from speeding up old tasks, but from fundamentally reorganizing production

**Annotation.** Five one-clause comparisons, each carrying an argument that would otherwise need a paragraph: the pull requests establish that counts without magnitudes are uninformative; "five minutes … five days" gives the missing dimension a range; the home inspector converts an aggregation problem into a picture; the Aghion–Jones–Jones line states the growth theory; and the electrification sentence reframes the note's entire contribution as the lesser of two mechanisms. All five turn on a contrastive hinge — *but if…* in the first and third, *not X, but Y* in the fourth and fifth, a plain juxtaposition in the second — and three of them are about what the measurement misses rather than what it finds. Collect these; they are the sentences that survive summarisation.

## Figure captions

Ten numbered figures and one unnumbered table. The pattern is constant and it is the corpus's cleanest: **a bold lead sentence naming the exhibit and its cut, then unbolded sentences giving the operational definitions, the external data's vintage, the reading, and the limitation.** Captions sit below the exhibit in both versions (in the PDF the caption text begins about twenty points under the image's lower edge; on the web it is a `<figcaption>` after the `<img>`). No caption states a sample size — "100,000" appears once in the whole document, in the body on p. 5, and never in a caption — and no caption gives the conversation sample's date window, because **the document never states one anywhere**: the only dates in the note are the OEWS "May 2024" vintage, the historical growth windows, and citation years. Axis information is given only as a transformation where a transformation was applied (Figure 3's "Axes are log (base 10) scaled", Figure 7's definition of the plotted column); no caption names a unit.

> **Figure 1: An overview of our method and some of our main results.** See below for how we validate Claude's estimates, the assumptions we make, and limitations of our analysis.

**Annotation.** A schematic whose caption forwards the reader to the three things the schematic omits — validation, assumptions, limitations — naming all three. The equivalent in the February 2025 paper forbids the reader from treating illustrative conversations as data; this one forbids the reader from treating the summary figure as the argument. Both do their ethical work in the caption's last sentence.

> **Figure 2: Claude's estimated human completion times show high correlation across prompt variations.** Prompt 1 asks Claude to estimate the time it would take an "employee with appropriate skills" to complete and Prompt 2 asks about a "human worker" who is "competent in the relevant field." The two prompts show a log-scale correlation of 0.89, indicating high agreement. Analysis performed on Claude.ai transcripts where users have consented to share them with us for research purposes.

**Annotation.** The bold lead is the finding. Then the caption does something no other caption in the corpus does: **it quotes the instrument.** Both prompt variants are given in their own words, so the robustness check is auditable from the caption alone. ## Limitations

### The Limitations section, verbatim and in full (pp. 19–20)

> Our approach has several limitations that we think warrant further research on this topic:

> - **Claude's predictions are imperfect and we lack real-world validation of Claude's time estimates**: AI systems are imperfect predictors, and can't see activity that happens after the user finishes their interaction with the model. While we expect these estimates will improve with models capabilities, using model estimates introduces a significant source of noise. While our estimates show that models are approaching human performance at estimating task times, and humans are far from perfect themselves, we lack real-world data to validate the estimates that Claude provides.

> - **Task taxonomy limitations**: Real jobs are more complex than an O\*NET task list, and the time allocations we estimate for each task are only approximate. Many important aspects of work—tacit knowledge, relationships, judgment under uncertainty—don't appear in these formal task descriptions, and the connections between tasks may matter just as much or more to productivity as the time savings for those tasks in isolation. While we show large predicted time savings for individual tasks, a recent randomized controlled trial studying end-to-end software features did not see time savings due to AI.

> - **Structural assumptions:** In our calculations above, we compare the time it would take a professional to complete a given task without AI to the time it took with AI. But this could either understate the productivity gains – since it takes additional resources we're not accounting for to hire an employee and communicate context, and possibly overstate it, if the quality of the AI's work is worse than a human's.

> - **Restructuring of organizations:** Historically, the largest productivity gains for individual firms have followed from restructuring business operations to adopt new technologies. Our model can help predict the effects of such a restructuring, but it cannot predict how companies might decide to restructure, or how quickly this process might happen.

> - **The role of innovation:** Technological innovation is the engine of economic growth. Our model does not capture how AI systems could accelerate or even automate the scientific process, nor the effects that would have on productivity, growth, and the structure of work.

> - **Limited data**: Our dataset is derived from Claude.ai conversations only. This sample is not representative of the full spectrum of AI uses, and there's likely some selection effect where the instances of tasks people use Claude for are the ones they think Claude will be most useful. Additionally, due to our finite sample size, we likely miss some less common AI tasks.

> The measurement infrastructure we develop here enables continuous tracking of the effect of AI on time savings at large scale. As models improve and better methods address these limitations, we can re-estimate these time savings and identify how these capability improvements translate into broader economic impacts. We expect to track these changes in the months and years ahead.

**Annotation.**

- **Placement.** A 24 pt section of its own, after every finding and after the bottleneck conjecture, immediately before the Conclusion — later in the document than the February 2025 paper's §4.1 and later in the argument too, since the aggregate has already been stated five times by then. What makes the placement work anyway is distribution: by the time a reader reaches p. 19, they have met a caveat in the Overview prose, one inside Overview bullet two, one in the validation section, one in each findings subsection, two inside the method ("implicitly assumes"), three in the Findings paragraphs and two in captions. **The Limitations section here is a consolidation, not a confession**, and that is only available to a piece that has already conceded everything once.
- **Form.** Six bullets, each a bold run-in naming a *source* of error rather than the error: the estimator, the taxonomy, the counterfactual, organisational change, innovation, the data. Ordered instrument → world, with the two that bound the *ambition* of the exercise (restructuring, innovation) in the middle and the sample last. Not ranked, not numbered, and no bullet says which matters most — which is the section's main weakness, since the first and the third are of quite different severity.
- **The strongest concession is the last clause of the first bullet.** "While our estimates show that models are approaching human performance at estimating task times, and humans are far from perfect themselves, we lack real-world data to validate the estimates that Claude provides." Two reassurances in subordinate clauses, then the concession in the main clause. The syntax puts the comfort first and the admission in the position of emphasis, which is the right way round; a reader who reads only the main clauses of this bullet gets "AI systems are imperfect predictors … using model estimates introduces a significant source of noise … we lack real-world data to validate the estimates that Claude provides." **Write limitation bullets so that the main clauses alone are the honest version.**
- **Both directions of a bias, in one sentence, with a mechanism each.** The third bullet: "this could either understate the productivity gains – since it takes additional resources we're not accounting for to hire an employee and communicate context, and possibly overstate it, if the quality of the AI's work is worse than a human's." Neither direction is asserted, both are mechanised, and the asymmetry in modality ("could either" / "possibly") grades them. Elsewhere the note *does* sign its biases — "might overstate current productivity effects to at least some degree" (p. 3), "would result in smaller time savings" (p. 13), "these differences might be even greater in practice" (p. 10), "an approximate lower bound" (p. 17) — so the reader is left with four signed statements pointing both ways and no net. **A referee's first question about this document is which bias dominates, and it is answerable in principle from the note's own numbers; nowhere is it answered.**
- **The disconfirming study is inside a limitation.** "While we show large predicted time savings for individual tasks, a recent randomized controlled trial studying end-to-end software features did not see time savings due to AI." The single most threatening piece of external evidence is placed in the second bullet of the Limitations, cited by an unresolvable anchor phrase, and mentioned once more on p. 13 inside a list of five. It appears nowhere in the Overview. That is the placement decision a referee will attack first, and the lesson for our own drafts is the inverse: **evidence that contradicts the headline belongs in the section that states the headline.**
- **What the section does not cover.** The aggregation's own assumptions. Hulten's theorem's scope conditions ("in a competitive equilibrium without distortions", footnote 2), the ten-year horizon that mechanically sets the annualisation, the null-improvement rule for unobserved tasks, the labour-share parameter, the outlier-trimming rule of footnote 1 and the geography of the sample against US-only weights are all raised — honestly, and in the methods text where they are used — but none of them reaches the Limitations section, and none is varied. The sibling report's "What it did not test" enumerates seventeen such gaps. **A limitations section that omits the assumptions the methods section admitted is a limitations section a referee will rewrite for you.**
- **The closing paragraph converts the section into a commitment.** "The measurement infrastructure we develop here enables continuous tracking … we can re-estimate these time savings … We expect to track these changes in the months and years ahead." The same manoeuvre as the February 2025 paper's "We aim to provide the preliminary framework … for this further study to take place": the limitations end by redefining the work as infrastructure rather than as a result. It is the right ending for a first wave and it is only earned because the bullets above it are specific.
- **The caveats stated outside the section**, for completeness, since half the note's qualification lives there: the Overview's limits paragraph (p. 3); "so we think these estimates might overstate current productivity effects to at least some degree" (bullet two, p. 3); the compression paragraph (p. 8); "it is possible that these differences might be even greater in practice" (p. 10); "the actual performance of current models will likely be worse than a human expert for many tasks" (p. 11); the refinement-and-iteration paragraph with the five trial results (p. 13); the Figure 6 caption's last sentence; the two "implicitly assumes" sentences (p. 14); "this exercise assumes that AI capabilities … remain the same" (p. 16); "should be taken as an exercise … not a prediction" (pp. 16–17); "an approximate lower bound … does not account for unevenness in adoption" (p. 17); "few of their tasks appear in our data" (p. 17); and the Conclusion's "it cannot predict which changes will occur, or how quickly" (p. 21).

## Close

### The Conclusion, verbatim and in full (pp. 20–21)

> Claude handles tasks of widely varying complexity—from simple food preparation questions that would take a few minutes to complete, to complex legal and management tasks that would take multiple hours. But what is the aggregate effect of this work?

> Based on Claude's time estimates per task (and assuming universal adoption over the next 10 years), we find that current AI use of current models implies a potential increase in US labor productivity of 1.8% per year—a doubling of the recent rate of labor productivity growth. Based on current AI use, these gains would be concentrated in technology, education, and professional services, while retail, restaurants, and transportation sectors would see minimal impact. We'll be tracking these changes over time as part of our Economic Index as model capabilities, products, and adoption continue to progress.

> These productivity gains come from making existing tasks faster to complete. Historically, though, transformative productivity improvements—from electrification, computing, or the internet—came not from speeding up old tasks, but from fundamentally reorganizing production. In futures like these, AI not only makes implementing features faster, but companies restructure meetings and code review to validate and ship those features faster, whether using AI or through other means.

> Our framework could be used to help estimate the effects of such restructuring, but it cannot predict which changes will occur, or how quickly. An important direction for future work is understanding this question—to get a better understanding of when and how firms are reorganizing themselves around emerging AI capabilities. The answer will determine when AI makes the jump from providing significant but bounded productivity boosts, to representing the kind of structural transformation that has historically defined technological revolutions.

**Annotation.**

- **The close opens on the range, not the headline, and then asks the question again.** "Claude handles tasks of widely varying complexity—from simple food preparation questions … to complex legal and management tasks … But what is the aggregate effect of this work?" A question mark in the second sentence of a conclusion, restating the question the Introduction posed, so the close is structured as answer-to-question rather than as summary. **A close that re-asks the paper's question before answering it cannot read as a summary block**, and that is the device to take, since our template forbids one.
- **What was learned, with its two assumptions in the same sentence.** "Based on Claude's time estimates per task (and assuming universal adoption over the next 10 years), we find that current AI use of current models implies a potential increase in US labor productivity of 1.8% per year". Five fences in one sentence: *Based on*, *assuming*, *current … current*, *implies*, *potential*. The restatement carries as much qualification as the first statement on p. 15, which is the test a restated number should pass. It fails one sub-test: "a doubling of the recent rate of labor productivity growth" drops the "nearly" of p. 15. **The close is where hedges get lost; check every restated number against its first statement, word by word.**
- **The distributional claim is restated in words, without numbers.** "these gains would be concentrated in technology, education, and professional services, while retail, restaurants, and transportation sectors would see minimal impact." No shares, and the coverage caveat that governed this finding on p. 17 ("few of their tasks appear in our data") is not restated — so the close reads as a claim about sectors where the body made a claim about sample coverage. That is the one substantive slip in an otherwise scrupulous document, and it is the kind our claims list is meant to catch.
- **The inversion that stops the close reading as a template.** The third paragraph reframes everything the note measured as the less interesting kind of gain: "These productivity gains come from making existing tasks faster to complete. Historically, though, transformative productivity improvements—from electrification, computing, or the internet—came not from speeding up old tasks, but from fundamentally reorganizing production." A note that spent twenty pages measuring speed-ups ends by saying speed-ups are not where transformation comes from. The February 2025 paper ends the same way — a measurement paper concluding that measurement is not the point — and in both cases the inversion is what makes the ending feel written rather than assembled. **If the close can be inverted honestly, invert it; the sentence that concedes what your instrument cannot see is the one a reader remembers.**
- **What comes next, and its threshold.** One question, named as future work, with the stake stated as a discrete transition: "An important direction for future work is understanding this question—to get a better understanding of when and how firms are reorganizing themselves around emerging AI capabilities. The answer will determine when AI makes the jump from providing significant but bounded productivity boosts, to representing the kind of structural transformation that has historically defined technological revolutions." The next question is not a bigger version of this one; it is the question whose answer decides how to read this one. That is the strongest available form of "what comes next", and it is stated without promising a deliverable.
- **What is promised, and what is not.** Three commitments in the document, all measurement and all in the first person plural: track the estimates over time (Overview bullet five and the Conclusion), "share aggregate datasets that researchers can use to make their own forecasts and conclusions" (p. 6), and re-estimate as methods improve (p. 20). Nothing is promised about direction. Note that the one promise with a deliverable — the aggregate datasets — is not kept in this release: the note ships no data, no code and no intermediate aggregates, and the four prompts are the only artefacts. For our own posts that is precisely the gap the reproduction section and the evidence drawers exist to close.
- **Recommendations: none, to anyone.** There is no policy prescription anywhere in the document, no addressee, no "policymakers can", not even the February 2025 paper's subjunctive offer to collaborate. The words *policy*, *policymaker* and *recommend* do not occur in the note at all, and *should* occurs exactly once — addressed not to any actor but to the reader's *reading*: "this estimate should be taken as an exercise". A note that publishes a national growth-rate number and declines to advise anybody is a defensible position, and it is the one this piece takes; where our template asks for recommendations to Anthropic, the nearest model here is the named open question of the last paragraph, not advice.
- **Title against ending.** Title: "Estimating AI productivity gains from Claude conversations" — an act, a quantity, a source. Ending: the estimate is given, then relabelled as the bounded kind of gain, then made contingent on a question the note cannot answer. The title promises an estimate and the close delivers one and says what it is worth. **Where a title names an act of estimation, the close must state the estimate and its standing** — which is the test our own titles have to pass, and the reason the title and the ending have to be written together.
- **First person in the close.** "we find", "We'll be tracking", "Our framework". All three rebuild agentless without loss: *Claude's estimates imply…*, *the Economic Index will track…*, *this framework could be used to estimate the effects of such restructuring, but cannot predict which changes will occur*. The last one is the instructive case: deleting "Our" leaves the framework as the subject of its own limitation, which is stronger than a first-person concession.
