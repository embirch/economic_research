## Claims

The page has no pagination and numbers no figure, so each claim carries its H2/H3 section and, where the number lives in a caption, the figure label from the Source inventory. Numbers are transcribed as published. Denominators are stated where the page states them and flagged where it does not.

**Headline claims (§Introduction, bulleted "here are some examples of what we found")**

1. **Optimism dominates.** "**In our sample, people are optimistic about the role AI plays in their work.** Positive sentiments characterized the majority of topics discussed. However, a small number of topics such as educational integration, artist displacement, and security concerns, came with more pessimistic outlooks." (§Introduction.) *Comparison: topics against each other on sentiment, within the sample. The unit is the **topic**, not the participant — the claim is that most topics were positive, not that most participants were.* Note the tension with the topic-sentiment caption, which names "personal control, job displacement, and autonomy" as the topics that "did introduce pause", a different list from the three named here ("educational integration, artist displacement, and security concerns"); and with the general-workforce quotation table, which files "Educational integration" on the **optimistic** side.
2. **The general workforce defends identity tasks and delegates routine ones.** "**People from the general workforce want to preserve tasks that define their professional identity while delegating routine work to AI.** They envision futures where routine tasks are automated and their role shifts to overseeing AI systems." (§Introduction.) *Comparison: within-participant, across task types; no number attached at this point (see Claim 16 for the 48%).*
3. **Creatives use AI despite stigma.** "**Creatives are using AI to increase their productivity despite peer judgement and anxiety about the future.** They are navigating both the immediate stigma of AI use in creative communities and deeper concerns about economic displacement and the erosion of human creative identity." (§Introduction.) *Comparison: creatives against their own stated concerns; implicitly against the general workforce.*
4. **Scientists want partnership but withhold core research.** "**Scientists want AI partnership but can't yet trust it for core research.** Scientists uniformly expressed a desire for AI that could generate hypotheses and design experiments. But at present, they confined their actual use to other tasks like writing manuscripts or debugging analysis code." (§Introduction.) *Comparison: desired use against actual use, within the scientist sample. "Uniformly" is asserted; the nearest number is the 91% of Claim 25.*

**Sample composition (§Method → Participants)**

5. **1,250 interviews in three samples.** "To test Anthropic Interviewer, we had it run 1,250 interviews with professionals—the general workforce (N=1,000), scientists (N=125), and creatives (N=125)—about their views on AI." (§Introduction; restated §Method → Participants.) *1,000 + 125 + 125 = 1,250 (checked). The two specialist samples are each 12.5% of the total — far above either group's share of any national workforce, so the pooled 1,250 is not a workforce sample and the post never pools it into one. The page states no sampling frame, no target population and no weights, and reports no pooled statistic, so the over-sampling is a design choice with no stated correction.*
6. **General-sample occupational mix.** "1,000 of our participants were recruited from a general sample of occupations (that is, we did not select participants from specific jobs). Of that group, the largest subgroups came from educational instruction (17%), computer and mathematical occupations (16%), and arts, design, entertainment, and media (14%)." (§Participants.) *Comparison: subgroups within the general sample. The three named shares sum to 47%; the remaining 53% is never broken out. No comparison to any external occupational distribution (BLS/OES, ACS) is offered, so the reader cannot judge how far "general" departs from the workforce — and these three groups are among the most AI-exposed in the Index's own occupational rankings.*
7. **Creatives mix.** "The first was from creative professions: predominantly writers and authors (48% of the sample), and visual artists (21%), with smaller groups of filmmakers, designers, musicians, and craft workers." (§Participants.) *48% + 21% = 69%; the residual 31% is spread over four named groups without shares. **Note the mismatch with the creatives emotion charts**, whose six "most prominent creative disciplines" are Writers, Visual artists, Craftspeople, Designers, Filmmakers and **Game developers** — a group absent from this sentence, while **musicians**, named here, is absent from the charts.*
8. **Scientists mix.** "The second was from science, which included physicists (9%), chemists (9%), chemical engineers (7%), and data scientists (6%), with representation across 50+ other distinct scientific disciplines." (§Participants.) *The four named shares sum to 31% of 125, i.e. roughly 39 people; "50+ other distinct scientific disciplines" over the remaining ~86 implies modal cell sizes of one or two. **The scientists emotion charts add Mechanical engineers and Astronomers** to the six most prominent disciplines, neither named here, and each therefore plotted on a cell of at most ~8 interviews.*
9. **Recruitment channel.** "We intend for the tool to interview general Claude.ai users, but for this initial test, we sought participants working across a range of professions and engaged them through crowdworker platforms (all participants had an occupation other than crowdworking that was their main job)." (§Participants.) *Comparison: stated intent (Claude.ai users) against what was done (crowdworker panels). No platform is named, no screening or attention-check procedure is described, and no incentive is stated.*
10. **Why the two specialist samples.** "We chose to add these two specialist subgroups because these represent professional domains where AI's role remains contested and is rapidly evolving. We hypothesized that creatives and scientists would reveal distinct patterns of AI adoption and professional concerns." (§Participants.) *This is the page's one stated hypothesis; see Open questions.*
11. **Interview length.** "The interviews conducted by Anthropic Interviewer appeared on Claude.ai and lasted about 10-15 minutes with each participant." (§Method → Interviewing.) *Approximate; no distribution, no median, no completion or drop-out rate.*

**General workforce (§Results → AI's impact in the general workforce)**

12. **Time saved and satisfaction.** "In the survey, 86% of professionals reported that AI saves them time and 65% said they were satisfied with the role AI plays in their work." (§AI's impact in the general workforce.) *Comparison: none — two one-sample proportions. Source is the **survey**, not the interview; the question wording, scale and any threshold for "satisfied" are not published. Base presumed N=1,000 but not stated.*
13. **Stigma.** "69% of professionals mentioned the social stigma that can come with using AI tools at work". (§AI's impact in the general workforce.) *Comparison: none stated; contrast with Claim 21 (70% of creatives) is the implicit one. The unit is **mentioned in the interview** — a Clio-derived prevalence, so a floor on the attitude rather than a measurement of it.*
14. **Secure vs anxious, and the remediation split.** "Whereas 41% of interviewees said they felt secure in their work and believed human skills are irreplaceable, 55% expressed anxiety about AI's impact on their future. 25% of the group expressing anxiety said they set boundaries around AI use (e.g. an educator always creating lesson plans themselves), while 25% adapted their workplace roles, taking on additional responsibilities or pursuing more specialized tasks." … "Notably, only 8% of professionals expressed anxiety without any clear remediation plan." (§AI's impact in the general workforce.) *Comparison: the secure group against the anxious group, and within the anxious group by response. **The bases change mid-paragraph**: 41% and 55% are of interviewees; the two 25% figures are "of the group expressing anxiety"; the 8% is again "of professionals". 41% + 55% = 96%, leaving 4% in neither category. Within the anxious 55%, boundaries (25%) and role adaptation (25%) leave 50% × 55% = 27.5% with neither of those two responses, against 8% said to have no remediation plan at all — so "remediation plan" must cover categories beyond the two described, which the page does not enumerate. No claim here is testable from the released transcripts as published, because the classifier outputs are not released.*
15. **Self-reported augmentation exceeds observed augmentation.** Body: "In the Anthropic Interviewer data, 65% of participants described AI's primary role as augmentative; 35% described it as automative. Notably, this differed from our latest analysis of how people use Claude, which showed a much more even split: 47% of tasks involved augmentation and 49% automation." Caption (augmentation-versus-automation figure): "Augmentation versus automation in professionals' self-reports to Anthropic Interviewer compared with observed Claude usage in the Anthropic Economic Index. Professionals described their AI use as 65% augmentative and 35% automative, while actual Claude conversations showed 47% augmentation and 49% automation. Economic Index percentages do not sum to 100% as some interactions were unclassified." (§Augmentation versus automation.) *This is the page's only claim that rests on an Economic Index number, and the most load-bearing claim in the post for our programme. Three things about the comparison, each checked by the wiki author: (i) **the Index side reproduces the third report exactly** — `wiki/reports/economic-index-2025-09-blog.md` Claim 32 records "for the first time, automation (49.1%) has become more common than augmentation (47%) overall", so 47/49 here is the August 2025 Claude.ai wave, rounded; (ii) **the two sides are not the same unit** — 65/35 is a share of **participants** classified on their described *primary* role, 47/49 is a share of **tasks** (Claude.ai conversation records) in the collaboration facet; (iii) **the denominators differ and the page says so for one side only** — 65 + 35 = 100 exactly, a forced binary, while 47 + 49 = 96 with an unclassified residual. Renormalising the Index side to the classifiable base gives augmentation 47.0/96.1 = 48.9% and automation 49.1/96.1 = 51.1%, so the gap the post describes is 65 against 49, not 65 against 47. The post does not perform this renormalisation.*
16. **Career reorientation toward overseeing AI.** "Professionals envisioned a future with both augmentation and automation—the automation of routine, administrative tasks with the maintenance of human oversight. 48% of interviewees considered transitioning their careers toward positions that focus on managing and overseeing AI systems rather than performing direct technical work." (§Augmentation versus automation.) *Comparison: none; one proportion. Base "interviewees" — the general workforce sample, on the placement of the paragraph, but not stated.*
17. **Barred professions expect policy to move.** "Professionals who were currently barred from using AI at work—for example, some lawyers, accountants, and healthcare workers—anticipated policy changes that would let them automate many tasks in the future." (§Augmentation versus automation.) *No number. A qualitative claim about a subgroup whose size is not published.*
18. **Uniform emotional profiles in the general workforce.** "Different professions exhibited remarkably uniform emotional profiles characterized by high levels of satisfaction. However, this was coupled with frustration, suggesting professionals are finding AI useful while encountering significant implementation challenges." (§AI's impact in the general workforce, after the emotion charts.) *Comparison: six O\*NET occupational categories against each other on six emotions. **No number is published for any emotion in any occupation** — the values exist only as positions on six radar-chart images, which per `room/director-2026-09-16-alt-text-ruling.md` are not recorded. The images carry empty alt text.*

**Creatives (§Results → AI's impact on creative professions)**

19. **Time saved.** "97% reported that AI saved them time" (§AI's impact on creative professions.) *Comparison: implicitly against the general workforce's 86% (Claim 12) — an 11-point gap the page states both halves of but never names as a comparison, tests, or notes could be a sampling artefact on N=125.*
20. **Quality improvement.** "68% said it increased their work's quality." (§AI's impact on creative professions.) *Comparison: none. No equivalent quality figure is published for the general workforce or for scientists, so this measure exists for creatives only.*
21. **Peer judgment.** "Similarly to the general sample, 70% of creatives mentioned trying to manage peer judgment around AI use." (§AI's impact on creative professions.) *Comparison: explicitly against the general sample's 69% (Claim 13). The comparison is the finding: a 1-point difference on bases of 125 and 1,000, presented as similarity — correctly, but with no interval given.*
22. **Universal desire for control, unstable in practice.** "All 125 participants mentioned wanting to remain in control of their creative outputs. Yet this boundary proved unstable in practice: Many participants acknowledged moments where AI drove creative decisions." (§AI's impact on creative professions.) *Comparison: stated preference against reported behaviour, within participant. 125/125 = 100% — the only saturated measure in the post, and the one most exposed to the demand-characteristics limitation the page itself names.*
23. **Divergent emotional profiles across creative disciplines.** "Disciplines exhibited divergent emotional profiles as seen in the figure above: game developers and visual artists reported high satisfaction, paradoxically paired with elevated worry. Designers showed an inverse pattern dominated by frustration with notably low satisfaction. Across all disciplines, trust remained consistently low, suggesting shared uncertainty about AI's long-term implications for creative work." … "The wide dispersion across the emotional spectrum confirmed that different creative professions experienced AI integration through very different emotional lenses." (§AI's impact on creative professions.) *Comparison: six creative disciplines against each other; and, in the word "divergent", against the "remarkably uniform" general workforce of Claim 18. **No number is published for any cell.** The six disciplines partition at most 125 interviews, of which writers are 48% and visual artists 21%, so designers — the discipline carrying the strongest claim here — is one of the "smaller groups" of Claim 7 and its cell size is not published.*

**Scientists (§Results → AI's impact on scientific work)**

24. **Barriers.** "Trust and reliability concerns were the primary barrier in 79% of interviews; the technical limitations of current AI systems appeared in 27% of interviews." … "Nevertheless, regulatory compliance constraints, concerns about skill atrophy, and cost barriers were each brought up in less than 10% of interviews." (§AI's impact on scientific work.) *Comparison: barrier types against each other, within the scientist sample (base 125). "Primary barrier in 79% of interviews" and "appeared in 27% of interviews" are different constructs — primacy versus mere appearance — reported in one sentence as if commensurable. Below 10% of 125 is fewer than 13 interviews.*
25. **Desire for more assistance, and its shape.** "91% of scientists expressed a desire for more AI assistance in their research, even if they didn't feel today's products fit the bill. Roughly one-third envisioned assistance primarily with writing tasks, but the majority wanted support across all of their research: critiquing experimental design, accessing scientific databases, and running analyses." (§AI's impact on scientific work.) *Comparison: within the 91%, writing-only against research-wide.*
26. **Scientists do not fear displacement, and why.** "Scientists didn't, in general, fear job displacement due to AI." — supported by tacit knowledge that "resists digitization", the human nature of research decision-making, classified-environment restrictions, and budget/time/specimen limits (§AI's impact on scientific work.) *Comparison: scientists against creatives, on displacement anxiety. No number for either side of that comparison; the creatives' anxiety is evidenced by quotation only, and the scientists' absence of it by the word "in general".*
27. **Computational versus experimental frustration.** "Most scientific fields reported high satisfaction, but with divergent frustration patterns: physicists and data scientists showed higher frustration, whereas chemical and mechanical engineers displayed minimal frustration." … "Trust remains relatively low across all fields". … "Unlike creative professionals who express high levels of concern about AI's impact, scientists show relatively low worry levels." (§AI's impact on scientific work.) *Comparison: computational against experimental fields, and scientists against creatives. **No numbers; radar-chart images only.** Note also that the scientists' chart caption names a different emotion set from the other two samples — "hope, satisfaction, frustration, worry, doubt, and trust" against "hope, worry, satisfaction, frustration, relief, and trust" — so **doubt replaces relief**, and the cross-sample comparisons in this claim and Claim 23 are not made over the same six axes.*

**Instrument performance (§Appendix → Participant experience with Anthropic Interviewer)**

28. **Participants rated the interview experience very highly.** "We found that participants were remarkably positive about Anthropic Interviewer. 97.6% of participants rated their satisfaction as 5 or higher, with 49.6% giving the highest rating. Similarly, 96.96% felt the conversation captured their thoughts well (5-7 rating). 99.12% of participants said they would recommend this interview format to others." (§Appendix.) *Comparison: none — no human-interviewer control arm, no alternative format, no benchmark from the survey-methods literature. Base is presumably all 1,250 but is not stated; the mixed precision (97.6, 49.6 to one decimal; 96.96, 99.12 to two) implies bases of ~1,250 without naming one. On 1,250, 99.12% is 1,239 and 96.96% is 1,212.*
29. **Feasibility claim for the method.** "This initial test demonstrated that Anthropic Interviewer shows promise at scale—we were able to conduct 1,250 interviews with a range of professionals to understand their feelings regarding AI at work. Research with this many participants would have been expensive and time-consuming with traditional "manual" interview methods." (§Looking forward.) *Comparison: against traditional manual interviewing, on cost and time. **Neither cost nor time is quantified for either arm**, and no traditional-method comparison was run.*

## Definitions (verbatim)

Every definitional statement on the page, quoted with its location. Where a construct is used without definition, that is recorded as an absence.

**The instrument**

- **What Anthropic Interviewer is** — "We built an interview tool called Anthropic Interviewer. Powered by Claude, Anthropic Interviewer runs detailed interviews automatically at unprecedented scale, feeding its results back to human researchers for analysis. This is a new step in understanding the wants and needs of our users, as well as gathering data for the analysis of AI's societal and economic impacts." (§Introduction.)
- **Its three stages** — "Anthropic Interviewer operates in three stages: planning, interviewing, and analysis. Below, we describe each of them in turn." (§Method → How Anthropic Interviewer works.) Caption of the three-stages diagram: "The three stages of Anthropic Interviewer's process."
- **Planning** — "In this phase, Anthropic Interviewer creates an interview rubric that allows it to focus on the same overall research questions across hundreds or thousands of interviews, but which is still flexible enough to accommodate variations and tangents that might occur in individual interviews." (§Planning.)
- **The system prompt as the methodology** — "We developed a system prompt—a set of overall instructions for how the AI model is to work—to give Anthropic Interviewer its methodology. This was where we included hypotheses regarding each sample, as well as best practices for creating an interview plan (this was established in collaboration with our user research team)." (§Planning.)
- **Plan generation and human review** — "After putting the system prompt in place, Anthropic Interviewer used its knowledge of our research goal (see section below) to generate specific questions and a planned conversation flow. There was then a review phase where human researchers collaborated with Anthropic Interviewer to make any necessary edits to finalize the plan." (§Planning.)
- **Interviewing** — "Anthropic Interviewer then conducted real-time, adaptive interviews following its interview plan. At this stage, we included a system prompt to instruct Anthropic Interviewer how to use best practices for interviews." (§Interviewing.)
- **Delivery surface** — "The interviews conducted by Anthropic Interviewer appeared on Claude.ai and lasted about 10-15 minutes with each participant." (§Interviewing.) Caption of the interface screenshot: "The interviews were conducted on an interface like this on claude.ai (above is the module now live for users)."
- **Analysis** — "Once interviews were complete, a human researcher collaborated with Anthropic Interviewer to analyze the transcripts. Anthropic Interviewer's analysis step takes as input the initial interview plan and outputs answers to the research questions alongside illustrative quotations. At this stage, we also used our automated AI analysis tool to identify emergent themes and quantify their prevalence across participants." (§Analysis. "automated AI analysis tool" links to Clio.)
- **The quantitative layer** — "We ran interviews to produce qualitative data, and supplemented them with quantitative data from surveys where participants answered questions on their behavioral and occupational backgrounds. We also had a separate AI analysis tool read the interview transcripts and cluster together emergent, overarching themes from the unstructured data—for example, on the percentage of participants who mentioned a specific topic or expressed a specific view in their interview." (§Method, opening. Both "a separate AI analysis tool" links point to Clio.)
- **What the instrument is for, as against Clio** — "We recently designed a tool to investigate patterns of AI use while protecting our users' privacy. It enabled us to analyze changing patterns of AI use across the economy. But the tool only allowed us to understand what was happening within conversations with Claude. What about what comes afterwards? How are people actually *using* Claude's outputs? How do they feel about it? What do they imagine the role of AI to be in their future? If we want a comprehensive picture of AI's changing role in people's lives, and to center humans in the development of models, we need to *ask people directly*." (§Introduction. The italics are the source's.)

**The constructs**

- **Augmentation and automation** — "In a previous analysis, we categorized AI uses into either *augmentation* (where AI collaborates with a user to perform a task), or *automation* (where AI directly performs tasks)." (§Augmentation versus automation. Italics the source's; "previous analysis" links to arXiv 2503.04761.) *This gloss is the post's entire definition of the pair. It does not reproduce the Index's five collaboration patterns, does not say which patterns aggregate to which pole, and does not state how a participant's interview was classified into one pole — only that "65% of participants described AI's primary role as augmentative". Per `/mnt/memory/standards/terminology.md`, collaboration is the name of the whole facet and automation = Directive + Feedback Loop, augmentation = Task Iteration + Learning + Validation; none of that structure appears here.*
- **The unclassified residual, Index side only** — "Economic Index percentages do not sum to 100% as some interactions were unclassified." (augmentation-versus-automation caption.)
- **Topics and sentiment** — "The different topics people discussed in their interviews with Anthropic Interviewer. Across all three samples we studied—the general workforce, scientists, and creatives—participants expressed predominantly positive sentiments about AI's impact on their professional activities. Certain topics did introduce pause, particularly around questions of personal control, job displacement, and autonomy. In this diagram, topics are roughly ordered from more pessimistic to more optimistic." (topic-sentiment caption, repeated verbatim on all three tabs.) *"Roughly ordered" is the only statement of how the ordering was produced; no sentiment score, scale or cut-point is defined, and no topic's prevalence is published.*
- **Topic categories, as labelled in the quotation tables** — general workforce: "**Career adaptation.**", "**Writing independence.**", "**Societal perspectives**.", "**Educational integration.**"; creatives: "**Control boundaries.**", "**Writer displacement.**", "**Workflow automation.**", "**Music production.**"; scientists: "**Security concerns.**", "**Content verification.**", "**Research assistance.**", "**Code development.**". (The three quotation tables; each table's columns are headed "Pessmistic"/"Optimistic" in the general-workforce table — the typo is the source's — and "Pessimistic"/"Optimistic" in the other two.) *These twelve are the only topic names given anywhere in text. The full topic list plotted in the topic-sentiment diagram is not published, nor is the number of topics.*
- **Emotions (general workforce and creatives)** — "Intensity of the top six emotions—hope, worry, satisfaction, frustration, relief, and trust—surfaced during interviews with professionals in the general workforce from six major occupational categories from the O\*NET database. Each axis represents one emotion, with distance from the center indicating the percentage of interviews in which participants expressed that emotion; points farther from the center reflect higher prevalence. We analyzed these emotions across the six most prominent occupational categories in our sample." (general-workforce emotion caption.) "Intensity of the top six emotions—hope, worry, satisfaction, frustration, relief, and trust—reported during interviews with creative professionals. The axis denotes the percentage of interviews in which the participant expressed the given emotion. We analyzed these emotions across the six most prominent creative disciplines in our sample." (creatives emotion caption.)
- **Emotions (scientists) — a different set** — "Intensity of the top six emotions—hope, satisfaction, frustration, worry, doubt, and trust—reported during interviews with scientists. The axis denotes the percentage of interviews in which the participant expressed the given emotion. We analyzed these emotions across the six most prominent scientific disciplines in our sample." ## Data and methods

The wiki author's words, with references to the page and to the dataset card.

- **Design.** A one-shot, non-experimental, cross-sectional qualitative study whose declared main purpose is instrument testing ("our main intention was to perform a practical test of Anthropic Interviewer", §Research goals). Three purposive strata; no control arm, no randomisation, no weighting, no follow-up wave. Each participant sat one AI-conducted interview of about 10–15 minutes on Claude.ai, plus a background survey and a three-item experience survey. Field dates are not published anywhere on the page; only the publication date (2025-12-04) is known.
- **Sample.** N = 1,250: general workforce 1,000, creatives 125, scientists 125. Recruited through unnamed crowdworker platforms, all with a non-crowdworking main job. Composition is published only as the shares in Claims 6–8. No country, age, sex, tenure, firm-size, income or education breakdown appears; the only geographic statement is in the Limitations — "Our sample primarily reflects Western-based workers". All participants consented to public release of their transcripts.
- **Three measurement layers, of which only one is released.** (i) **Interview transcripts**, analysed qualitatively by a human researcher working with Anthropic Interviewer, which "outputs answers to the research questions alongside illustrative quotations". (ii) **Clio-style thematic classification** of the transcripts, which produced every "% of interviews/professionals mentioned X" number in the post (Claims 13, 14, 21, 22, 24, 25) and every emotion chart; the page calls it "our automated AI analysis tool" and links to Clio (`wiki/reports/clio-insights-2024-12.md`). (iii) **A background survey** on "behavioral and occupational backgrounds", source of Claims 12, 16, 19, 20 and, by its placement, 25. The post names no model version for any layer.
- **Classifier detail.** None beyond the above. No prompt, no taxonomy, no codebook, no inter-rater or human-validation check, no classifier-accuracy figure, no seed, no temperature, no model name. Where the Index reports publish a validation appendix for their classifiers, this post publishes nothing; there is no methodology appendix.
- **The released dataset.** `Anthropic/AnthropicInterviewer` on Hugging Face (fetched 2026-09-16; the card and the two directory listings only — **no data file was opened**, per `team/agents/programme-lead.md`). Recorded from the card and file tree: one subset, `AnthropicInterviewer`, 1,250 rows total, in three splits — `workforce` 1,000 rows, `creatives` 125, `scientists` 125 — matching the post's strata exactly. Two columns, per the card's viewer header: `transcript_id` (string, length 9 — the preview shows `work_0000`, `work_0001`, … so ids are split-prefixed and zero-padded) and `text` (string, 4.84k–26.8k characters, i.e. each row is one whole interview as a single field, speaker-turn-prefixed: the preview rows begin "Assistant: Hi there! I'm Claude from Anthropic's research team…"). Format CSV; language English; modality text; size category 1K–10K; total 11.4 MB. File tree at `main`: `interview_transcripts/creatives_transcripts.csv` (1.12 MB), `interview_transcripts/scientists_transcripts.csv` (1.07 MB), `interview_transcripts/workforce_transcripts.csv` (9.25 MB), plus `README.md` (1.7 kB) and `.gitattributes`; five commits, one contributor (`kunal-anthropic`); 1,130 downloads in the month before the fetch; ten community discussions; 40 models listed as trained or fine-tuned on it.
- **Documentation of the release.** The card is the entire documentation: a three-line description, the split sizes, "All participants provided informed consent for public release of their raw interview transcripts", a licence line, a contact address and the same BibTeX key as the post (minus the `date` field). There is **no data dictionary, no codebook, no field dates, no occupation field, no demographic field, and no schema documentation** of the kind `Anthropic/EconomicIndex` releases ship as `data_documentation.md`. **Licence discrepancy to record:** the repository's sidebar metadata reads "License: mit", while the card body reads "Data released under CC-BY, code released under MIT License" — and no code is present in the repository, so the MIT clause covers nothing that was released. Anyone relying on the licence should resolve this before publishing derived text.
- **What the release does and does not permit.** It permits re-reading all 1,250 interviews and re-deriving themes from scratch. It does **not** permit reproducing a single published number in this post: the 86%, 65%, 69%, 41%, 55%, 25%, 25%, 8%, 65/35, 48%, 97%, 68%, 70%, 79%, 27%, 91% and the four Appendix percentages all come from the survey layer or the classifier layer, neither of which is released, and the transcripts carry **no occupation, no discipline, no demographic and no survey field** — so the general-workforce mix of Claim 6, every occupational emotion chart, and every discipline-level claim (Claims 23, 27) cannot be reconstructed, even approximately, from the public data. This is the single most important feasibility fact about the release and any brief that proposes to use it must state it. *(A definitive column- and value-level profile of these three CSVs belongs to the data steward; nothing above rests on opening a file.)*
- **The one Economic Index quantity used.** 47% augmentation / 49% automation, taken from the third report's companion post. The post does not state the wave, the window, the surface or the sample size on the Index side, nor that the Index shares are of classifiable records. Those facts sit in `wiki/reports/economic-index-2025-09-blog.md` and `wiki/reports/economic-index-2025-09-report.md`: V3 = August 2025, Claude.ai only, collaboration facet, automation 49.1% / augmentation 47.0%.
- **Statistics.** None. No confidence interval, no standard error, no significance test, no regression, no weighting, no sensitivity check, no multiple-comparison adjustment, and no cell size for any subgroup chart anywhere on the page. Every comparison in the post — creatives' 97% against the general workforce's 86%, creatives' 70% against the general workforce's 69%, the 65/35 against the 47/49, and all eighteen radar-chart contrasts — is a comparison of point estimates, several of them on bases of 125 or smaller.
- **External literature cited.** Two links, both in the Limitations, both for the proposition that self-reported use diverges from measured use: <https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S277250302300021X> and <https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2451958824002021> (glossed on the page as "the case" for "smartphone use"). Not fetched in this thread and therefore not characterised here beyond the link text. No other external work is cited anywhere in the post; there is no reference list.

## Limitations (verbatim)

The page carries an H3 `Limitations` under `Conclusions and limitations` — a genuine, itemised limitations section, unusual in this corpus for naming self-report bias against the post's own headline finding. Quoted in full, with the framing sentences that precede it and the other bounding statements elsewhere on the page.

**The framing**

- "Our initial use of Anthropic Interviewer has some important limitations that affect the scope and generalizability of our findings. Our findings should be interpreted as early signals of AI's impact on work, rather than definitive conclusions about its long-term effects on professional practice and identity." (§Limitations, opening.)

**The eight named limitations, verbatim**

- "*Selection bias.* Because they were engaged through crowdworker platforms, the experiences of the participants in our study might differ significantly from those of the general workforce, biasing responses toward more positive or experienced perspectives on the subject."
- "*Demand characteristics.* Participants knew they were being interviewed by an AI system about their AI usage, which could have changed their willingness to engage, or changed the kinds of responses they gave compared to an interview with a human."
- "*Static analysis.* We captured a snapshot of professionals' current AI usage and attitudes, but with these data, we can't track how these relationships develop over time, or how initial enthusiasm might change with extended use."
- "*Emotional analysis.* As Anthropic Interview is text-only and can't read tone of voice, facial expressions, or body language, it might miss emotional cues that affect the meaning of our interviewee's statements." *("Anthropic Interview" — the missing "er" is the source's.)*
- "*Self-report versus objective measures.* We noted above that participants' descriptions of their AI usage might differ from their actual practices (as has been found to be the case for smartphone use). This could be due to social desirability bias, imperfect recall, or evolving workplace norms around AI disclosure."
  - "Indeed, our interview data revealed key discrepancies when compared with real usage data. This gap between perception and practice reinforces the inherent ambiguity in self-reports: for example, interview responses may capture aspirational usage or social desirability effects. Understanding these discrepancies will be crucial for interpreting the findings in this kind of research."
- "*Researcher interpretation.* Like all qualitative research, our analysis reflects our own interests and perspectives as researchers. Although we used systematic methods to identify patterns, different researchers might emphasize different aspects of these interviews or draw alternative conclusions."
- "*Global generalizability*. Our sample primarily reflects Western-based workers, and cultural attitudes toward AI, workplace dynamics, and professional identity likely vary significantly across global contexts."
- "*Non-experimental research.* Although many participants reported productivity gains and quality improvements, we cannot determine whether AI usage directly *caused* these outcomes or the extent to which other factors contributed."

**Bounding statements elsewhere on the page**

- "Note that, in this initial study, our main intention was to perform a practical test of Anthropic Interviewer; the goals below nonetheless provided interesting data which we analyze below." (§Research goals.)

## What it did not test

*The wiki author's inference, not the post's own statements.* Each item is something a reader of this page cannot learn from it, or a test the design leaves open.

1. **The self-report/usage gap is never measured within a person.** Claim 15 compares a December 2025 crowdworker panel's self-descriptions against an August 2025 Claude.ai conversation sample — different people, different periods, different units (participants vs tasks), different denominators (100 vs 96), and an unknown overlap in AI provider. The post lists five explanations and tests none. Its own §Looking forward promises exactly the missing design (interviewing AI for Science grantees *and* reading their Claude conversations); as of this page it had not been done. **This is the single largest open hole in the post and the most obvious place for outside work, but note carefully what it would require: participant-level links between interview text and usage data, which the public release does not contain.**
2. **Renormalisation is not done, so the headline gap is overstated as presented.** The post sets 65% against 47% (an 18-point gap) when the like-for-like figure on the classifiable base is 48.9% (a 16-point gap). The direction survives; the magnitude as printed does not. Nothing on the page acknowledges this.
3. **The unit mismatch is not addressed at all.** "65% of participants described AI's primary role as augmentative" is a modal, person-level, self-classified label; "47% of tasks" is a record-level classifier output. A person whose conversations are 55% automation and 45% augmentation is "automative" on one construct and contributes to both shares on the other. The post does not say how a participant's interview was mapped to a single pole, so the 65/35 split is not reproducible even in principle.
4. **No number in the post is reproducible from the released data.** The transcripts carry only `transcript_id` and `text`. Every percentage comes from the unreleased survey layer or the unreleased classifier layer, and the transcripts carry no occupation, discipline, demographic or survey field. So the release supports *new* qualitative analysis of the same interviews and supports *nothing* by way of replication — including the occupational and disciplinary emotion charts, which cannot be rebuilt because the stratum labels beyond split membership are absent.
5. **Cell sizes are never published for any subgroup.** Eighteen radar charts are drawn over six occupational categories, six creative disciplines and six scientific disciplines. Creatives and scientists are 125 each; the named shares imply that "designers", "craftspeople", "game developers", "mechanical engineers" and "astronomers" sit in the residual tail — plausibly single-digit cells. The strongest discipline-level claims in the post (designers' inverted profile; chemical and mechanical engineers' minimal frustration) rest on exactly those cells, with no N, no interval, and no noise check.
6. **The emotion axes differ between samples, and the post compares across them anyway.** General workforce and creatives are plotted on hope/worry/satisfaction/frustration/relief/trust; scientists on hope/satisfaction/frustration/worry/doubt/trust. The sentence "Unlike creative professionals who express high levels of concern about AI's impact, scientists show relatively low worry levels" compares two charts whose axis sets were selected separately, by an unstated "top six" rule. Whether "worry" means the same thing when "doubt" is available as an alternative label is untested.
7. **"Intensity" is measured as prevalence.** All three captions promise intensity and deliver "the percentage of interviews in which the participant expressed that emotion". A profession where every participant mentions mild frustration outranks one where a third are furious. No intensity scale exists in the published measure, and the post's readings ("high levels of satisfaction", "elevated worry", "minimal frustration") are all prevalence readings dressed as intensity.
8. **Classifier validity is never checked.** No human-coding comparison, no inter-rater agreement, no accuracy figure, no model version, no prompt, no sensitivity of any percentage to the classifier. The Index's own reports rerun a headline share under a second classifier and report the difference (V3: 45% vs 49% automation under Sonnet 3.7 vs Sonnet 4); this post, whose numbers are *all* classifier outputs, reports no such check.
9. **Interviewer effects are named and not measured.** "Demand characteristics" is listed as a limitation; no arm of the study was run with a human interviewer, with a non-AI-branded interviewer, or with the AI-topic concealed. The post's own instrument-performance numbers (97.6% satisfied, 99.12% would recommend) are themselves collected by the instrument under evaluation, from participants paid to complete it — the most demand-characteristic-exposed measurement in the post, and the one offered as evidence the method works.
10. **No benchmark for the method's claimed advantage.** "Research with this many participants would have been expensive and time-consuming with traditional 'manual' interview methods" — no cost, no hours, no comparison study, and no assessment of *quality* against a human-interviewer baseline. Whether AI interviews elicit comparable depth, or systematically shallower or more agreeable answers, is the central methodological question the pilot does not pose.
11. **Selection is named but never bounded.** Crowdworker recruitment is acknowledged to bias "toward more positive or experienced perspectives", with no attempt to quantify the bias: no comparison of the general sample's occupational mix to BLS/OES or ACS, no weighting, no comparison to a probability panel, no reported AI-experience distribution. Note that the three largest general-sample groups (education 17%, computer/mathematical 16%, arts/design/media 14%) are precisely the occupations most over-represented in Claude usage in the Index — so the panel may lean AI-experienced in exactly the way the limitation fears, and the page gives the reader no way to check.
12. **No geography beyond one clause.** "Primarily reflects Western-based workers" is the whole of it: no country counts, no language, no region. The Index's cross-country results (AUI, the automation–adoption gradient) cannot be linked to attitudes here, and the post makes no attempt to.
13. **No time dimension, by the post's own admission, and none available.** "Static analysis" is listed as a limitation. A single wave, undated. There is no earlier Interviewer wave to compare against, and the transcript release carries no timestamp, so even a later reuse of this dataset cannot place it in time more precisely than "before 2025-12-04".
14. **Occupation is self-reported and never validated.** The Index's occupational measure is inferred from the task; here, occupation comes from a background survey and the specialist strata come from screening. The two are therefore not comparable constructs, and the post — which uses "six major occupational categories from the O\*NET database" for its charts — never says so. Any post that joins this survey's occupational cut to an Index occupational cut must handle that mismatch explicitly.
15. **The topic layer is almost entirely unpublished.** The topic-sentiment diagram is the post's lead figure and the basis of its lead finding ("Positive sentiments characterized the majority of topics discussed"), yet no topic list, topic count, topic prevalence or sentiment score is published, and the diagram is an image. Twelve topic names survive, in the quotation tables. The lead finding is therefore unauditable, and it is a claim about topics, not about people — a majority of topics being positive is consistent with a minority of participants being positive.
16. **The optimism headline and the anxiety numbers are never reconciled.** The same sample is "optimistic about the role AI plays in their work" (§Introduction) and 55% "expressed anxiety about AI's impact on their future" (§AI's impact in the general workforce). Both can hold — present-tense satisfaction with future-tense anxiety — but the post never states that as the finding, never cross-tabulates the two, and never reports how many participants held both.
17. **Productivity and quality claims are self-reported and uncorroborated.** 86% "AI saves them time", 97% of creatives, 68% "increased their work's quality", a writer going from 2,000 to over 5,000 words a day, a photographer from 12 weeks to 3. No output measure, no logged time, no independent quality rating, no baseline. The post names this ("Non-experimental research") and stops there. Nothing in the released data allows anyone else to go further.
18. **The four vivid magnitudes are single anecdotes presented without distribution.** 2,000→5,000 words; 12 weeks→3; "$2,000 per day" photographer lost; "60% AI, 40% my ideas". Each is one participant. No distribution of any of these magnitudes is reported, and the post does not say how typical any is — while these are the numbers most likely to be quoted onward.
19. **No wage, employment, price or firm outcome appears anywhere.** The post's economic content is entirely attitudinal and self-reported. Displacement is discussed exclusively through participants' fears and one voice actor's report that "Certain sectors of voice acting have essentially died"; no employment or earnings series is consulted for voice acting, illustration, copywriting or any other named occupation. The obvious test — do the occupations whose practitioners report the most displacement anxiety show it in labour-market data — is neither run nor mentioned.
20. **Anxiety is never related to measured exposure.** The post has occupational labels (for its own analysis, unreleased) and attitudes; the Index has occupational task shares and automation shares. Whether anxiety tracks measured Claude exposure at the occupation level is not tested here — a gap later addressed for a different instrument and sample by `survey-81k-economics-2026-04` ("perceived job threat against observed exposure", per `wiki/INDEX.md`), which post-dates this one by five months and is not a follow-up to it.
21. **The stigma finding is not tested against disclosure behaviour.** 69% and 70% "mentioned" stigma; nothing measures whether stigma suppresses use, changes the channel of use (personal account versus employer account), or biases the self-reports elsewhere in the same interview. The post raises "evolving workplace norms around AI disclosure" as a source of self-report error and does not follow it into the data it holds.
22. **Employer permission is mentioned once and never measured.** "Professionals who were currently barred from using AI at work" — no prevalence, no occupational breakdown, no comparison of barred and unbarred participants on any measure, although this is precisely the institutional variable that would separate "won't" from "can't" in the automation/augmentation split.
23. **Scientists' and creatives' specialist strata are never compared to their counterparts in the general sample.** The general sample already contains 14% arts/design/entertainment/media and some scientists. Whether the purposively recruited creatives differ from the creatives who arrived in the general sample — a direct check on the specialist-recruitment design — is not reported.
24. **The instrument's own design choices are not varied.** One rubric per sample, one system prompt, one 10–15 minute length, one adaptive policy, one analysis pipeline. No arm varies the prompt, the length, the question order or the degree of adaptivity, so the sensitivity of any result to the instrument is unknown — in a post whose stated main purpose was to test the instrument.
25. **Adaptivity is itself an unmeasured source of variation.** Because the interviews were "real-time, adaptive", no two participants answered the same questions. The post never reports how much the realised interviews diverged, nor whether a prevalence such as "69% mentioned stigma" is confounded with how often the interviewer chose to raise stigma. The released transcripts make this checkable by others; the post does not check it.
26. **The licence question is unresolved in the release.** Sidebar "mit" against a card body reading "Data released under CC-BY". Anyone quoting the transcripts at length needs this settled and cannot settle it from the published material.
