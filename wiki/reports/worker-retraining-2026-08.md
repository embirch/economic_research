## Claims

Numbers are reproduced as published, with the PDF page. Tables are pointed at, not transcribed (`room-protocol`). The report's own framing is comparative throughout: almost every headline is a treatment–control difference, and the ones that matter most for policy are differences *between* classes of programme.

### The question and the evidence base

1. **Why the question is asked at all.** "Among experts and the public, worker retraining is the most popular policy for mitigating labor market disruption from AI.¹ In this report, we ask if these efforts would work." (PDF p.2; footnote 1 is "Karger et al. (2026), Figure 16."). Restated on PDF p.4: "in a recent survey of economists, AI experts, and superforecasters, 'Retraining support' was the most favored policy response to AI-driven job loss, beating out enhanced unemployment insurance, a jobs guarantee, and universal basic income (Karger et al. 2026)." *Comparison:* retraining against the other named policy responses, in an outside forecasting survey.

2. **The evidence base.** "We screened the literature for high-quality randomized trials, drawing on 146 impact estimates from 56 randomized trials conducted in the United States from 1973 to the present. The programs trained job seekers—mostly low-income adults and youth—for positions such as nursing aides, IT support technicians, and welders. On average, they run six months and cost roughly $13,000 per person." (PDF p.2.)

3. **What else the search turned up.** "We find 7 randomized or discontinuity-based studies of interventions in Europe involving training; a pair of judge-randomization studies from the US and Denmark; and 56 randomized studies in the US." (PDF p.5.)

4. **The claim to comprehensiveness.** "No previous meta-analysis has included nearly so many US-based randomized studies." (PDF p.5.) Quantified on PDF p.56: "Thirty-seven of the 56 studies are in Smedslund et al. (2006), zero are in Haelermans and Borghans (2012), 2 are in Card, Kluve, and Weber (2018), and 26 are in Peck et al. (2021)." The arXiv abstract's form is "the most exhaustive meta-analysis in this niche".

5. **Scale of the policy being assessed.** "In 2017, the US government spent $14 billion per year on job search assistance, counseling, and worker retraining, and reached an estimated 10.7 million people (GAO 2019, Figure 4)." (PDF p.4.) Against that: "A recent White House report described these programs as 'largely ineffective' (CEA 2019)." (PDF p.4.)

### The headline results

6. **Average impacts are positive but small — the report's central finding.** "The programs in which training was a primary component lifted employment by an average 2.8 percentage points in the second year after randomized assignment to treatment, and 1.7 points in years 3–5. The impacts on pre-tax earnings are $1,139 and $791 per year (in 2025 dollars). All these averages are highly significant, statistically." (PDF p.6, §1.1, pointing to Table 2 in §6.5.2.) The executive-summary form, with the base rate attached: "programs on average increased employment by 1.7 percentage points, compared to a baseline employment rate of 63% in the control group. They increase earnings by $800 per year on average. These figures are per person offered treatment, which matters because compliance in these studies is often low." (PDF p.2.) *Comparison:* treatment against control, at ~2 years and at 3–5 years.

7. **Table 2 is the spine of the paper** (PDF p.58). It gives, for each of two samples — all 146 estimates from 56 studies, and the 78-estimate training-primary subset — a control-group mean, a REML impact estimate, both standard errors and an N, for eight outcomes: programme take-up, any-training take-up, and employment and earnings at each of three horizons. That is sixteen impact estimates; the text's reading counts twelve (see the internal-disagreement table above). The text's reading (PDF p.59): "In Table 2, 11 of the 12 mean impact estimates are 2–4 times their standard errors, making them highly significant. (All are ITTs, expressed per person offered treatment.) The exception is for the short-term impact of training-primary programs on earnings, which may reflect temporary earnings losses while in training." The table's own note fixes the estimator and the horizons (PDF p.58): "Impacts are estimated with restricted maximum likelihood (REML) meta-analysis. Control group averages are computed using the corresponding REML weights… Sample sizes vary because of missing data."

8. **The same result stated as a proportion.** "From a base employment rate of about 60%, it boosts the chance of having a job by 2.6-2.8 points in the medium term (year 2) and 1.7–1.8 points in the longer term… The average program lifts pre-tax earnings by 7.8% in the medium term, meaning by $1,000–1,100/year from $12,500 in the full sample and $14,700 in the training-primary sample. The long-term earnings impacts are 4.6%, i.e., $700–800 from bases of $14,400 and $16,100 (all figures in 2025 dollars)." (PDF p.59.)

9. **Costs, and the benefit–cost verdict.** "Among the 67% of training-primary interventions whose write-ups include cost information, costs average $13,598 per treatment group member. Fully accounting for the benefits requires projecting the long-run impacts, since most studies observe subjects for just a few years. We estimate that the net present value (NPV) of impacts on earnings, tracking a stream of benefits that lasts to retirement, is $14,146. It rises to $19,525 when including employer-paid taxes and other fringe benefits. (See §6.5.3 and Appendix G.) In light of the great uncertainties in the projection of impacts, this result should be read as a little better than break-even." (PDF p.6.)

10. **The fiscal result, which is the one a policymaker would quote.** "Interestingly, training programs ultimately generate flows to government equal to about three-quarters of their costs, through higher tax revenue and lower use of public welfare. So when government funds them, the long-term fiscal cost is only ~24% of the upfront cost.⁶" (PDF p.6.) Footnote 6 immediately qualifies it: "The payback is substantially lower if Social Security contributions are not viewed purely as taxes, but as securing future benefits for which the government is liable." The five summary statistics are "a narrow B-C ratio of 1.44, a wide one of 1.80, and an MVPF of 2.52", "covers some 76% of its costs", and "The societal IRR is 5.97%" (PDF p.61; the full ledger is Table G1, PDF p.122).

### The large federal programmes

11. **JTPA.** "In the late 1980s, the Job Training Partnership Act (JTPA) lifted employment among low-income adults by about 2.3 points, from a base of about 70%.⁷ It lifted annual pay by $1,100 in 2025 dollars, from about $13,000 for women and $17,500 for men. There were no clear benefits for low-income youth (GAO 1996)." (PDF p.7; footnote 7: "Figures approximate average results for years 3–5. See Figure 4.") The §4.2 reading of the same evidence: "The significant impacts amount to 3 percentage points. The JTPA experiment boosted the wage earnings of adult women and men by $500–600/year, with some tapering in year 5. There are no clear impacts for young people." (PDF p.28.)

12. **Job Corps.** "It did not affect employment or earnings in follow-ups extending 20 years, except for a transitory 1–2 percentage point employment bump in years 3–5 (Schochet 2021)." (PDF p.7.) The §4.3 verdict: "Unfortunately, the strong signal from Figure 5—essentially no impact overall—looks like the main verdict from the Job Corps evaluation." (PDF p.34.)

13. **WIA.** "An evaluation of the Workforce Investment Act, the successor to the JTPA, returned essentially zeros for the programs for low-income adults and for dislocated workers (Fortson et al. 2017). Estimates were as often negative as positive and lacked statistical significance. One challenge was a low uptake differential: many people offered training did not take it, while many in the control group found training anyway. As a result, the treatment-control difference in participation was only 15 percentage points." (PDF p.7.) §4.4's own power statement: "The WIA evaluation unfortunately turns out to have little power to detect the effects of job training. The treatment-control difference in training take-up was modest, 16 percentage points at best. And relatively little was spent on training. The delivered training may have boosted employment and earnings. But when the ITT is statistically indistinguishable from zero, so is the LATE." (PDF p.35.) Note the 15-point figure on p.7 and the 16-point figure on p.35 for the same quantity.

14. **The summary of the federal record.** "We are aware of no other instance of a national government committing this much energy to rigorous assessment of whether a family of social programs works. Unfortunately, the answer, at least from the national trials of the JTPA, Job Corps, and WIA, is that the main-line programs have not moved the needle much on labor market outcomes." (PDF p.36.)

15. **National Supported Work Demonstration, for scale and for the founding result.** Funding: "five departments of the federal government joined the foundation in pouring a remarkable $82.4 million into the demonstration and randomized evaluation of supported work (four times that in today's dollars…)" (PDF p.17); sample: "Across 10 sites, 6,616 people entered the experiment, evenly split between treatment and control" (PDF p.18). Result for AFDC women: "The treatment-control difference—the ITT—settles at 7.1 percentage points (statistically significant at p < 0.1)" (PDF pp.20–21); ex-addicts "17.2 percentage points in the last quarter" (PDF p.21); earnings "supported work boosts pay by $75/month in the final quarters of tracking, or $900/year" (PDF p.21). Long-run: "the earnings bump averaged $441/year (in 1978 dollars) in 1982–86… That is about half the $900/year MDRC found through surveys circa 1979." (PDF p.23.)

16. **The most quotable caveat in the whole report — the denominator behind a headline impact.** "A passing statement in the final report (MDRC 1980, p. 43, note 1) suggests that roughly a third of women eligible for the program applied. Thus, the job impact among eligibles was about 8/3 ≈ 2.5 points." (PDF p.24.) And: "The balanced take here is that a) boosting employment 8 points in a group chosen precisely because it was hard to help is an achievement and b) the intervention did not come close to working for everyone." (PDF p.24.)

17. **Self-reported versus administrative earnings, quantified.** In the Job Corps study, "the 1998 treatment-control difference is much larger going by self-report than by Social Security data, at $972 versus $218. The difference between these two differences is itself highly significant, statistically (SBM, Table 4)." Recomputing the administrative difference on the self-reporting sample "nearly doubles the Social Security treatment-control difference, to $393, and strongly suggests that attrition bias is present in the self-reports." (PDF p.31.) In the JTPA 30-month report, for previously arrested young men, "The contrast between the two is extraordinary: –$4 versus –$4,209." (PDF p.27.)

18. **A worked example of selective highlighting in a published evaluation.** "the employment TOT for the 20–24-year-old Job Corps entrants was 4.2 percentage points in 2015, with statistical significance. However, across the whole follow-up period, this employment TOT is generally not as large and statistically significant… And the most statistically significant result in the figure is the 4.6-percentage-point employment reduction for the 18–19-year-olds in 2013 and 2014. That result is in itself hard to explain, and is not highlighted." (PDF p.33.) The report's own discipline in response: "In presenting and assessing the evidence, to avoid inadvertently filtering for promising or significant results, we treat the two outcomes and three age groups symmetrically, showing results for all." (PDF p.32.)

### Compliance, and the gap between ITT and LATE

19. **The compliance wedge.** "In our collection of trials, treated individuals are 66 percentage points more likely to receive the targeted training and only 28 percentage points more likely to receive any training compared to the control group." (PDF p.7.) Detail (PDF p.59): "In training-primary interventions, the experimental offer of entry is accepted 75% of the time, while only 9% of the control group manages to access the treatment. Dividing that difference, 66%, into the ITTs quoted just above produces what we call 'LATE-program' estimates 50% larger. Similarly, the impact on participation in any training program is only 28%, because many control group members find alternative trainings. If these substitutes are equally effective, then the impacts of training per se—the values of 'LATE-training'—are 1/0.28 ≈ 4 times the ITTs reported in the lower rows of Table 2."

20. **What the per-trainee rescaling does and does not change.** "We find that this estimate, likely an upper bound, is about 2–4 times higher than our main estimates, although it does not change the benefit-cost picture." (PDF p.7.)

21. **Why the ITT/LATE choice matters for the AI question, stated as an argument rather than a result.** "But the LATE could indicate a program's potential to help in a context where it is more needed and wanted (provided benefits do not fall much as participation broadens to a larger swath of people). This could make the LATE a better fit for modeling a world of AI-driven job displacement: one could imagine a limited supply of training and much more readiness to train among those offered seats. On the other hand, this readiness might also increase training receipt and general resourcefulness among those not offered spots." (PDF p.14.) The choice made: "We default to the ITT in what follows since it's easier to define and seems just as likely to fit the future labor markets we imagine given the high uncertainty." (PDF p.14.)

### Dislocated workers, and the two judge-randomization studies

22. **The dislocated-worker evidence is thin and mixed** (PDF pp.7–8, §1.5). Denmark: "an average 52 days of classroom-based training helped people change occupations; the effect was possibly larger for people coming out of occupations exposed to offshoring. The study does not detect similar effects for on-the-job training (Humlum, Munch, and Rasmussen 2025)." US: TAA "workers who could access the support did go on to work more and earn more. Sometimes they did so by changing occupations or locations. Since the assistance included both training and an extension of unemployment insurance, the effects of training per se cannot be distinguished (Hyman 2018)." Pilots: "Randomized studies of pilot programs for dislocated workers in New Jersey (Corson et al. 1989) and Texas (Bloom 1990) find that a combination of job search assistance and training was no better than job search assistance alone for reducing unemployment within 12 months."

23. **Hyman (2018), the numbers the report carries forward.** Instrument relevance: "each 10 percentage point increase in an investigator's average approval rate lifts the probability of approval in individual cases by 6 points, and with great statistical significance" (PDF p.39). Balance: the F test on all correlations "returns a p value of 0.18… There is only an 18% chance that this much correlation would be measured if the treatment were unrelated to all of the controls, which is not strongly reassuring." (PDF p.40.) Main result: "Hyman (2018, p. 24) calculates that workers net an extra 20 months of work and $50,000 in earnings over the 10 years." (PDF p.40.) Mobility: "people who could tap TAA assistance were 20–40 points more likely to move to a new commuting zone. Impacts were similar for moving to a new industry. These findings are big if true: transitional assistance indeed helps people transition to better work settings after economic disruption." (PDF p.41.) Verdict: "We view the topline results as plausible but are unsure how much to attribute them to training rather than the income support during training." (PDF p.41.)

24. **Humlum, Munch and Rasmussen (2025), the largest impact in the review.** Sample: "the HMR sample consists of 167,222 unemployment episodes experienced by 127,713 people, who saw any of 536 caseworkers between 2012 and 2017" (PDF p.44). Result: "Being assigned classroom training increases employment by about 26 hours/month in the second year after entering the unemployment system (HMR, Table D.2). Dividing that by a typical 160 hours/month yields a substantial 16% increase in employment, which is among the very largest in this review… being assigned to the classroom training increases employment seven quarters after the start of the unemployment spell by about 20 percentage points" (PDF p.45). Contrast with on-the-job training: "for on-the-job training, the confidence intervals are about twice as wide and the central impact estimates are in general statistically indistinguishable from 0. Despite this imprecision, there is enough statistical power to reject the hypothesis that classroom and on-the-job training have about the same impact. The p values for this hypothesis range between 0.03 and 0.22 for quarters 6–11" (PDF p.46). Offshoring cut: "The impact estimates for the more-exposed are over twice as large, though the difference is not statistically significant (p = 0.14 for hours, weaker for other outcomes)." (PDF p.47.) The report's discount: "the study's results taken together are surprising, which causes us to put somewhat less weight on them." (PDF p.43.)

25. **The European and discontinuity-based evidence, which the report reviews but does not pool.** Appendix C's opening states the asymmetry and the reason the report offers for it (PDF p.109): "High-quality evidence on training's impacts is much thinner outside the US. In Europe, perhaps one reason social experiments are seldom run is that entitlements such as individualized workforce assistance are viewed more as rights that should not be arbitrarily withheld for research." What Europe supplies is "three efforts that ultimately do not generate strong and clean experiments, and an American-style evaluation in the UK in which training was a secondary component" (PDF p.109). The UK case (the ERA) is the only one with a training result, and it runs the wrong way: "Across the three target groups, training was thus anti-correlated with employment and earnings impacts. There are many ways to explain that pattern, so we do not view it as evidence that training does harm. Still, the evaluation of the ERA provides little evidence that these offers of government training helped." (PDF p.114.) Take-up in the ERA is the reason offered: "only a minority of participants took advantage of these incentives: about 15% in the NDLP group, 33% in the WTC group, and 10% in the ND25+ group (Hendra et al. 2011, Table 3.5)" (PDF p.113). Appendix D adds the two regression-discontinuity studies — "We found two RDD-based studies of training, and review both here" (PDF p.114) — and closes the second with "The answer: not really, as far as we can tell" (PDF p.115). *Comparison:* the European and discontinuity evidence against the US randomised evidence; none of it enters the new meta-analysis, which is US-only (PDF p.55).

### The sector-programme result

26. **The headline.** "Some 'sector programs' boost earnings 10 times as much. A few programs have lifted pay by $5–10,000 per year by intensively screening applicants, involving employers in deciding what to teach, and tracking local trends in the demand for skills. From the government's perspective, these programs clearly pay for themselves through higher tax revenue and lower use of public benefits." (PDF p.2.)

27. **Cost and benefit, side by side — the comparison the result rests on.** "As a group, the sector programs cost no more than training-primary programs—$11,602 versus $13,598 per treatment group member, on average. Yet the discounted present value of their impacts on pre-tax earnings comes to $60,319 instead of $14,146. The 7x benefit-cost ratio is not as large as that for the most successful ones. But it is still enough that if the government successfully ran such programs, they would pay for themselves through higher tax revenues and lower use of public benefits." (PDF p.9.)

28. **The sector meta-analysis proper** (Table 6, PDF p.76; the same eight rows as Table 2, for the expanded family of sector programmes, N between 6 and 17). The text's reading: "In all four plots, point estimates range from negative to positive. Yet the random-effects means are positive, with statistical significance at p < 0.05 in all cases except in the fourth (long-term impact on employment). Subject to the caveat that the sample of studies shifts from plot to plot, sector programs on average cause a partially transient employment bump, about 3.0 points, and a more permanent increase in pre-tax pay, $3,000–4,000/year in 2025 dollars." (PDF p.76; forest plots are Figures 13 and 14, PDF pp.77–78.)

29. **Sector LATEs.** "The average sector experiment generated a 56-point differential in participation in the program being evaluated, as shown in Table 6; that implies that each LATE-program is 1.79 times the corresponding ITT. As with training programs more generally, the impact on take-up of any training is about 25%. However, if alternative training programs are substantially less effective than the tested sector programs, then the LATE-training, at 4 times the ITT, should not be interpreted as an estimate of the impact of sector programs." (PDF p.76.)

30. **Selection into sector programmes is visible in the control groups — the report's own best evidence against its headline.** "The control-group means in Table 6 also capture differences in the populations served, reflecting the selectivity of sector programs. For experiments incorporating training, the long-term employment rate among controls is 60% compared to 80% for sector programs. The control groups from sector programs also earn 50–75% more depending on the time horizon. Sector programs draw from populations that are higher paid even without training." (PDF p.76.)

31. **The sector benefit–cost figures.** "we estimate the benefit-cost ratio of sector programs at 7.18, almost five times the 1.44 for the larger group. (See Table G2 in the appendix.) If the government were to fund a statistically average sector program, it would pay for itself twice over. As a result, the MVPF would be infinite, meaning that positive good would be produced at negative fiscal cost." (PDF p.77; Table G2 is PDF p.123, giving B/C narrow 7.18, B/C broad 8.07, MVPF ∞, government net/gross cost –1.88, societal IRR 34.03%.) Compare Appendix G's wording, "thrice over" (PDF p.121), recorded in the divergence table above.

32. **The multiple, restated more conservatively after the sample is widened.** "In sum, this critically motivated expansion of the collection of studies in Katz et al. fundamentally reinforces the optimism about sector programs. Even when averaging in programs that did not manage to work closely with employers, the earnings impacts are 3–5 times higher than for the average job training program." (PDF p.77.)

33. **Selectivity, quantified.** "They filter out >80% of applicants. Their admissions processes select for workers that employers would probably not find on their own, but who have the grit and smarts to succeed." (PDF p.2.) "An evaluation of Per Scholas and three other programs reports that the programs take about 1 in 5 applicants (Hendra et al. 2016, p. 34)." (PDF p.9.) "The four WorkAdvance programs performed 'intensive screening' of applicants, based on staff judgments about subtle qualities such as commitment and motivation. They accepted about 20% of applicants (Hendra et al. 2016, p. 29). In 2025, 70,000 people applied for 5,000 spots at Per Scholas." (PDF p.78.)

34. **Replication failure, quantified.** "An attempt to replicate one strong program, the CET in San Jose, failed at all 14 trial sites, including 4 run by the CET itself outside of San Jose (Miller et al. 2005). Only two replications of the sector approach have boosted clients' incomes in a randomized trial: the expansion of Year Up outside its Boston home (Fein and Dastrup 2022); and St. Nicks Alliance in New York, one of three new sites in the WorkAdvance demonstration (Yusim et al. 2025, Table 3)." (PDF p.10.) And: "In our report, we found three promising programs that were deliberately replicated or scaled and subjected to randomized evaluations: the Center for Employment Training (CET), replicated at 14 sites; Per Scholas, expanded through the WorkAdvance demonstration; and Year Up, scaled from three cities to eight. CET failed at every site and WorkAdvance's new sites did worse than the original; only Year Up showed consistent gains." (PDF p.16.)

35. **The standout programme, and its cost.** "Year Up boosted incomes by an extraordinary $8,000/year, with the impact showing no diminishment after seven years (Fein and Dastrup 2022, Exhibit 2-1), although this also had standout costs of around $30,000 per participant. The other eight PACE evaluations produced no clear impacts on employment or earnings." (PDF p.68.) And in WorkAdvance: "However, 7–10 years after randomization, the impact of St. Nicks rose to $8,000/year, with statistical significance (Yusim et al. 2025, Table 3)." (PDF p.68.)

36. **The hiring-commitment result, which outcompetes the sector-programme trait in their own regressions.** "the apparently powerful impact of an employer committing to hire graduates—$8,000–9,000 in year 2 and beyond—owes to three programs: the Wildcat program that inspired the NSWD, Year Up, and the Wisconsin Regional Training Partnership." (PDF p.63, reading Table 3.) Summary form: "Programs backed by a hiring commitment from employers increase the earnings of admitted students an order of magnitude more. The result is driven by a small number of studies but is intriguing in light of the excitement about 'sector programs,' to which we will soon turn." (PDF p.65). And, on the definition: "(In our meta-analysis in §6.5, this explanatory factor is outcompeted by whether employers commit to hiring graduates.)" (PDF p.72.)

### Correlates of impact

37. **The meta-regressions** (Table 3, PDF p.62; eight columns, employment and earnings at medium and long term, with and without multiple imputation, on the "survivors" of block-by-block selection; the selection rule itself is quoted in `Data and methods` from PDF pp.52–53). The text's reading (PDF p.63): "We see that the medium- and long-term impacts on employment have fallen by about 0.07 points per year since the 1970s. Programs tested in the South (the reference region in the first panel) did not work as well for employment; employment impacts in the other regions (Northeast, Midwest, West) were 2–4 percentage points higher. Classroom training showed no clear advantage, and it appears harder to help dislocated workers." The sample is also widened for this exercise: "For statistical power, the sample includes all studies, not just training-primary ones" (PDF p.63), and year-1 impacts are dropped.

38. **Who the training worked for — the heterogeneity the table carries and the text mostly does not.** Table 3's target-population panel (PDF p.62, omitted category welfare or low-income adults) is where the report's subgroup results live: coefficients for substance-abuse, justice-involved, dislocated-worker, youth and disability populations, for employment and earnings at two horizons. The text names only three of them — the substance-abuse and justice-involved impacts as driven by one study, the disability impacts as driven by two, and dislocated workers as "harder to help" (PDF p.63) — and says nothing about the negative youth coefficients, which are legible only in the table. Every one of these comparisons is governed by the report's own cross-group caveat (PDF p.61, quoted under `Limitations`). The values are not transcribed here; read Table 3 on PDF p.62.

39. **Strong coefficients that rest on one or two programmes — flagged by the authors themselves.** "Other statistically strong results are associated with small numbers of programs. The sole program with long-term results that expressly targeted people with drug problems or past involvement with the law—the National Supported Work Demonstration—entirely drives the impressive impacts on the 'justice-involved.' The two programs that served people with intellectual disabilities boosted employment about 8 points in the long term." (PDF p.63.) Also, in footnote 49: "The relatively low impact on programs for dislocated workers solely reflects the null results of the WIA (§4.4)." (PDF p.63.)

40. **Training works better when unemployment is high — and the mechanism the authors favour is not the flattering one.** "Where the unemployment rate is 1 point higher, a training program boosts employment by 1.2 points more in the medium term (year 2 after randomization) and earnings by $250/year. However, the association is much weaker in the longer term (years 3–5)." (PDF p.63.) The favoured explanation: "A final explanation is that when unemployment spikes, competition for training increases. That reduces the alternatives available to the control group, against whom results in the treatment group are benchmarked. In effect, each training program becomes more needed and less redundant… that could lift the effect of offering training (ITT) even without any change in the impact of training on the trained." (PDF pp.63–64.) The supporting evidence: "when we switch from participation in the program of interest to participation in any training program, the medium-term association with the unemployment rate becomes even bigger and quite statistically strong, at 4.7 points (standard error 1.8)." (PDF p.64.)

41. **Persistence.** "This 'persistence' term, shown in the top row of the table in Appendix F, is statistically strong throughout. It is smaller in the medium term for employment (as expected), but not for earnings (surprisingly). In the long-term regressions without multiple imputation, the medium-term impacts gain coefficients of 0.48 and 0.52. This suggests that the medium-term associations with unemployment mentioned above—1.2 points of employment impact and $250/year of earnings impact—persist into years 3–5 at half those levels." (PDF p.64; the table is Appendix F, PDF pp.117–118.) The conclusion drawn: "Overall, the results relating to the unemployment rate suggest that training helps people bounce back faster after losing their jobs. However, the lack of longer-term association suggests that local job market conditions do not matter for whether training puts people on a permanently better work track." (PDF p.64.)

### The four prior meta-analyses, as this report reads them

42. **Smedslund et al. (2006).** "The programs increased employment by 9.7% (not 9.7 percentage points) in the first year, 9.2% in the second year, and 3.7% in the fifth year… The parallel figures for earnings impacts are 4.3%, 4.4%, and 1.1%, the last being difficult to distinguish from 0" (PDF p.48); "programs that included skills training raised employment by 5.6% and earnings by 4.5%… They are statistically indistinguishable from the averages for programs that did not incorporate skills training." (PDF p.49.)

43. **Haelermans and Borghans (2012) — the publication-bias benchmark.** "Across the 71, and across the various follow-up periods, the random-effects mean impact on wage earnings is 3.9%, with great statistical significance." Then: "Haelermans and Borghans (2012, Table 5, row 1, penultimate column) estimate that adjusting for this publication bias cuts the average wage impact of on-the-job training from 3.9% to 2.6%. Turning that around, in this collection of non-randomized academic studies, publication bias inflates the typical reported impact by 50%." (PDF p.49.)

44. **Card, Kluve and Weber (2018).** "This second edition of the work gathers 857 impact estimates from 207 papers… Training programs are best represented, with 418 estimates. Of these, 54 result from randomized experiments" (PDF p.50). Findings carried over: "Job training lifts employment by 1.4–2 percentage points in the first year from the start of training, and 4.0–4.6 points more than that in year 2 and beyond"; "The average impact of job training on the long-term unemployed is 12 points higher than for 'regular' recipients of unemployment insurance"; "The average impact is 6 points higher for women"; "The average impact is 3 points higher for those younger than 25 than those older"; programmes "increased employment by about 3 points extra for each 1-point rise in the national unemployment rate or 1-point drop in economic growth"; "This meta-analysis finds little publication bias"; "Experimental estimates are about 1 point lower on average than non-experimental ones, but that result has essentially no statistical significance." (PDF p.51.) The report's own weighting of these: "We find the results for the time-profile of impacts—low in the first year, then rising—especially plausible. We are less confident in the subgroup findings, causally interpreted." (PDF p.51.)

45. **Peck et al. (2021), whose trait coding this report borrows.** "career pathways programs increased employment in the professions they targeted—by an impressive 72%, from 26 percentage points to 45 points… More broadly, the programs increased total employment by about 6 points, from a base of about 60%… Impacts on earnings are broken out by timeframe; they amount to $1,040/year in the first three years after treatment, over a base of $16,320." (PDF p.52.) On its method: "the study compiles a list of more than 75 program traits… Coding these traits—assigning the 0's and 1's for some 75 traits and 46 studies—constitutes a distinctive contribution. It will inform our analysis of sector programs in §7." (PDF p.52.)

46. **Where the five agree and disagree.** "The studies concur on the first-order question: average impacts on earnings or employment are positive, though not transformational. There is little consistency on the second-order question of which traits of an intervention are associated with greater impact. One reason is that different studies check different traits. Another may be that these meta-regression findings are fragile given the small samples and intercorrelations among traits." (PDF p.48.)

### The extrapolation to AI

47. **The premise, stated as an absence of evidence.** "We have no research evidence on how well job training helps people adjust when large language models start doing their jobs. In search of partial insight, this report nevertheless reviews the available evidence on job training programs." (PDF p.80.)

48. **Who is at risk, as the report frames it from Anthropic's own prior work.** "Americans' top concern about AI is job loss (Anthropic 2026). If current data on AI usage are any guide, college-educated workers—software developers, paralegals, accountants—are most at risk (Massenkoff and McCrory 2026). But this is highly uncertain. Fast advances in robotics and autonomous vehicles would affect different jobs. And the employment of less-educated white-collar workers, like those in data entry or customer service, might fall sooner (Richmond 2026)." (PDF p.4.)

49. **The adaptive-capacity framing, taken from Manning and Aguirre (2026).** "On average, workers measured as more exposed to AI also have more wherewithal to adjust. That is somewhat reassuring. Still, there are big exceptions. For example, 1.7 million people work as 'secretaries and administrative assistants, except legal, medical, and executive'; they score high on AI exposure (59%) and low on adaptive capacity (14%), which puts them in the lower right of the plot." (PDF p.82; Figure 15 is copied from that study.)

50. **The four lessons, §8.1–8.4** (PDF pp.83–84), verbatim as section headings: "The sort of short-duration programs that are prominent in this review could help some 'low-skill' workers displaced by AI."; "Short-duration programs are ill-matched to the needs of more-skilled workers."; "The Trade Adjustment Assistance (TAA) approach is worth considering for people who are demonstrably fired because of AI."; "Public and private funders should aggressively support demonstrations, evaluations, and scaling of sector programs."

51. **The one optimistic mechanism the report offers.** "And there is reason to hope that past performance will underpredict future performance. When serving people with good work histories, sector programs might not need to be nearly so selective. And, as explained in §7.3, the average sector program has an impact per complier (the LATE) twice its impact per person offered a spot in an experiment (the ITT). If demand for training surges relative to supply, then more people offered spots may take them, raising the average impact relative to the ITTs achieved in experiments." (PDF p.83.)

52. **Why years-long reskilling breaks the model.** "Deep reskilling of humans takes years. Humlum, Munch, and Plato (2025) tracks Danish workers who suffer a serious injury on the job… A subset of injured workers who happened to have this option took it, and ultimately earned 25% more than before. They did so, however, after a typical four years of college." And: "if the demand for skills starts shifting rapidly and unpredictably—if it is hard to know what the growth occupations will be in four years—then it will be challenging for any training intermediary to align a yearslong training pipeline to the needs of employers." (PDF pp.83–84.)

53. **The TAA design flaw for the AI case.** "Of course, the TAA's trigger-based approach only works for people who can make the case that they lost their jobs to a disruptive force such as AI. When the labor market transforms not through firings, but by firms not hiring new people in certain roles when old people leave or when the firms grow, trigger-based aid will not fire." (PDF p.84.)

54. **The bottom line.** "We find that, on average, the programs have clearly positive but small impacts. Our current suite of training programs may be a defensible government expenditure, in that the investments ultimately cover their initial cost over a recipient's working life. But the impacts are small enough that they would not leave a dent in a persistently high unemployment rate. We see promise in the cluster of sector programs, although these have not been tested at scale." (PDF p.85.) And the executive-summary version: "But it seems doubtful that we currently have programs capable of meeting the moment." (PDF p.3.)

55. **The recommendation.** "A 'fire drill' evaluation meant to rapidly scale and test leading job retraining programs could pay lasting dividends." (PDF p.3.) Expanded: "funders should invest dramatically more in learning which components of the model are essential, and how best to replicate the model in more locations for more kinds of workers. One potential option is a 'fire drill' that attempts to rapidly scale one of these programs for a targeted population." (PDF p.84.) And the no-regret framing: "Some of these investigations are evergreen: yielding helpful evidence regardless of AI's impact on unemployment. Whether or not AI disrupts the labor market, we will not regret having learned how to best help workers adapt." (PDF p.85.)

## Definitions (verbatim)

All quotations are the report's own words.

**What the project is and is not (PDF p.5):**
> "This project is not purely a systematic review, nor more specifically a meta-analysis. It was not structured from the start to mechanically filter and synthesize findings—though we do ultimately conduct a meta-analysis. Our primary approach is to make intensive use of our judgment to think critically about the findings and claims in individual studies and the portrait they paint."

**The evidentiary standard (PDF p.5):**
> "Impose a high evidentiary standard. We favor randomized trials, along with studies that make prima facie plausible claims to frame compelling natural experiments. Designs in the latter category include regression discontinuity and 'judge randomization.'"

**"Aggressive" searching (footnote 4, PDF p.5):**
> "'Aggressive' searching included scanning reports that were not previously online, at the Library of Congress and the Department of Labor library; contacting staff at organizations that produced certain reports, in search of copies; tracking down authors now in retirement; scouring the 2005 Digest of Social Experiments; and following up on studies listed in earlier reviews as in-progress."

**The scope of "training" — the nine techniques (the list is on PDF p.11; the two scope sentences that follow it are on PDF p.12):**
> "Career Navigation 1. Maintenance and sharing of job listings. 2. Assistance with the mechanics of searching for a job. Direct workforce training 3. Teaching 'soft skills,' such as how to dress for and comport oneself in a job interview and in the workplace. 4. Classroom teaching in academic fundamentals (adult basic education). 5. Teaching vocational skills. Work-based learning 6. On-the-job training. 7. Apprenticeships. 8. Subsidies for private-sector employment. 9. Direct public employment of people who need work."

> "We will generally limit our review of job training programs to items 3–6."

> "In general, we are interested in programs to help people move to new occupations, usually by investing in their skills in some way."

The list is the report's own taxonomy of "techniques" that "Governments and private organizations have employed… to help people get jobs, and get better jobs" (PDF p.11); it is introduced as §2.1, not carried over from any statute.

**Training-primary vs training-secondary (PDF p.12):**
> "Most workforce programs mix approaches—e.g., perhaps job search assistance coupled with a payment for training. In the meta-analysis in §6.5, we will distinguish roughly between 'training-primary' programs, in which training is a primary feature according to Claude's reading of reports, and training-secondary ones."

Operational form (PDF p.56): "We erred toward inclusion, while having Claude assess whether job training was a primary feature in each program. Roughly half the experiments cleared this hurdle: 33, with 78 impact estimates. We meta-analyze the full set of studies as well as this 'training-primary' subset."

**Intention-to-treat (PDF p.13):**
> "The ITT is the simple difference in outcomes between those who did and did not receive the offer, regardless of participation. So all else equal, when the impact on participation—25pp in the above example—is smaller, so is the ITT. In a market saturated with effective training programs, the ITT may be small just because the control group sought other similar programs."

**Local average treatment effect (PDF p.13):**
> "The LATE measures the impact on people whose participation was determined by the offer, calculated by dividing the ITT by the difference in participation. So if our experiment increased participation in training by 25pp, and its ITT was a $500 increase in income, the LATE is ($500/25pp =) $2,000."

**LATE-program and LATE-training (PDF p.59):**
> "In training-primary interventions, the experimental offer of entry is accepted 75% of the time, while only 9% of the control group manages to access the treatment. Dividing that difference, 66%, into the ITTs quoted just above produces what we call 'LATE-program' estimates 50% larger. Similarly, the impact on participation in any training program is only 28%, because many control group members find alternative trainings. If these substitutes are equally effective, then the impacts of training per se—the values of 'LATE-training'—are 1/0.28 ≈ 4 times the ITTs reported in the lower rows of Table 2."

**The three timeframes (PDF p.59):**
> "Following CKW, the timeframes are short-term (year 1 after randomization), medium-term (year 2), or long-term. We implement the latter as years 3–5, subject to data availability, because most studies stop follow-up before year 6, and many well before that."

Table 2's note states it more tersely (PDF p.58): "Short-term outcomes have a ~1-year follow-up, medium-term ~2 years, and long-term 3–5 years."

**The estimator (PDF p.59):**
> "Since some studies produce estimates for multiple populations, which may not be fully independent, we cluster standard errors by study. We use the dominant random-effects estimator, restricted maximum likelihood (REML). The results are therefore geared to represent the impact of the typical study, not the average impact on participants across all studies."

**Random effects vs common effect (Appendix E, PDF p.116):**
> "It is often more realistic to assume that different studies measure somewhat different things—in our case, the impact of different kinds of training on different kinds of people in different contexts. The random-effects approach accepts this complexity but tames it (usually) by assuming that across various studies the average impact is distributed according to a normal or 'bell' curve. The center of this curve is what we will call the random-effects average. In practice, random-effects meta-analysis up-weights small studies."

**Publication bias (Appendix E, PDF p.116):**
> "Publication bias arises when the studies producing the biggest impact estimates (or in some cases, the smallest) are more likely to be published and thus to enter a meta-analysis."

> "Publication bias is especially pernicious in random-effects meta-analysis. For the bias surfaces mostly among the small polls, to which random-effects methods give disproportionate weight."

**Cross-group comparisons — the caveat that governs every heterogeneity claim in the report (Appendix E, PDF p.116):**
> "In discussing meta-analyses, we will make cross-group comparisons. Do privately run programs work better than public ones? Do they work better in the Midwest? Even when the individual studies are compelling as to causation—thanks, say, to randomization—comparisons across them are not. If by chance effective approaches were evaluated in New York and not in New Jersey, that does not prove that training works better in New York."

**Sector programme — the report's three quoted sources and its own working definition (footnote 8, PDF p.8):**
> "It is difficult to offer a pithy definition. As Conway and Giloth (2014) observe, 'the range of tactics and the variety of institutions that may be involved in an initiative make drawing a tight boundary around the field of sectoral employment development challenging.' A helpful characterization comes from Katz et al. (2022): 'Sectoral employment programs train job seekers for "high-quality" employment in specific industries and occupational clusters that are believed to have strong current local labor demand and opportunities for longer-term career advancement… The programs attempt to forge strong employer relationships, do some upfront screening of applicants, combine soft skills (or work-readiness) training with occupational skills training, are involved in job development and placement, provide wraparound support services to help participants complete the program, and often include follow-up services to participants after program completion and to employers after job placement.' Hendra et al. (2016) are more specific about employer involvement: 'To qualify as a sector program, an initiative must bring together multiple employers in a given field to collaborate on developing a qualified workforce.' In our meta-analysis, we use employer involvement with the training curricula as a key distinguishing factor."

**The operational definition, chosen from Peck et al.'s trait coding (PDF p.72):**
> "When the table is scanned by rows rather than columns, two nearly synonymous traits stand out as universal: 'curricula adapted for employers' needs' and employers having 'input on curriculum or program development.' We therefore provisionally equate 'sector program' with employer involvement in curricula. That resonates with the core idea of forging a strong link between pedagogy and workplace needs."

**Intention versus execution — why the sector sample is built on stated plans (PDF p.74):**
> "One can argue that the low-fidelity sites should be deleted from the sectoral strategy evidence record. Loosely speaking, they don't count because they didn't do it, or didn't do it right. This makes sense when the goal is to learn from success. But it is sometimes more meaningful to generalize from what was planned than what was done. For a decision-maker pondering whether to greenlight a project to bring sector-based training to more people, the decision is not exactly whether to do that but whether to back people who plan to do it. The proper reference class for predicting impacts is then the set of past projects with similar stated plans."

**Career pathways, the rival paradigm (PDF p.52):**
> "Career pathways programs are about supporting participants through steps of graduated difficulty, such as schooling, on-the-job training, and work experience (Fein 2012; Sarna and Strawn 2018, Exhibit 2.1)."

**Judge randomization (PDF p.37):**
> "It is called 'judge randomization' (Martin, Annan, and Forst 1993; Kling 2006) even when no judges are involved. In our context, the idea (as an example) is that people participating in an unemployment program are arbitrarily assigned to a caseworker within a given employment office; and if some caseworkers are more apt to assign clients to on-the-job training, that constitutes a natural experiment in on-the-job training. Here, the propensity of a caseworker to assign a particular treatment is the instrument. It replaces the coin toss in a randomized experiment."

**Ashenfelter's dip (PDF p.12 and Appendix A.2, PDF p.104):**
> "Often, researchers compare people's incomes before vs. after the training. This is fraught since people might sign up specifically because their earnings have taken a hit; most of them will see their income recover regardless of training. (This confound is called Ashenfelter's dip…)"

> "The study reveals a peculiar pattern, now called the 'Ashenfelter dip.' People who entered MDTA training in 1964 had a drop in earnings beforehand, in 1962–63 (Ashenfelter 1978, Table 1). In the same pre-treatment years, the non-participants' earnings rose."

**Attrition bias (PDF p.14):**
> "One econometric bugaboo not banished by randomization is the possibility of selective attrition—i.e., disappearance of subjects in a way that throws off the comparison between treatment and control groups… Thus, attrition bias is a known unknown. We don't know how things went for those who exited the study early, so we don't know how their presence would have affected the results."

**Observer and demonstration effects (Appendix A.3, PDF p.105):**
> "Experimentation changes that which is being experimented upon. For example, 'Hawthorne effects' can occur. Trainers may train somewhat differently because they know their performance is being measured. Then there are 'John Henry' effects: members of the control group may strive harder to compensate for their perceived disadvantage."

**Displacement / general-equilibrium effects (Appendix A.4, PDF p.106):**
> "One unhappy externality effect is displacement or general equilibrium effects (Heckman et al. 1999; Crépon & Van Den Berg 2016). If someone in an experiment's treatment group is trained in welding and gets a great factory job, isn't she just taking that slot from someone else? The someone else would probably not be part of a study, so her fate would be missed when tallying the program's impacts."

**The five benefit–cost summary statistics (PDF p.60):**
> "A narrow benefit-cost (B-C) ratio. The numerator is the net present value of lifetime impacts on total compensation (not just pre-tax earnings). The denominator is upfront training costs."

> "A broader B-C ratio, which nets out from the denominator the reduction in spending on other training."

> "The marginal value of public funds (MVPF), which is the ratio of the net benefit to the participant to the net cost for governments. Here, government is assumed to fund the training."

> "The ratio of net cost to gross cost for government, which indicates how much training pays for itself through higher tax revenue and lower spending on public benefits (again, assuming government pays for it)."

> "The internal rate of return (IRR) from the societal point of view, which is the discount rate at which costs and benefits balance."

**The ITT basis of every dollar figure (PDF p.61):**
> "All dollar figures are expressed per treatment group member, not per trainee. In other words, they are on an ITT rather than LATE basis. That distinction, however, does not affect the five summary statistics."

**How lifetime benefits are projected (Appendix G, PDF pp.118–119):**
> "A key ingredient for understanding benefits is: how long do the earnings gains last? Only three experiments in our meta-analysis have produced initially positive earnings impacts and then been followed beyond 6 years, which makes the evidence on long-term persistence quite thin. We estimate lifetime impacts by drawing on research on the persistence of income shocks."

> "we assume participants start training at age 27, the average in the sample, and work till age 65. The year 3–5 earnings impact, expressed as a fraction of control group earnings, constitutes the shock that propagates forward."

> "We discount future impacts with the US government's real long-term cost of funds, as proxied by the market yield on a 30-year TIPS bond, currently 2.82%."

**Fringe benefits and the representative occupation (Appendix G, PDF p.119):**
> "According to the Employer Costs for Employee Compensation (ECEC) data set of the Bureau of Labor Statistics, pre-tax wages accounted for 72.4% of total compensation in 2025, in the occupational group 'office and administrative support occupations.' We take this entry-level occupational category as representative of the jobs trainees graduate into."

**The other benefit–cost parameters, each a borrowed constant (Appendix G, PDF pp.120–121):** employee payroll tax "at the standard 7.65% of pre-tax earnings"; employer-paid taxes "at 6.85% of total compensation"; income tax "We use the average of the four, 17.2%"; sales tax "the national average sales tax as of January 1, 2026, was 5.69%"; benefits offset "the average ratio of benefit reduction to earnings increase is 14.8%"; work-related spending "at 8.34%"; unpaid time "at fully 50% of the earnings impact of job training", modified because "this cost depends in principle on the impacts on employment, not earnings."

**What is deliberately excluded from the ledger (Appendix G, PDF p.121):**
> "Unlike Schaberg and Greenberg, we do not factor in the deadweight loss from raising government revenue to pay for training. As Hendren and Sprung-Keyser (2020) point out, the use of any particular deadweight loss multiplier implies a theory about how revenue is raised. We sidestep this question, preferring to separate the analysis of job training from the question of how it is paid for."

**Signaling vs human capital (Appendix A.6, PDF p.108):**
> "If job training programs also help people mainly by certifying their smarts, grit, and conformity, that sharpens the worry that the gains are zero-sum: certification just gets people to the front of the line in the competition for a fixed supply of jobs. In this view, money spent on training is entirely wasted via knock-on effects."

## Data and methods

In the wiki author's words, with page references.

**The shape of the project (PDF p.5).** Five declared moves: a high evidentiary standard favouring randomised trials plus regression-discontinuity and judge-randomization designs; an aggressive archival search; a new meta-analysis of the 146 US estimates; individual narrative reviews of the most important national studies; and deeper engagement with sector programmes. The report is explicit that it is a hybrid — a judgment-led evidence review that contains a meta-analysis rather than a pre-specified systematic review (PDF p.5).

**Where the sample came from (PDF p.55).** The search frame is six prior reviews — "Greenberg and Shroder (2004); Smedslund et al. (2006); Dutta-Gupta et al. (2016); Card, Kluve, and Weber (2018); Peck et al. (2021); and Katz et al. (2022)" — plus "recent follow-ups on the PACE and WorkAdvance evaluations". The resulting sample is 56 studies and 146 impact estimates, of which 24 studies report results for more than one demographic, organisation or locality (PDF p.55); 33 studies with 78 estimates are training-primary (PDF p.56). Coverage against the prior reviews is stated in claim 4.

**Claude as the extraction instrument — the methodological novelty (PDF pp.5, 55).** "Since most of the experiments are documented by long reports submitted to government agencies, Claude is used to extract traits and impacts" (PDF p.5); "We deploy Claude Opus—with engaged oversight—to extract the needed information on each experiment from the thousands of pages of reports" (PDF p.55). The controls: "we instruct Claude to: a) produce a 'reasoning table' documenting sources and logic for extracted values and b) perform an adversarial review of all extractions, by reading the reasoning table and returning to sources. As in analyses done without the aid of AI, as we learn our way around the data and analyze it, we spot-check and revise the extraction." (PDF p.55.) Note for this programme's purposes: **Claude appears here only as a research tool; no Claude usage data enters the analysis.**

**Imputing significance where primary sources report only stars (PDF p.55).** "for binary outcomes, we use that formula anyway, while for the continuous outcome of earnings, we follow Greenberg, Michalopoulos, and Robins (2006, note 9) in imputing p = 0.005, 0.03, 0.075, and 0.55 for p < 0.01, p < 0.05, p < 0.1, and p > 0.1. These uncertain imputations influence the meta-analytic, random-effects results." (Footnote 38, PDF p.50, records that Smedslund et al. imputed "p = 0.005, 0.03, 0.075, and 0.1" — a different fourth value — and that this report does "nearly the same".)

**Estimation (PDF pp.58–59).** REML random-effects meta-analysis of ITTs, standard errors clustered by study. Four outcomes — programme take-up, any-training take-up, employment, earnings — with the last two split across three horizons, estimated separately for the full sample and the training-primary subset; control-group means computed with the same REML weights; sample sizes vary by cell because of missing data (Table 2, PDF p.58). Earnings are in 2025 dollars throughout. The horizons are the report's own implementation of Card, Kluve and Weber's: short-term is year 1, medium-term year 2, long-term years 3–5 "subject to data availability, because most studies stop follow-up before year 6" (PDF p.59).

**Correlates (PDF pp.61–64).** Traits are grouped into blocks; each block is regressed alone on the outcome; the survivors are then run together. The procedure is copied from Peck et al. (2021) — "we copy the block-based method in the Peck et al. (2021) meta-analysis (§6.4)" (PDF p.61) — and the selection rule is stated only where Peck et al.'s method is described, at PDF pp.52–53: "Only the 'survivors' are retained: those statistically significant at p < 0.1 and having a standardized coefficient exceeding 0.1 in magnitude." Missing values are handled by multiple imputation with ten imputed data sets per outcome and timeframe, reported alongside complete-case columns. Appendix F repeats the same regressions with the previous horizon's impact added as a persistence control.

**The sector sample (PDF pp.71–74).** Built from Peck et al.'s public-use trait coding: every randomised evaluation Peck et al. rates as having either "curricula adapted for employers' needs" or employer "input on curriculum or program development"; three are dropped "for lack of strong textual evidence that the programs were designed to substantially involve employers in curriculum development, or for lack of medium- or long-term impact estimates" (footnote 54, PDF p.72, names them: "Green Jobs and Health Care in Minneapolis, IBEST, and YouthBuild… and Career Pathways in Louisiana"); CET and its replications are added because Peck et al. excludes them for not being career-pathways programmes. The result is "a good case for adding 10 randomized evaluations of sector programs to the 9 (of 8 programs) in Katz et al." (PDF p.74). Table 5 (PDF p.73) records, per programme, the textual evidence of employer involvement, the last follow-up, and whether outcomes came from surveys or official records.

**Benefit–cost (PDF pp.60–61, Appendix G pp.118–124).** The framework follows Schaberg and Greenberg (2020)'s WorkAdvance analysis, with three perspectives (treatment group member, government, society) and ten line items, "except in the estimation of lifetime impacts on pre-tax earnings" (PDF p.118), which is this report's own construction: the years 3–5 proportional earnings impact is treated as an income shock and propagated to age 65 using Karahan and Ozkan (2013)'s age-varying serial correlation (0.7 at 24, rising to 0.98 at 45), against an ACS-based lifecycle earnings profile rescaled to match the meta-analytic control mean at age 31, discounted at 2.82%. Crime and health benefits are deliberately excluded (footnote 46, PDF p.60; Appendix G, PDF p.124).

**Outcome-data provenance (PDF pp.14–15).** The report repeatedly weighs survey-based against administrative outcomes and prefers the latter: "linking to government data substantially dispels the worry about attrition bias (Ashenfelter 1978), but many studies—including half of the sector program evaluations—are limited to surveys." Table 1 records that 70% of all estimates and 59% of training-primary estimates use official records (PDF p.57); Table 5 records the source programme by programme.

**Released files and data availability.** The report names no Anthropic-hosted data file and no Hugging Face dataset. Its code and data are a third-party GitHub repository and a GitHub Pages web interface (PDF pp.1, 6, 64–65). **Nothing in this report is an Economic Index release**, and the data steward's atlas has nothing to confirm about it; any brief that builds on this paper builds on its *claims, method or open questions*, not on a released Anthropic file, and must layer its own data — which under the working criteria means an Economic Index release supplies the foundation and this paper supplies the thread.

## Limitations (verbatim)

**On the fit between this evidence base and an AI shock (PDF p.4):**
> "The body of evidence and experience is of course an imperfect match for an AI-disrupted future. If layoffs mainly affect white-collar workers, the new raft of long-term unemployed would be the most educated and highly paid in history. These workers on average have more liquid savings and know how to navigate the job market (Manning and Aguirre 2026). If they do return to school, it could take them years to reskill. The evidence gathered here is about training programs that last weeks or months and mostly target low-income people on the margins of the job market."

**On the extrapolation itself (PDF p.3):**
> "In applying these lessons to AI, we are, of course, forced to extrapolate into a highly uncertain world. We don't know if or when technological advances will cause a surge in unemployment; it's unclear which jobs would be most affected, and the unemployed population may look different from the subjects in these studies (mostly low-income or persistently jobless individuals)."

**On the absence of direct evidence (PDF p.80):**
> "We have no research evidence on how well job training helps people adjust when large language models start doing their jobs."

**The report's own named key limitation — scope (PDF p.80):**
> "A key limitation of this report, however, is that it only covers stand-alone job training programs. Those are only one channel through which people gain job skills. Beyond our scope are high school vocational programs, apprenticeships, community colleges, four-year colleges, professional schools, and online education."

**On its own evidentiary standard (footnote 3, PDF p.5):**
> "We do not claim to have perfectly applied our 'high evidentiary standard.' Notably, we do not cover any matching studies even though some are arguably as credible as research we include."

**On extraction error, quoting the predecessor it inherits the risk from (PDF p.55):**
> "The humble warning in Card, Kluve, and Weber (2010)—'There are likely to be measurement errors and errors of interpretation in the extraction of information from the studies.'—applies as well to this AI-accelerated effort. The collection and collation of studies involves many judgment calls—some here made by humans and some by AI. Does an intervention involve training enough that it belongs in the sample? Is training primary in the intervention? If an impact is reported for months 7–18 since randomization, is that a short-term (year-1) or medium-term (year-2) impact?"

**On imputed significance (PDF p.55):**
> "Another limitation is typical in this literature: many non-academic impact studies do not report continuous metrics of statistical significance, such as standard errors… These uncertain imputations influence the meta-analytic, random-effects results."

**On the difficulty of the inclusion boundary (PDF p.56):**
> "Because workforce programs are diverse and multifaceted, job training features more prominently in some than others, and drawing the line between training and non-training interventions is difficult."

**On cross-group comparisons in its own results (PDF p.61):**
> "That said, the caveat about cross-group comparisons bears repeating: no matter how rigorous the individual studies, meta-analytical comparisons among them are not as dispositive as to which factors cause training to be more effective as distinct from merely being correlated with them."

**On the thinness behind particular coefficients (PDF p.63):**
> "Other statistically strong results are associated with small numbers of programs."

**On the sector-programme population (PDF p.76):**
> "Sector programs draw from populations that are higher paid even without training."

**On whether the definitional trait is the right one (PDF p.74):**
> "It is of course arguable whether the trait we have singled out as definitional—employer engagement in curricula—truly is."

**On post-hoc theorising about why programmes work (PDF p.69):**
> "It is appropriate to theorize post hoc about why certain programs work. But it is also tricky. The sample of studies is small. It is tempting to explain away failures as not being true sector programs. Comparisons across trials are subject to the caveat that correlation is not causation."

**On which sector-programme ingredient matters (PDF p.79):**
> "It is unclear—and a question for further research—exactly how important each of these components is."

**On non-random placement of the evidence itself (Appendix A.5, PDF p.107):**
> "The programs whose randomized evaluations reach our eyes do so only after passing through filters of non-random selection and self-selection (Allcott 2015). As a result, randomized evaluations as a group are not strictly representative of job training programs as a whole. That's not a criticism, just a caution when generalizing."

> "Governments and foundations may be more ready to pay for long-term follow-ups of programs that have performed well in the shorter-term, which would bias the long-term evidence before us."

Repeated where it bites hardest (footnote 44, PDF p.59): "However, there may be a tendency to follow successful programs longer."

**On selection into randomised evaluation of sector programmes (PDF p.69):**
> "The placement of randomized studies is indeed non-random. Several programs got evaluated more than once."

**On the benefit–cost projection, the report's own three caveats (Appendix G, PDF pp.123–124):**
> "These benefit-cost calculations are subject to several limitations. First, we assume that the income gains from these programs decay slowly, following the literature on income shocks (Karahan and Ozkan 2013). This matters less for sector programs, since they pay for themselves even looking only at the years where subjects are tracked. But for the training-only programs, the bulk of the benefit accrues in the extrapolation beyond the studies' follow-up windows. Without that persistence, they would have a benefit-cost ratio under 1."

> "Second, jobs have benefits outside of wages. They might decrease crime or improve mental health. Including these would increase the benefits side of the ledger, but we opt not to because the research is so uncertain on this. Finally, we assume away general equilibrium or displacement effects. If trainees displace other workers seeking the same jobs, the benefits we measure are inflated."

**On the same risk, in the executive summary (PDF p.2):**
> "The programs pass a cost-benefit test, but this relies on uncertain assumptions about how long the benefits last."

**On displacement effects, the evidence that cuts against the whole ledger (Appendix A.4, PDF p.106):**
> "Micro evidence on the program, covering only the participants, would have been encouraging. But because of these displacement effects, the authors conclude, 'the program seems to have had very little net benefits.' This result suggests that studies overestimate the benefit of job search assistance, because they miss the side effects on those outside the study."

And the report's own defence, which is a conjecture rather than a result: "We think displacement effects are probably lower for training programs that increase people's skills, especially in the long term." (PDF p.106.)

**On the ITT/LATE choice (PDF p.14):**
> "A final complication of the LATE is that sometimes study subjects participate in a separate training program, so that there are different ways to define it: the impact of the targeted training or the impact of any training at all (Kline and Walters 2016)."

**On the limits of the Hyman study (PDF p.37):**
> "The study's largest limitation from our point of view is that the extension of unemployment insurance was contingent upon being in training, making it impossible to statistically distinguish the effects of training and income support."

**On opacity in both judge-randomization studies (PDF pp.41, 47):**
> "Reasons to doubt include that this is a large, complicated, opaque, non-experimental study. Complexity brings more opportunity for bugs and increases the degrees of freedom in defining the sample and running the numbers, which widens the space for p-hacking, conscious or unconscious."

> "Overall, the study is tricky to evaluate. The methodology looks sound. And the claim to quasi-experimental identification of impacts is stronger than in Hyman (2018), because of the birthday rule. But like Hyman (2018), the study is also intrinsically opaque and complex."

**On the European evidence (PDF p.80):**
> "The handful of randomized and discontinuity-based studies from Europe have not been very informative as to the impacts of training (see Appendices C and D)."

**On sector programmes' ceiling (PDF p.79):**
> "While sector programs surely have not hit their limits, just as surely those limits exist. One reason Year Up has expanded from Boston to other cities, rather than scaling up where it started, is that its approach depends on deep-pocketed corporate partners, who fund much of the training and commit to hire the graduates. In each city, the supply of such partners is limited. Indeed, Year Up runs for a year—half training, half internship—which is long by the standards of sector programs. So as far as the evidence goes, effective sector programs are confined to skill sets that can be taught in months."

**On scaling (PDF p.16):**
> "Empirically, researchers have found that scale-ups (Allcott 2015; Bold 2018) and government-run programs (Vivalt 2020) tend to do worse."

> "This is one reason that scaling a program to serve more people can completely change how it functions."

**On what a crash scale-up would do (PDF pp.10, 3):**
> "The challenge of replicating sector programs would surely be compounded in any effort to scale them up on a crash basis, whether led by public or private funders."

> "Attempts to set up sector programs often fail. It can take years to cultivate relationships with local employers and develop competence in tracking regional trends in the demand for skills. A speedy expansion could copy the form and lose the essence."

**On the untested status of the recommendation (PDF p.85):**
> "We see promise in the cluster of sector programs, although these have not been tested at scale."

**On what the average programme could not do in an AI shock (PDF p.85):**

## What it did not test

The wiki author's inference, not the report's claims. Each item is something the report's own text leaves open, asserts without testing, or places out of frame. Several of these are the openings a post could take.

1. **Anything about AI's actual effect on displaced workers.** The report says so itself (PDF p.80). Everything here is historical evidence about programmes for low-income and dislocated workers, 1973 onward; the AI content is §8's extrapolation, built from three cited papers (Manning and Aguirre 2026, Massenkoff and McCrory 2026, Karger et al. 2026) and no new estimation.
2. **Whether the trained occupations are the exposed occupations.** The programmes trained people as "nursing aides, IT support technicians, and welders" (PDF p.2) and for "IT support and computer repair, software development, medical assisting, nursing, medical billing, construction, building maintenance, manufacturing, accounting, and bookkeeping" (PDF p.9). Several of those destinations are precisely the occupations Anthropic's own exposure work ranks as most exposed. The report never maps its training destinations onto any exposure measure — not Massenkoff and McCrory's observed exposure, not Manning and Aguirre's index, not O*NET. Whether the sector-programme pipeline points at occupations AI is itself covering is unasked.
3. **Whether Claude's extraction is accurate, and by how much.** The report's quality controls are a reasoning table, an adversarial re-read and human spot-checks (PDF p.55). No error rate, no human-versus-Claude agreement statistic on a held-out subsample, no sensitivity of any headline number to extraction error is reported. For a paper whose novelty is partly that AI extraction let it cover more US randomised studies than any predecessor, the extraction's accuracy is the one thing not measured.
4. **Publication bias in its own meta-analysis.** The report reviews funnel plots and bias adjustments in three of the four predecessor meta-analyses, and Appendix E explains why "Publication bias is especially pernicious in random-effects meta-analysis" (PDF p.116) — which is the estimator it uses. It then argues from institutional features ("most studies reviewed here were funded by government agencies or foundations", PDF p.15) that its own literature is "relatively undistorted", and reports no funnel plot, Egger test or trim-and-fill for its own 146 estimates. Its own Appendix A.5 gives the reason this argument is not sufficient: long-term follow-ups are commissioned selectively.
5. **Multiple testing in its own meta-regressions.** The report criticises CKW because "Many hypotheses are tested, without any adjustment for the fact that a certain number of findings will be statistically significant by chance" (PDF p.51), and praises Schochet's care about multiple hypothesis testing (PDF p.32). Its own block-by-block "survivor" procedure selects regressors at p < 0.1 (with a standardised coefficient above 0.1) across the trait blocks catalogued in Table 1 (PDF p.57), and then reports conventional stars on the survivors, with no correction, no pre-registration and no reported false-discovery rate.
6. **Minimum detectable effects beside its nulls.** Several results are null or nearly so — the long-term employment impact for sector programmes (Table 6, PDF p.76: a standard error of 0.8 on a 1.3-point estimate, N = 6), "Classroom training showed no clear advantage" (PDF p.63), the WIA zeros. The WIA's power is discussed qualitatively (PDF p.35) but no MDE is computed anywhere, for the new meta-analysis or for any cell of it.
7. **Whether the sector-programme premium survives its control-group difference.** The report reports that sector control groups have 80% long-term employment against 60%, and earn 50–75% more (PDF p.76) — and that sector programmes screen out more than 80% of applicants. It does not reweight, match on control-group baseline, or condition the sector/non-sector contrast on the served population. The headline "10 times as much" is a difference between two random-effects means over two differently selected sets of studies.
8. **Which of the four named sector ingredients does the work.** Selectivity, soft-plus-vocational skills, coaching and graduation into work are named (PDF pp.78–79) and the report says their relative importance is unknown. Its own regressions offer a partial answer it does not pursue — the employer hiring commitment outcompetes the sector-programme trait (PDF p.72) — but that coefficient rests on three programmes (PDF p.63) and is not decomposed further.
9. **The "10 times" and "3–5 times" multiples are never reconciled.** Both appear as headline characterisations of the same phenomenon (PDF p.2 vs PDF p.77), on different samples; no test of the difference between the two sector-programme means and the training-primary means is reported, and no confidence interval is attached to the ratio.
10. **Sensitivity of the benefit–cost verdict.** Appendix G concedes that without the persistence assumption "they would have a benefit-cost ratio under 1" (PDF p.123) — a sign flip on the report's central policy claim — but reports no sensitivity table over the persistence parameter, the discount rate (only the IRR), the 50% unpaid-time charge, or the 72.4% wage share. The only robustness evidence offered is the 28-estimate ratio in the same paragraph.
11. **The imputed p-values are never varied.** Imputing p = 0.005/0.03/0.075/0.55 from significance stars drives the REML weights, and the report says so (PDF p.55). No alternative imputation is run, even though footnote 38 (PDF p.50) records that Smedslund et al. used a different fourth value.
12. **Displacement and general-equilibrium effects.** Assumed away in the ledger (PDF p.124), argued to be smaller for skills-building training (PDF p.106) — argued, not estimated. The Crépon et al. (2013) result the report cites is the one piece of evidence in the review that could overturn the societal benefit calculation, and it is discussed only in an appendix.
13. **Whether the intention-based sector sample changes the answer.** The report makes a careful case for generalising from stated plans rather than executed fidelity (PDF p.74) and builds Table 5 accordingly, but reports no split of its sector results by the fidelity column it assembled (Strong / Moderate / Mixed / Weak evidence of employer involvement), nor by survey versus official outcome source, though both columns exist.
14. **Non-US meta-analysis.** The new meta-analysis is US-only (PDF p.55). European evidence is narrative (Appendix C, PDF pp.109–114; Appendix D, PDF pp.114–115) and is not pooled, so the claim that European studies "have not been very informative" (PDF p.80) is not quantified; nor is the Danish judge-randomization result, the largest in the review, reconciled with the US means.
15. **Whether ITT or LATE is the right object for an AI shock.** The report frames this as the key modelling choice for its own extrapolation (PDF p.14), argues both sides, and then defaults to ITT. No scenario arithmetic is offered showing what the policy conclusion would be under the LATE.
16. **Anything about training delivered or assisted by AI.** The intervention classes are the report's own nine-technique taxonomy (PDF p.11), of which items 3–6 are in scope (PDF p.12); none of the nine is AI-delivered or AI-assisted instruction. Online education is explicitly out of scope (PDF p.80). The possibility that the cost, speed or scalability of retraining is itself altered by the technology causing the displacement — which would break the historical cost base of $13,598 — is not raised.
17. **Any Economic Index data, despite the cover line.** No release, no usage measure, no occupational cut. This is the corpus's clearest example of an Anthropic economics publication whose evidentiary base is entirely external.
18. **The report's own internal disagreements.** "Twice over" versus "thrice over" for the sector programmes' fiscal payback (PDF pp.77, 121); 15 versus 16 percentage points for the WIA take-up differential (pp.7, 35); Hendra et al. 2016 p.34 versus p.29 for the same selectivity fact (pp.9, 78); "11 of the 12 mean impact estimates" against the sixteen printed in Table 2 (pp.58–59); and Table 1's row label "Cost per treated" against its own note that costs are per treatment group member (p.57) — the denominator the arXiv abstract then gets wrong. None is flagged in the document.
