## Data and methods

What the page states, in the wiki author's words, with references. Four methodological elements are deferred to the appendix and are listed as pointers only (see *Source*).

- **Instrument.** A survey, described in the Discussion as "an email survey of quantitative social scientists". Questions quoted on the page: the genAI-use question, the command-line coding-assistant question plus a tool-verification follow-up, a multi-select research use-case question, a self-reported output battery, and two 10-point slider belief questions. No full questionnaire is published on the page, and no response-option list for the use-case question beyond the categories named in the text (generate code, edit prose, methods advice, background on prior research, draft prose).
- **Population and frame.** "active quantitative social scientists" (*"A new survey…"*). The page does not state the invitation count, response rate, the frame from which names were drawn, or any country, region, language or institution-country breakdown.
- **Field window.** "late February and March 2026" in the body; "February and March 2026" in the Summary; the Discussion dates the state of play "as of March 2026". Survey week is used as a control variable, so week of completion was recorded.
- **Sample size.** 1,260 respondents. No disaggregated cell sizes are published: the adopter group is 20% of the sample (≈250 by arithmetic, a figure the page does not state), and no n is given for any discipline, career stage, gender or university group.
- **Recruitment and its acknowledged selection.** Respondents "were recruited for a study that offered access to Claude Max accounts", and the page states twice that this tilts the sample (*"A new survey…"*; Discussion). The only selection check is a comparison against "an earlier sample that received a more generic invitation", reported in appendix Table A2, not here.
- **Linkage to usage data.** **None is described anywhere on the page.** Adoption, tool choice and all output measures are self-reported (Fig. 5: "Outcomes are self-reported by respondents"). The page does not say whether Claude Code usage logs, the Claude Max accounts offered at recruitment, or any Economic Index data were linked to survey responses, nor whether such a linkage was considered or ruled out.
- **Derived variables.** Gender from name classification (no classifier named, no accuracy figure, no ambiguous-name or non-binary category mentioned). University status from the Nature Index Leading Institutions top 25, plus a private/public split. Discipline categories defined in the appendix. "Number of AI use cases adopted" is a count over the Figure 4 categories.
- **Estimators.** Adoption differences: group differences tested at p<0.05; no model stated. Output differences: a regression of self-reported six-month counts on coding-agent use, "controlling for career stage, discipline, and the week they completed the survey", with the model itself described in the appendix; inference by robust standard errors, with 95% confidence intervals "scaled by the outcome mean" and stars at p<0.05 and p<0.01. Percentage effects are reported relative to the outcome mean (10% to 75%). No weighting, no non-response adjustment, no multiple-comparison correction and no power or minimum-detectable-effect calculation is mentioned.
- **Released data.** None. The page offers no data download, no replication package, no pre-registration link and no OSF/AEA registry identifier. The five references are to external literature.
- **Design of the wider study.** A randomized experiment "providing researchers with access to Claude Code" is running; results are promised but not reported. The page states no arm sizes, randomisation unit, outcomes or pre-registration for that experiment.

## What it did not test

**This section is the wiki author's inference, not the page's text.** It records adjacent questions the page's own design could have addressed but did not, and framings the page uses without support on the page. Items that the appendix may settle are marked; the appendix is `wiki/reports/coding-agents-social-sciences-2026-05-appendix.md` and is not summarised here.

*Adjacent questions the design could have addressed*

1. **No linkage to observed usage, anywhere.** Adoption, tool choice, intensity and output are all self-reported, yet respondents were recruited with an offer of Claude Max accounts by the firm that operates Claude Code and holds its usage logs. The page does not report a linkage, does not say a linkage was attempted, and does not say why not. This is the single largest missing validation, and it is the one Anthropic was uniquely placed to run.
2. **No validation of self-reported output against public records.** Working papers posted, grant submissions and journal submissions are partly observable in SSRN, NBER, SocArXiv, OpenAlex and funder registries. The paper's central quantitative result rests on recall over six months with no external check and no reliability estimate.
3. **No pre-adoption baseline.** The output window (the six months before the survey) overlaps adoption, so the selection story the authors name — "early adopters were already more productive researchers" — is left entirely unbounded. A retrospective pre-period count, or a within-person change, was available to a survey and was not asked for (or at least not reported).
4. **No MDE or power statement beside the null.** "We find no evidence that coding agent users are submitting more new papers to journals" is the paper's most consequential negative, drawn from roughly 250 adopters and low-count outcomes. Nothing on the page says what effect size the design could have ruled out, so the null cannot be distinguished from an underpowered test.
5. **No multiple-comparison discipline.** Figure 3 asserts p<0.05 for every group difference and Figure 5 stars several of six output measures; there is no correction, no family-wise statement, and no indication whether the comparisons were specified in advance.
6. **The gender gap is not decomposed.** It is said to persist within discipline and career stage and among those who have tried AI, but no conditional estimate is published, and prior programming training, co-authorship role, teaching and service load, field sub-speciality and computing access are neither measured nor discussed. The name classifier is unnamed and unvalidated on this sample: no accuracy rate, no treatment of ambiguous or non-Anglophone names (which would plausibly misclassify unevenly across the disciplines with the lowest adoption), and no non-binary or unclassified category.
7. **The two rates behind every headline ratio are withheld.** "Twice as many" (gender) and "40% more likely" (top universities) are ratios whose numerators and denominators never appear. A reader cannot check either, or compute a difference in percentage points, or judge cell size.
8. **The private/public university gap is named and never quantified** — not in the body, not in a caption, not in the Summary.
9. **Intensity is not measured.** Adoption is a weekly binary. Hours, sessions, share of analysis delegated, or number of tasks handed off are absent, so no dose-response relationship between agent use and output can be examined — the natural robustness check on a selection-driven story.
10. **Automation versus augmentation is never measured**, although the page's framing is explicitly an automation claim ("core research tasks can be handed off to machines"; "irreducibly human steps … can, for the first time, be automated") and Anthropic has a standing five-mode instrument for exactly this distinction. The use-case question (Figure 4) records *what* AI was used for but not *how much oversight* the researcher kept.
11. **Nothing about the research itself.** Methods chosen, specification count, robustness practice, replication packages, pre-registration, code volume, or whether agent-assisted papers differ in topic or design. The paper's most ambitious conjecture — that AI's "distinctive analytical choices could stamp our collective understanding" — has no measure attached. The authors flag quality as out of scope; analytical *content* is a separate omission they do not flag.
12. **Geography and language are absent.** No country, region or institution-country composition is reported, so the results cannot be joined to the Economic Index geography work even though Claude Code availability, pricing and language coverage vary by country, and the low-adoption fields here (public health, education, communication) plausibly differ in national composition too.
13. **Non-response is asserted, not bounded.** "We expect that the respondents are both heavier users and more optimistic about LLMs than non-responders" is a direction without a magnitude; invitation counts and response rates are not on the page (they may be in the appendix), and no bounding exercise, weighting or Lee-style bound is attempted.
14. **Beliefs have no reference point.** The 88%-above-5 figure is reported as a finding from a sample the authors say is self-selected on interest in AI tools. No comparison group, no external benchmark, and no anchoring or acquiescence check.
15. **The belief gap has an untested rival explanation.** 70% rating their own paper productivity above the field's welfare is consistent with a general tendency to rate self-relevant outcomes above collective ones. No control pair of questions of that shape is reported, so the "researchers fear field-level costs" reading is one of at least two.
16. **Survey-week variation is used as a control, not as evidence.** Adoption was measured across a multi-week window two months after a burst of attention to Claude Code; whether adoption rose within the fielding window — a cheap descriptive diffusion result the data supports — is not reported.
17. **Field gradients are asserted without a statistic.** "roughly tracks differences across fields in overall AI use, but differences in coding agent adoption are steeper on average" has no ratio, correlation or spread measure behind it, and adoption rates for sociology, management sciences and psychology — three of the five largest groups — are never published.
18. **Recruitment incentive as a driver of the tool mix.** 86% of adopters report Claude Code, in a sample recruited with free Claude Max accounts, by Anthropic. Whether the offer shaped adoption, tool choice, or the willingness to report either is not discussed anywhere on the page.

*Framings used without support on this page*

19. **"AI chatbots" for a question about "genAI models".** The Summary's 81% is labelled "have tried using AI chatbots in research"; the question asked was "Have you previously used genAI models to aid your research process?" — broader, and not restricted to chat interfaces. The body then contrasts "turn-taking AI chatbots" against coding agents as if the 81% measured the former.
20. **"particularly for writing code and editing prose"** (Summary) attaches the use-case findings to the 81% group, but the use-case question was asked of AI users and is reported split by adopter status, not as a property of the 81%.
21. **"coding agents" is measured as command-line tools only.** Footnote 1 says so for desktop apps, but the question stem itself lists **Cursor**, an IDE rather than a command-line tool, alongside Codex and Claude Code, and Google Antigravity is admitted only via the follow-up. The measured construct is therefore both narrower than "coding agents" (excluding desktop agents) and looser than "integrated into your command line". The page notes the first mismatch and not the second.
22. **"autonomously write and execute analysis code"** (Summary) describes the tool's capability, not anything the survey observed. No respondent was asked whether the agent executed code without review.
23. **"Adoption is highly uneven" / "suggest higher inequality"** compares gaps across two panels of Figure 3 by eye; no inequality statistic (variance ratio, dissimilarity index, or interaction test between adoption type and group) is reported.
24. **"more technologically fluent … facing stronger career pressures"** (the juniors explanation) — neither fluency nor career pressure is measured.
25. **"Researchers mainly use AI to code and edit, not write"** (section heading) versus the body's "only a third of all AI users have used it to draft prose at all": a third having drafted prose is a substantial minority, and the heading's "not write" overstates it. Editing prose is itself writing assistance.
26. **"posting more working papers and sending out more grant proposals"** in a section heading, and "Coding agent users are posting more working papers and applying for more grants" in the Discussion, both stated in the indicative, against the same section's explicit instruction that the differences "should not be interpreted as causal". The Summary and Discussion carry the caveat; the headings do not.
27. **"we show that coding agents are diffusing into the social sciences"** rests on a single cross-section of a non-representative sample. Diffusion is a change over time; the page has one wave and no earlier adoption rate to compare against (the generic-invitation sample in Table A2 is used for composition, not for a diffusion trend).
28. **"The way we study the economy and politics … is increasingly through analysis decisions made in part by AI coding agents."** "Increasingly" again requires two points in time, and "analysis decisions made in part by" requires a measure of what the agent decided. Neither is on the page.

*Relationship to the Economic Index, for the ledger*

29. This is the corpus's only publication where a **survey instrument and Anthropic's own first-party usage data could have been joined at the individual level** and were not. It is also one of the few that measures a *population* (quantitative social scientists) rather than a stream of conversations, and the only one that measures *beliefs*. Both features make it the natural counterpart to the Index's task-level measurement — and the page itself makes no connection to the Index, its taxonomy, or its released data.
