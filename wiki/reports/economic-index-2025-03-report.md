## Claims

Numbers are quoted as published. "§" refers to a section heading on the page; captions are cited by the figure they sit under.

**Sample and pipeline**

1. **The re-measurement window is 11 days and 1 million conversations.** "We reran our previous analysis on data from the 11 days following the launch, covering 1 million anonymized Claude.ai Free and Pro conversations." (§ "What's changed since the launch of Claude 3.7 Sonnet?"). Comparison: the same pipeline as the first report, run on a later window. No calendar dates for the window are given in the body; the figure caption dates the new sample "Feb '25 - March '25" and the first sample "Dec '25 - Jan '25" (the latter is a typo on the page for Dec '24 – Jan '25; see Data and methods).
2. **Model composition of the sample is asserted, not quantified.** "The vast majority of the data we analyzed was from Claude 3.7 Sonnet, as it is set as the default on Claude.ai and our mobile app." (§ "What's changed since the launch of Claude 3.7 Sonnet?"). No share is given; conversations on other models are in the sample.
3. **Each conversation is mapped to one of ~17,000 O\*NET tasks.** "our privacy-preserving analysis tool, Clio, maps each conversation to one of 17,000 tasks in the U.S. Department of Labor's O\*NET database. We then look at the overall patterns in the occupations and high-level occupational categories associated with those tasks." (§ "What's changed since the launch of Claude 3.7 Sonnet?"). Same taxonomy as V1; this is the continuity that licenses the V1→V2 comparison. The "17,000" is a round number, not a file quantity: the release's own `onet_task_statements.csv` holds 18,429 distinct task statements over 974 occupations (`data/releases/release_2025_03_27.md`).

**V1 → V2 change in the occupational mix**

4. **Coding, education, science and healthcare shares rose.** "Since the launch of Claude 3.7 Sonnet, we've observed a rise in the share of usage for coding, as well as educational, science, and healthcare applications" (opening bullets). The body states the increase as "modest": "we see that the proportion of usage in several occupational categories has increased modestly, including coding, education and the sciences" (§ "What's changed since the launch of Claude 3.7 Sonnet?"). Healthcare appears in the opening bullet but not in the body sentence and carries no number anywhere on the page. Comparison: share of Claude.ai Free and Pro traffic across top-level O\*NET occupational categories, V2 against V1.
5. **The only percentage attached to the change is +3 points for computer and mathematical occupations.** "computer and mathematical occupations represent the category with the largest absolute increase (+3%), while several categories like education and the sciences show notable percentage increases." (caption, § Appendix → "Full change across occupational categories"). Note the caption's own distinction between absolute (percentage-point) and "percentage" (relative) increases; no relative figure is given for education or the sciences.
6. **The measure is a share, not a level.** "Note that the graph shows the share of usage rather than absolute usage." (caption, § "What's changed since the launch of Claude 3.7 Sonnet?"). The V1→V2 comparison is therefore compositional by construction.
7. **Three candidate explanations are offered for the non-coding increases, and none is chosen.** "While this increase in coding usage was expected due to Claude 3.7 Sonnet's improved scores on coding benchmarks, the increase in these other categories could reflect either ongoing diffusion of AI throughout the economy, novel applications of coding to those domains, or unexpected capability improvements in the model." (§ "What's changed since the launch of Claude 3.7 Sonnet?"). Also listed under open questions.

**Extended thinking by occupation**

8. **Extended thinking is concentrated in technical and creative-technical occupations, led by computer and information research scientists at almost 10%.** "Tasks associated with computer and information research scientists lead with almost 10% using extended thinking, followed by software developers at around 8%. Tasks associated with digital creative roles like multimedia artists (~7%) and video game designers (~6%) also show substantial usage." (§ "How are people using extended thinking mode?"). Comparison: across O\*NET occupations, restricted to those above a prevalence floor — "Occupations shown are limited to those with at least 0.5% representation in the data" (caption).
9. **The characterisation of the pattern is qualitative.** "Our analysis reveals that Claude 3.7 Sonnet's extended thinking mode is predominantly used in technical and creative problem-solving contexts." (§ "How are people using extended thinking mode?"). No overall rate of extended-thinking use across the whole sample is published anywhere on the page.
10. **A task-level thinking dataset is released.** "we're releasing a new dataset that maps each O\*NET task to its associated thinking mode fraction." (§ "How are people using extended thinking mode?"). This is `task_thinking_fractions.csv`, and `has_thinking_ratio` in the cluster file.

**Automation and augmentation, V1 → V2**

11. **The augmentation/automation balance is unchanged; augmentation is 57%.** "Our analysis shows the balance of augmentation and automation is essentially unchanged in our new data, with augmentation still comprising 57% of usage." (§ "How does augmentation vs. automation vary by task and occupation?"). Comparison: V2 against V1 on the same five-mode classification. The word "still" is the only place the V1 value (also 57%) is stated.
12. **Learning rose from about 23% to about 28%.** "we did see some change in *types* of automation and augmentation uses—for example, we saw *learning* interactions, where the user asks Claude for information or explanation about different topics, rise from ~23% to ~28%." (§ "How does augmentation vs. automation vary by task and occupation?"). Caption: "The balance of augmentation and automation has stayed relatively constant in the two months between our data samples (V1 and V2), though the share of Learning conversations has grown appreciably."

We do just that in this report" (§ "How does augmentation vs. automation vary by task and occupation?"). This is the report's headline data contribution alongside the clusters.
14. **Community and Social Service tasks approach 75% augmentation.** "we find some categories are highly augmentative; for example, Community and Social Service tasks, which includes education and guidance counseling, approach 75% augmentation." (§ "How does augmentation vs. automation vary by task and occupation?"). Comparison: across high-level occupational categories with ≥0.5% representation.
15. **Production and computer/mathematical categories sit near 50-50.** "At the other end of the extreme, tasks associated with production or computer and mathematical occupations, we see the balance skew closer to 50-50%." (same section).

(same caption).

20. **A 630-cluster bottom-up taxonomy of Claude.ai usage is released, described as first-of-its-kind.** "we're releasing a first-of-its-kind bottom-up taxonomy of usage on Claude.ai. The benchmarks, the accuracy levels and the size of the improvement are not published.

## Data and methods

In the wiki author's words, with references.

**Platform and sample.** Claude.ai Free and Pro conversations only — no Team/Enterprise, no first-party API, no Claude Code (§ "What's changed since the launch of Claude 3.7 Sonnet?"). One million anonymised conversations drawn from the 11 days following the Claude 3.7 Sonnet launch. The page does not give the window's calendar dates in the body; the headline figure caption dates the new sample "Feb '25 - March '25" and the V1 sample "Dec '25 - Jan '25". The latter is a typographical error on the live page — the first report's window was December 2024 to January 2025, and the surrounding text says the samples are "two months" apart, which is inconsistent with a Dec 2025 start for a March 2025 report. Recorded here so that nobody quotes "Dec '25" as a fact. Model composition of the sample is described only as "The vast majority of the data we analyzed was from Claude 3.7 Sonnet, as it is set as the default on Claude.ai and our mobile app."

**Filtering.** Two changes from V1, both in § "Additional methodological details": (i) the V1 filter that kept only conversations judged relevant to an occupational category was dropped, leaving only exclusion of conversations that flagged Anthropic's safety classifiers — the stated motive is to preserve more data for the bottom-up taxonomy, and the stated effect is "similar results", unquantified; (ii) Claude 3.7 Sonnet replaced Claude 3.5 Sonnet at every point in the Clio pipeline, on the grounds of higher classification accuracy on internal, unpublished benchmarks. Both changes sit between V1 and V2 and are not separated from the behavioural change the report reports.

**Taxonomies.** Top-down: O\*NET, "17,000 tasks in the U.S. Department of Labor's O\*NET database", with tasks rolled up to occupations and to top-level occupational categories (SOC major groups; the release ships `SOC_Structure.csv` and `onet_task_statements.csv` for the join). Bottom-up: a Clio-generated hierarchy over the same conversations, 630 level-0 clusters in three levels, with descriptions, prevalence, collaboration ratios, an O\*NET task mapping and a thinking ratio per cluster.

**Interaction-mode classification.** The five-mode collaboration classification carried over from the first report — Learning, Task Iteration, Validation (augmentation) and Directive, Feedback Loop (automation), plus a "none" residual in the released files. The report gives glosses, not a full definition, for each mode and no definition at all for Validation and Feedback Loop; the automation/augmentation assignment appears explicitly only in the release data dictionary (Definitions 21).

**Thresholds.** Occupation and category leaderboards are restricted to units "with at least 0.5% representation in the data" / "in the overall dataset" (captions in § "How are people using extended thinking mode?", § "How does augmentation vs. automation vary by task and occupation?", § Appendix). Cluster prevalence figures are bucketed into 100 buckets and replaced by bucket means for privacy (release documentation). No other privacy thresholds are stated on the page.

**Statistical methods.** None beyond shares, ratios and one cumulative-distribution curve. The report publishes no standard errors, confidence intervals, significance tests or regression; every V1→V2 comparison is a difference in point estimates. No weighting to any population is applied or claimed.

**Released data and code.** `release_2025_03_27` on Hugging Face, as listed under Source. Two notebooks ship: `v2_report_replication.ipynb` (the report's figures and analysis) and `cluster_level_data/cluster_level_example_analysis.ipynb` (example analyses of the cluster file). Licence per the dataset card: data CC-BY, code MIT.

**Wiki author's checks on the released files (not claims of the report; run 2026-09-16, and since confirmed in detail by the data steward in `data/releases/release_2025_03_27.md`, which supersedes this paragraph wherever the two differ).** (a) `automation_vs_augmentation_v1.csv` sums to 84.21% across its six rows while `automation_vs_augmentation_v2.csv` sums to 100.00% — the two versions are on different bases, consistent with the dropped occupational-relevance filter. (b) Renormalising each file over the five named modes (dropping "none") reproduces both published numbers: augmentation is 57.45% in V1 and 56.94% in V2 ("still comprising 57%"), and learning is 23.27% in V1 and 27.99% in V2 ("~23% to ~28%"). Taken at face value the raw V1 learning cell is 18.92% and the raw V2 cell is 27.08%, so anyone quoting the file without renormalising will not match the report, and a naive V1 automation share comes out at 34.6% rather than the comparable 42.6%. (c) `task_pct_v1.csv` has 3,514 rows and `task_pct_v2.csv` 3,365, each including a pseudo-task `none` — so the released task-share files cover roughly a fifth of the O\*NET task list, and V2 covers 149 fewer tasks than V1 even though V2 drops the relevance filter; the two lists are not a panel (733 tasks appear only in V1, 584 only in V2, per the steward's profile). (d) `cluster_level_dataset.tsv` has 630 rows, 630 distinct level-0 names, 145 distinct level-1 names and 30 distinct level-2 names, confirming "630 granular clusters … organized into three levels of hierarchy".

## Open questions, conjectures and promised follow-ups (verbatim)

## What it did not test

The wiki author's inference, not the report's words. Each item is something the report had the data for, or a construct it used without validating.

**Adjacent questions the released data could have answered**

1. **Whether the V1→V2 change survives the pipeline change.** Two things changed between samples besides the calendar: the occupational-relevance filter was dropped and the classifier model was swapped from 3.5 to 3.7 Sonnet. The report asserts "similar results" without re-running V1 data through the V2 pipeline, which is the one comparison that would separate measurement change from behaviour change. The released files show V1 and V2 on different bases (V1 sums to 84.2%, V2 to 100%), so the comparison is not innocent.
2. **Whether any V1→V2 difference is distinguishable from noise.** No interval, test or minimum detectable effect anywhere. With ~1M conversations the sampling error on a category share is tiny, which makes the absence of any uncertainty statement a presentational rather than a power problem — but it also means the report never says how large a change would have had to be to count as a change, while resting a headline on "essentially unchanged".
3. **Share versus level.** Every number is a share of traffic. The report never reports growth in absolute conversations, so a "rise in the share of usage for coding" is consistent with coding being flat and everything else falling. The report flags the share/level distinction in a caption but does not act on it.
4. **Launch effect versus diffusion.** The window is the 11 days immediately after a model launch, a period in which the user mix is most likely to be skewed toward early adopters and coding-forward users. The report offers "ongoing diffusion", "novel applications of coding" and "unexpected capability improvements" as candidate explanations but does not use a pre-launch window inside the same two months, or a non-3.7 holdout inside the same sample, to separate them — even though the sample contains non-3.7 conversations ("the vast majority" is not all).
5. **Composition versus within-occupation change.** No decomposition of the share change into changes in the mix of users/occupations versus changes in how a given occupation's tasks are used.
6. **The overall extended-thinking rate.** Occupation-level rates from ~6% to ~10% are published; the sample-wide rate is not, so a reader cannot tell whether "computer and information research scientists lead" means twice the base rate or a tenth above it.
7. **Extended thinking crossed with collaboration mode.** `has_thinking_ratio` and the five `collaboration:*_ratio` fields sit in the same cluster row, so "is extended thinking used more for directive or for learning interactions?" was one correlation away and is not reported.
8. **Extended thinking against task complexity, wage or Job Zone.** The O\*NET join that the first paper used for the wage gradient was available; the report describes extended thinking as being for "more complex questions" and then never tests the rate against any complexity or wage measure.
9. **The top-down/bottom-up comparison it says the data enables.** No measure is given of what share of conversations or clusters map poorly onto O\*NET — which is the quantity implied by the stated motivation for building the taxonomy. The cluster file carries an `onet_task` field, so the coverage gap could have been quantified.
10. **Cluster prevalence.** Six clusters are named as "particularly interesting" with no prevalence figures, no selection rule beyond interest, and no ranking. `percent_records` and `percent_users` are both released, so the breadth-versus-depth question (uses common across many users versus heavy use by few) was available and untouched.
11. **Automation/augmentation at the cluster level.** The release ships collaboration ratios per cluster; the report analyses collaboration only through the O\*NET top-down frame, so the question of whether the bottom-up view gives a different automation share is left open.
12. **The task-coverage counts.** V2's released task file covers 149 fewer tasks than V1's despite a less restrictive filter; the report's task-curve appendix discusses the shape of the curve but not the count of tasks touched, and does not reconcile the two.
13. **Healthcare.** Named in the opening bullet as rising and then absent from the body, the figures and the conclusion.
14. **How much data the safety-classifier filter removes.** Not reported, so the effect of the one remaining filter on the composition of the sample is unknown.
15. **Anything below the 0.5% floor.** Every leaderboard excludes small occupations by construction, so "no occupational categories where automation dominates" is a statement about large categories only, and the most automatable niche occupations are the ones most likely to be excluded. It is also inaccurate about the categories that *are* plotted: two of the seventeen exceed 50% automation (Claims 16).

**Constructs used without validation in this report**

16. **Extended thinking as a proxy for task difficulty.** The mode is a user-set toggle. Its rate mixes task complexity with user sophistication, habit, product defaults and the price/latency a user is willing to pay. The report describes it as enabling the model "to think for longer when answering more complex questions" and then reads occupational rankings off it as if it measured the task. No validation, and no statement of whether the toggle was equally available or equally defaulted for Free and Pro users.
17. **The five-mode collaboration classification under a new classifier.** The modes are re-measured with a different model than V1 and the accuracy claim rests on "internal benchmarks" that are not described, not published and not versioned. No human-validation exercise is reported in this document.
18. **Occupation inferred from the task, not the user.** The whole occupational frame attributes conversations to occupations through O\*NET task matches; the report's own "fine artists" caveat concedes the mapping can misdescribe the activity, and no validation of the task→occupation attribution is reported here.
19. **The "none" / "filtered" residual.** A sixth category appears in the released data and never in the report; what it contains, and whether it grew or shrank between V1 and V2, is not discussed although it is the arithmetic difference that makes the two versions non-comparable at face value.
20. **The cluster hierarchy itself.** 630/145/30 levels generated by a model, with no stated stability check (would a re-run produce the same 630?), no inter-level coherence measure and no human audit reported.
