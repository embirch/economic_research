## Definitions (verbatim)

Every construct, gate, factor, measure variant and threshold, quoted in full with its page. The three displayed equations are images in the PDF; they are transcribed here character-for-character from the typeset equation as rendered on the page, including the under-brace labels, and were checked against the rendered page image (see Verification). Everything else is quoted from the document's text.

**1. The weighted usage count (p.2).** Introduced by: "We begin at the task level. We only consider a task as covered if it sees sufficient traffic in our Economic Index samples (Handa et al. 2025), with more weight given to API usage (indicating deeper integration in production systems):"

> WorkUsage_t  =  ClaudeWorkUsage_t  +  APIUsage_t
> (under-braces, left to right) "Weighted work-related count for task t" ; "Observed work-related usage in Claude.ai" ; "Observed usage in 1P API"

**2. ClaudeWorkUsage_t (p.2).** "*ClaudeWorkUsage*_t is the count of tasks t in Claude.ai that were classified as work-related. We rely on the use case primitive from Appel et al. (2026) to restrict to work-related transcripts, as opposed to educational and personal use cases.¹ Restricting the counts to work-related uses seems to better capture the scope for labor market impacts. For example, using AI to explain a science lecture (coursework) or to get advice on treating an injury (personal use) seem fairly removed from automating the work of teaching or nursing."

**3. APIUsage_t (p.2).** "The final term, *APIUsage*t, counts all 1P API traffic of task *t*. We do not differentiate between API calls that are work related, since API calls typically signal integration into production workflows.²"

**4. The usage gate (p.2).** "The strict gate we impose is that WorkUsage_t must be 100 or 0.0025% of traffic.³ The task counts we observe have a long tail of low-count uses that likely reflect uncommon behavior, testing, or classifier errors. The exact cutoff has little impact on the job rankings. Tasks that do not meet the gate of WorkUsage_t ≥ 100 are assigned an exposure of 0."

**5. Justification of the 100 threshold — note 3 (p.11).** "Usage of 100 represents 0.0025% of traffic using the previous two Anthropic Economic Index reports (2M from Claude.ai and 2M from 1P API), which is similar to the median share of time spent on a particular O\*NET task (calculated by combining our time fraction estimates and employment estimates from BLS), 0.0014%. See Tomlinson et al. (2025) for a related gating approach."

**6. Task grouping across occupations (p.2).** "Some occupations share tasks, for example the task 'Observe and evaluate students' performance, behavior, social development, and physical health' appears for ten types of K-12 teachers. Similarly, several tasks differ by just a word or comma. Since we do not record the contextual information to assign these to one job or another, we group these identical and highly similar tasks and allocate the task count equally across jobs according to employment shares."

**7. What the measure is meant to be (pp.2–3).** "This measure is meant to identify initial steps toward implementation and automation. It does not measure the intensive margin of AI use (increased adoption in debugging vs. email drafting, for example), because the connection with usage and work performed is fuzzy and highly dependent on the task. At the level of a job, however, coverage will increase as more of its tasks are observed and automated."

**8. Observed exposure of a task, r̃_t (p.3).** Introduced by: "Exposure of a task t is then given by r̃_t:"

> r̃_t = 𝟙{WorkUsage_t ≥ 100} × 𝟙{β_t ≥ 0.5} × α_t

**9. The β term (p.3).** "where β_t is the task's exposure from Eloundou et al. (2023), set to 1 if β is above zero. This amounts to ounting any tasks that are theoretically doable with an LLM or an LLM plus tools like websearch and image recognition.⁵ We upgrade the tasks with a β of 0.5 because many LLMs today will have such capabilities, and the extent to which they are not actually helpful will be captured in the usage and automation measures." [The word "ounting" is printed as such on p.3; no correction is made here. Note 5 (p.11): "See the exact elicitation in the Appendix of Eloundou et al. (2023)."]

**10. The α automation factor (p.3).** Introduced by: "The term α_t is a factor meant to upweight tasks seeing relatively more automative uses. It is defined as follows:"

> α_t = 1/2 + 1/2 × ( ClaudeWorkUsage_t × AutoShare_t + APIUsage_t ) / ( ClaudeWorkUsage_t + APIUsage_t ) ,
> (under-brace on the fraction) "Automation share"

**11. What α means at its bounds (p.3).** "where *AutoShare*_t is the share of Claude.ai usage that is automative. A task that sees only augmentative uses and does not appear in the API transcripts would have α_t equal to 0.5 (because the Automation share gets zeroed out). A task with only automative uses would have α_t = 1. The goal here is to have coverage scale with the degree of automation, but for augmentative uses to still count towards coverage."

**12. What a high-exposure job looks like (p.3).** "In this framework, the jobs that have high exposure will be those whose tasks have at least some usage, are theoretically doable with an LLM, and are mostly used in automative patterns."

**13. The job-level measure, R_o (p.4).** Introduced by: "Then our job-level measure is calculated as:"

> R_o = ( Σ_{t ∈ 𝒯_o} w_t · r̃_t ) / ( Σ_{t ∈ 𝒯_o} w_t )

**14. The terms of R_o and how to read it (p.4).** "where T_o is the set of tasks for job o, w_t is the fraction of time spent on task *t* (Tamkin and McCrory, 2025), and r̃_t is the task-level exposure. This measure can be loosely interpreted as the share of a job being performed or accelerated by an LLM, though the automation weighting means it is not a pure percentage of task coverage. Comparing two jobs that differ by 0.10 on the measure, the higher-coverage job could have a 10 percentage point higher share of their day covered by AI, or a 20 percentage point higher automation share in our data."

**15. Imputation of the work share — note 1 (p.10).** "The use-case primitive (which is needed to calculate the share of work-related queries) was introduced in the September data, so in the August data we impute the percent of work-related Claude.ai traffic using a model trained on embeddings of the September tasks."

**16. Why non-work conversations are excluded, and why API calls are not — note 2 (pp.10–11).** "Of course, some non-work conversations could impact the relevant jobs. People getting nutrition plans and detailed biology instruction from AI may rely less on professional biology tutors and nutritionists. In practice, counting these transcripts tends to increase noise overall given the range of inquiries around neurology and animal care, for instance. There may be personal use cases of API workflows. We still weight these the same as work-related Claude.ai tasks because personal questions (say, nutrition advice) that are routed through an API call seem more likely to represent incipient automation."

**17. High exposure (the treated group) (p.5).** "In the main text, we designate the top quartile workers as high exposure. Below, we vary the threshold of exposure used to define workers as high exposure. We estimate pooled effects using the same difference-in-differences regression and control group as before, except every point estimate uses a different treated group."

**18. The insured unemployment rate (p.6).** "Using these numbers in the numerator—and using the number of CPS respondents in that SOC group and state in the denominator—we calculate the insured unemployment rate, which is an estimate of the share of people in that job category currently receiving unemployment benefits."

**19. The UI source — note 6 (p.11).** "The specific data product is ETA 203 'Characteristics of the insured unemployed,' available at this link: https://oui.doleta.gov/unemploy/DataDownloads.asp"

**20. The task-granularity fix that was explored (p.8).** "A potential fix we explored is grouping O\*NET tasks with (i) a shared IWA and (ii) semantic similarity of at least 0.7. This leverages the grouping done by O\*NET researchers to validate a seeming conceptual match across two tasks. In practice, this mostly agrees with our slightly simpler task grouping described in the main text, with a Spearman correlation coefficient of 0.9."

**21. The comparison exercise and its statistic (p.8).** "There are many potential ways to measure job exposure. Here we explore the correlation between several different formulations. In the heatmap below, each cell gives the Spearman (rank-rank) correlation between the two job-level coverage measures."

**22. Every measure variant, quoted in full (pp.8–9).** The nine bullets as printed (the second and third run together in the original; the break is shown here as printed):

- "Baseline: this is our primary measure, observed coverage, described above."
- "Claude.ai usage: raw usage counts from the Anthropic Economic Index, summed to the occupation level and weighted by time fraction. This has a high Spearman correlation with our baseline measure (0.81) and the Eloundou et al. (2023) measure (0.72).Eloundou β (time-frac): Mean Eloundou et al. ## Data and methods

In the wiki author's own words, with page references.

- **Unit of construction.** The measure is built bottom-up: O\*NET task → task-level observed exposure r̃_t → time-weighted average over a job's tasks → R_o (pp.2–4). Occupations enter only through O\*NET's task lists and through time-fraction weights taken from Tamkin and McCrory (2025).
- **Inputs to the task-level measure.** Three: Claude.ai work-classified conversation counts (work-related by the use-case primitive of Appel et al. 2026), first-party API counts with no work filter, and Eloundou et al. (2023) β capability scores (pp.2–3). A fourth, AutoShare_t (the automative share of Claude.ai usage for the task), enters through α.
- **Three multiplicative components.** r̃_t is the product of a hard count gate (WorkUsage_t ≥ 100), a hard capability gate (β_t ≥ 0.5), and a continuous automation weight α_t ∈ [0.5, 1]. A task failing either gate scores exactly 0 (p.2, p.3). Note what this implies: the measure is binary in usage and capability and continuous only in automation — a task with 100 observations and one with 100,000 receive the same r̃_t if their automation shares match.
- **Two asymmetries between the platforms.** Claude.ai counts are filtered to work-related transcripts; API counts are not (p.2, note 2). And in α's numerator, Claude.ai counts are multiplied by AutoShare_t while API counts enter at full weight — which is where "more weight given to API usage" (p.2) actually bites, since the displayed WorkUsage_t equation on p.2 is an unweighted sum of the two despite its "Weighted work-related count" under-brace. (Wiki author's reading of the two equations, not a statement the appendix makes.)
- **Imputation across waves.** The use-case primitive exists in only one of the two waves; for the other, the work-related share of Claude.ai traffic is imputed from a model trained on embeddings of the wave that has it (note 1, p.10).
- **Employment analysis.** Difference-in-differences on the Current Population Survey: 22–25-year-olds in top-quartile-exposure occupations against 22–25-year-olds in no-exposure occupations, monthly/quarterly from 2016 to 2025, with "ChatGPT release" (a dashed line at end-2022 in Appendix Figure 1) as the treatment date. Event-time coefficients and a pooled post-period estimate are reported (pp.4–5). The appendix does not state the regression equation, the fixed effects, the clustering of standard errors, or the weighting.
- **Two robustness exercises on that analysis.** (i) The treated group is redefined at every percentile cutoff from about 50 to about 95, re-running the same regression with the same control group (pp.5–6, Appendix Figure 2). (ii) The outcome is rebuilt from administrative unemployment-insurance claims — ETA 203, Department of Labor, aggregated to state × quarter × SOC major group — over CPS respondent counts in that SOC group and state, giving an insured unemployment rate (pp.5–6, note 6). Because UI data carry only major SOC groups, exposure quartiles for that check are formed over about twenty broad categories rather than detailed occupations, which the appendix says makes exposure "less precise" (p.5).
- **Task-granularity work.** Three candidate remedies for arbitrary task specificity are considered and two rejected by example (DWA aggregation, DWA-plus-similarity); the third — shared IWA plus semantic similarity ≥ 0.7 — was implemented and compared to the main-text grouping by Spearman correlation (pp.7–8). The similarity model and embedding source are not named.
- **Measure comparison.** Ten job-level measures are constructed and compared pairwise by Spearman rank correlation, displayed as a lower-triangle heatmap (pp.8–9, Appendix Figure 4). The set spans usage-only, capability-only (Eloundou β), success-adjusted, imputation-adjusted, two coarser aggregation levels (DWA, IWA) and two aggregation weights (time fraction, core weight). The number of occupations over which the correlations are computed is not stated, and no standard errors or bootstrap intervals accompany any correlation.
- **Public data.** The appendix ships no code and no data file, and names none. The job-level and task-level measures the main paper releases live in the `labor_market_impacts/` folder of the public Economic Index dataset (`job_exposure.csv`, `task_penetration.csv`); whether those files reproduce r̃_t and R_o as defined here, and which Economic Index waves feed them, is a data question and has been put to the steward (see Verification).

## What it did not test

The wiki author's inference, not the appendix's words. Robustness checks it could have run are marked **[could have run]**.

1. **The gate-invariance claim is asserted, never shown.** "The exact cutoff has little impact on the job rankings" (p.2) is the only unsupported robustness assertion in a document otherwise built on robustness checks. **[could have run]** the same Spearman-correlation exercise of Appendix Figure 4 across count gates of, say, 25, 50, 100, 250 and 1,000 — the machinery was already built.
2. **The β gate is never varied.** The measure uses 1{β_t ≥ 0.5} while the text says β is "set to 1 if β is above zero" (p.3), two rules that coincide only because Eloundou et al. give β ∈ {0, 0.5, 1}. No result is shown for the stricter gate β_t = 1, which would drop every "LLM plus tools" task. **[could have run]**
3. **α's functional form is never varied.** The 1/2 + 1/2 × (automation share) form fixes the floor at 0.5 and the range at 0.5. No alternative (α = automation share, a different floor, a convex weight) is scored, although the interpretation in claim 5 — that 0.10 on R_o could be a 10pp coverage difference or a 20pp automation difference — is entirely a consequence of that choice.
4. **The two platforms are never separated in the headline measure.** Claude.ai-only and API-only versions of R_o are not reported, and the "Claude.ai usage" variant in Figure 4 changes two things at once (drops the API and drops the gates and α), so the contribution of the API term cannot be read off. **[could have run]** an API-only column in the same heatmap.
5. **The work-filter asymmetry is never tested.** Note 2 (pp.10–11) argues from plausibility that non-work Claude.ai transcripts should be dropped and non-work API calls kept; no version of the measure that counts all Claude.ai traffic, or that work-filters the API, is compared. **[could have run]**
6. **The wave imputation is never validated.** Note 1 (p.10) imputes the work-related share for one wave from a model trained on the other. No held-out accuracy, no comparison of imputed against observed shares on the wave that has both, and no version of the measure built on the primitive-bearing wave alone. **[could have run]**
7. **The employment analysis has no power statement.** The pooled estimates are −0.0023 (SE 0.0058) in the CPS and +0.001 (SE 0.002) in the UI data; neither is paired with a minimum detectable effect, so a reader cannot tell which effect sizes the data could have ruled out. This is the single most consequential omission for a null result.
8. **No pre-trends test.** Appendix Figure 1's lower panel plots event-time coefficients back to 2016, but no joint test of pre-period coefficients, no pre-trend slope and no honest-DiD-style sensitivity to trend violations is reported.
9. **No alternative treatment date or event window.** ChatGPT's release is the only break considered; GPT-4, Claude's own releases, and the 2023–24 tech-sector layoffs are not used as alternative or placebo dates. **[could have run]** a placebo break in, say, 2019.
10. **No placebo outcome and no placebo group.** Older workers appear only in the main paper's framing, not here; no non-exposed-but-similar occupational placebo is run within this appendix, and the DiD is not re-estimated on an outcome that AI exposure should not move.
11. **No outcome other than unemployment.** Wages, hours, employment counts, transitions into and out of employment, vacancy postings and entry-level hiring are untouched in this appendix, though the measure could be attached to any of them.
12. **The DiD specification is never written down.** No equation, no fixed-effect structure, no clustering level, no weighting, no sample restriction beyond ages 22–25. **[could have run]** at minimum a stated specification and standard errors clustered at the occupation level, with an alternative clustering shown.
13. **Composition inside the top quartile is not examined.** The quartile is a mix of Computer & Mathematical and clerical work (p.6); no heterogeneity by SOC major group, by sector, or by education is reported, so an offsetting pattern within the treated group would be invisible.
14. **The Figure 4 correlations carry no uncertainty and no N.** The number of occupations is never stated, and no bootstrap interval accompanies correlations that are used to justify dropping measure variants (0.99, 0.97, 0.9). **[could have run]** bootstrap intervals over occupations.
15. **Rank correlation only.** Every measure comparison is Spearman. Two measures can rank occupations near-identically and still differ enough in levels to change a quartile cut — which is exactly the object the employment analysis uses. No level comparison, no share-of-jobs-switching-quartile table. **[could have run]**
16. **The IWA-plus-similarity fix is reported by one number.** The 0.9 Spearman (p.8) is not accompanied by the list of occupations that move most, by the similarity model used, or by sensitivity to the 0.7 threshold. **[could have run]** the same correlation at 0.6, 0.8 and 0.9.
17. **No sensitivity to the time-fraction weights themselves.** w_t comes from Tamkin and McCrory (2025) estimates; core weight is tested as an alternative, but the estimated time fractions are never perturbed, and O\*NET's own task-importance or task-frequency ratings are not used as a third weighting.
18. **The classifier is never audited here.** Task assignment is attributed to "our classifier" (p.7) with no accuracy, no confusion analysis and no human validation in this document; the specificity-bias problem it raises on p.7 is described, illustrated and then left in place.
19. **Employment-share allocation of shared tasks is not stress-tested.** Splitting a shared task's count by employment shares (p.2) mechanically gives large occupations more of the count; no alternative (equal split, share by time fraction) is compared, and no statement is made of how many of the 18,000 tasks are affected.
20. **Note 4 is orphaned.** Endnote 4 (p.11), on dropping the success rate, has no visible marker in the body text — markers 1, 2, 3, 5 and 6 all appear, 4 does not. Whatever sentence it was meant to support is not identifiable from the document. (Wiki author's observation from the printed pages.)
21. **The appendix does not state which Economic Index waves or public release files underlie any number in it**, so none of its figures is reproducible from the document alone.
