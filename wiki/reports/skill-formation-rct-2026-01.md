## Claims

Numbered; each carries its page and figure/table reference, the number as published, and the comparison it rests on. Numbers that appear only in a chart image are marked "(read from Figure N)". Numbers that appear only in web-page image alt text are marked "(alt text)" — there are none in this entry; the web page's two charts carry no alt text and no captions, so their values are recorded here from the paper.

**Headline: the treatment effect on skill**

1. **Using AI assistance reduced the quiz score by 4.15 points on a 27-point quiz, reported as "a 17% score difference or 2 grade points", Cohen's d=0.738, p=0.010** (PDF p. 9; Figure 6, p. 10; abstract-level restatement p. 2). Comparison: mean post-task quiz score of the treatment group (AI assistant available) against the control group (no AI), between subjects, n=52, 26 per arm. The web page states the same result as "participants in the AI group scored 17% lower than those who coded by hand, or the equivalent of nearly two letter grades" and gives group means the paper does not: "the AI group averaged 50% on the quiz, compared to 67% in the hand-coding group". Figure 6's quiz panel shows the treatment mean at ≈50.0% and the control mean at ≈65.4% (read from Figure 6, p. 10); 4.15/27 = 15.4 percentage points, not 17. See `## Verification`.
2. **Controlling for warm-up task time as a covariate, the effect remains significant: Cohen's d=0.725, p=0.016** (p. 9). Comparison: the same contrast, adjusted for the pre-treatment warm-up completion time.
3. **Quiz-score treatment effect ≈0.69 s.d., 95% CI ≈[0.17, 1.23]** (read from Figure 6, right panel, p. 10; starred at p<0.05). Comparison: standardised difference in group means, plotted with the control group scoring higher.

**Headline: the treatment effect on speed**

4. **Task completion time did not differ significantly: p=0.391** (Figure 6, left panel, p. 10). Group means ≈23.0 minutes (AI) against ≈24.7 minutes (No AI), 95% CIs ≈[20.7, 25.5] and ≈[21.7, 27.5] (read from Figure 6, p. 10); time treatment effect ≈0.23 s.d., 95% CI ≈[−0.32, 0.80] (read from Figure 6, p. 10). The web page renders this as "participants in the AI group finished about two minutes faster, although the difference was not statistically significant"; the paper's own summary is "we did not find a statistically significant acceleration in completion time with AI assistance" (p. 2) and "Our results do not conclusively find a speed up or slow down using AI in this task" (p. 11). Comparison: mean minutes to complete the two Trio tasks, capped at 35 minutes, treatment against control.
5. **4 of the 26 control participants did not complete the second task within the 35-minute limit; every participant in the AI condition completed the second task** (p. 11). Comparison: completion rates within the time cap, by arm. This censoring is not adjusted for in the time outcome (see `## What it did not test`, item 4).
6. **Median completion time in the AI condition was 19 minutes** (p. 14). Comparison: within-arm median, offered as context for the finding that some participants spent up to 6 minutes composing a single query.

**Heterogeneity**

7. **The control group's average quiz score is higher at every level of coding experience (1–3 years, 4–6 years, 7+ years)** (p. 11; Figure 7 caption, p. 10: "The control group (No AI) average quiz score is higher across all levels of coding experience"). Comparison: condition means within each experience stratum. The paper adds that the novice stratum is tiny: "While we do observe a slightly lower average completion time in the AI group among novice programmers, due to the small group size of the 1-3 year participant group (n=4), the difference in task time was not significant" (p. 11).
8. **Score decomposition by question type — exploratory, explicitly not pre-registered: "the largest score gap occurs in the debugging questions and the smallest score gap in the code reading questions"** (p. 11; Figure 8, p. 11). Values read from Figure 8 (AI / No AI, in %): Task1 ≈51.7 / ≈66.5; Task2 ≈44.4 / ≈60.2; Conceptual ≈46.6 / ≈62.8; Debugging ≈38.0 / ≈59.2; CodeReading ≈57.0 / ≈67.0 (read from Figure 8, p. 11). Comparison: condition means within each quiz subarea. The web page states the same ordering without numbers: "The largest gap in scores between the two groups was on debugging questions".
9. **Self-reported learning was higher in the control group; both groups reported high enjoyment** (p. 11; Figure 9, p. 12, 1–7 scale). **The treatment group found the Trio task easier than the control group, but both groups found the post-task quiz similarly challenging** (p. 11; Figure 10, p. 12, 1–5 scale). Comparison: post-task self-report by condition and by study stage.

**Mechanisms (qualitative analysis of screen recordings)**

10. **Six AI interaction patterns, with average completion time and average quiz score** (Figure 11, p. 13; described pp. 12–14; the same figure appears as the right panel of Figure 1, p. 2). Low-scoring: **AI Delegation** n=4, 19.5 min, 39%; **Progressive AI Reliance** n=4, 22 min, 35%; **Iterative AI Debugging** n=4, 31 min, 24%. High-scoring: **Conceptual Inquiry** n=7, 22 min, 65%; **Hybrid Code-Explanation** n=3, 24 min, 68%; **Generation-Then-Comprehension** n=2, 24 min, 86% (times and scores are the figure's own printed labels, Figure 11, p. 13). Comparison: clusters of treatment-group participants against one another, on the same two outcomes. The paper bands them as "high-scoring interaction patterns (65%-86% quiz score) vs low-scoring interaction patterns (24%-39% quiz score)" (pp. 18–19) and defines the high band as "clusters of behaviors where the average quiz score is 65% or higher" (p. 13). The six named groups total 24 participants, against a treatment arm of 26 (see `## Verification`).
11. **Query type counts across the treatment arm: explanation q=79, generation q=51, debugging q=9, capabilities questions q=4, appreciation q=4; "11 queries have multiple (two) labels"** (Table 3, p. 16; text p. 14). Comparison: frequency of each query type; explanation is the modal query.
12. **"21 out of 25 participants in the treatment group asked an explanation question"; "Only 16 of 25 or two thirds of the participants used AI to generate code. 4 of these participants only asked for code generation and no other types of question. In fact, 3 of the 8 lowest-scoring participants asked AI to generate code without asking for explanations"** (p. 14). Comparison: participant counts by query repertoire, and the overlap between pure-generation use and the bottom of the score distribution. Note the denominator 25 against the arm's 26 (see `## Verification`).
13. **Some participants spent up to 11 minutes in total interacting with the assistant, and up to 6 minutes composing a single query, within a 35-minute task** (pp. 2, 14; Figure 12, p. 15). The web page states this as "Several took up to 11 minutes (30% of the total time allotted) composing up to 15 queries"; the paper's §1.1 form is "Some participants asked up to 15 questions or spent more than 30% of the total available task time on composing queries" (p. 2). Comparison: the distribution of AI-interaction time and query counts within the treatment arm, offered as the explanation for the null speed effect.
14. **More queries correlate with longer completion time (all queries r=0.35, p=0.088; debugging-query ratio r=0.43, p=0.033); more debugging queries correlate with lower quiz scores (all queries r=−0.22, p=0.293; debugging-query ratio r=−0.41, p=0.043)** (Figures 18 and 19, p. 27; summarised p. 14). Comparison: within-treatment-arm correlations across participants; only the debugging-ratio correlations reach p<0.05.
15. **Adoption style and speed: participants who directly pasted AI code (n=9) finished fastest; those who manually retyped it (n=9) or used both (n=4) finished at a pace similar to the control group; a further n=4 mostly wrote their own code and only asked clarification questions** (p. 15; Figure 13, p. 16). **For quiz score "there was no notable difference between groups that typed vs directly pasted AI output"** (p. 15). Comparison: task time and quiz score across four adoption styles within the treatment arm, against the control mean. These four groups total 26.
16. **Errors: the median treatment participant hit 1 error in the whole task (Q1–Q3 0.0–3.0); the median control participant hit 3 (2.0–5.0); by task, treatment medians are 0.0 for both tasks, control medians 2.0 for both** (Table 4, p. 17; text p. 17). **"Among the 12 participants who completed both tasks without encountering errors, only two were in the control group"** (p. 17). **Trio-specific errors (RuntimeWarning, TypeError) are much rarer in the AI arm** (p. 17; Figures 14 and 15, p. 17). Comparison: error counts per participant by arm, and error composition by type.
17. **Active coding time was lower in the AI condition, and the control group achieved higher quiz scores with higher active time; within each condition, higher active time correlates with *lower* quiz score** (p. 18; Figure 16, p. 18). Comparison: minutes of active coding against quiz score, by condition and by experience band; the paper attributes the within-condition sign to experienced programmers coding faster.
18. **The delegation group's speed advantage: "This group finished much faster than the control group (19.5 minutes vs 23 minutes), but this group only accounted for around 20% of the participants in the treatment group"** (p. 19). Comparison: the AI Delegation cluster's mean time against a control figure of 23 minutes — which does not match Figure 6's control mean of ≈24.7 minutes (see `## Verification`).
19. **Participant feedback ran the other way from the productivity story: control participants called the task fun and the instructions helpful; treatment participants "wished they had paid more attention to the details of the Trio library", reporting feeling "lazy" and that "there are still a lot of gaps in (their) understanding"** (p. 18; full text in Tables 6 and 7, pp. 26, 28). Comparison: free-text feedback by condition, from the quarter of participants who left any.

**Pilot studies**

20. **Pilot D (n=20, 2 tasks) found both effects significant: task time Cohen's d=1.11, p=0.03, and quiz score Cohen's d=1.7, p=0.003, with the AI group faster and lower-scoring** (p. 9; Figure 5, p. 9, whose panels print p = 0.033* and p = 0.003**). **Task completion within 35 minutes was 60% in control against 90% in the AI group** (p. 9). Comparison: the same two outcomes as the main study, on an earlier task and quiz design. The main study's power analysis then "assumed a conservative effect size of d = 0.85 (half of the observed learning effect) to account for the potential effect size inflation typical in pilot studies" (p. 9); the realised main-study effect, d=0.738, came in below that assumption.
21. **Pilot non-compliance was the binding constraint on design: Pilot A (P1, n=39) "35% of participants in the no AI condition used AI assistance"; Pilot B (P1, n=107) "25% of participants used AI and screen recording was not an option from the participant platform"; Pilot C (P2, n=20) showed local item dependence in the quiz; Pilot D (P2, n=20) showed Python-syntax delays unrelated to Trio** (Table 2, p. 8; text p. 8). Comparison: across platforms and designs; the fix was to move to a platform with screen recordings (P2), split the quiz across pages, cut five tasks to two, and add syntax hints.
22. **Web-page summary claim not made in the paper: the meta description reads "A randomized trial with 52 developers found AI assistance lowered comprehension scores, except when used for explanations rather than delegation."** The paper's own statement of the same point is hedged: "Our qualitative analysis does not draw a causal link between interaction patterns and learning outcomes" (web page) and the clusters are small (n=2 to n=7).

**The outcome measures**

- PDF pp. 6–7, the four question types: "**Debugging** The ability to identify and diagnose errors in code. This skill is crucial for detecting when AI-generated code is incorrect and understanding why it fails." / "**Code Reading** The ability to read and comprehend what code does. This skill enables humans to understand and verify AI-written code before deployment." / "**Code Writing** The ability to write or pick the right way to write code. Low-level code writing, like remembering the syntax of functions, will be less important with further integration of AI coding tools than high-level system design." / "**Conceptual** The ability to understand the core principles behind tools and libraries. Conceptual understanding is critical to assess whether AI-generated code uses appropriate design patterns that adheres to how the library should be used."
- PDF p. 6, the instrument: "The two tasks in our study cover 7 core concepts from the Trio library. We designed a quiz with debugging, code reading, and conceptual questions that cover these 7 concepts. We exclude code writing questions to reduce the impact of syntax errors in our evaluation; these errors can be easily corrected with an AI query or web search. We tested 5 versions (Table 2) of the quiz in user testing and preliminary studies based on item response theory. For example, we ensure that all questions are sufficiently correlated with the overall quiz score, that each question has an appropriate average score, and that the questions are split up such that there is no local item dependence between questions (i.e., participants could not infer the answers to a question by looking at other questions). The final evaluation we used contained 14 questions for a total of 27 points. We submitted the grading rubric for the quiz in our study pre-registration before running the experiment."
- PDF p. 11, the subarea construct: "Each question in the quiz belonged to exactly one task (e.g., Task 1 or Task 2) and exactly one question type (e.g., Conceptual, Debugging, or Code Reading)."
- PDF p. 25 (§C.1), the knowledge categories: "The evaluation covers 7 core concepts from the Trio library: 1. **Async and await keywords**: When to use await keywords within async functions. … 2. **Starting Trio functions**: Basic Trio usage including how to spawn tasks and how spawned tasks with different durations behave. 3. **Error handling in Trio**: Understanding error propagation patterns and how to catch errors in child tasks. … 4. **Coroutines**: When calling async functions, how to debug co-routine never awaited errors. 5. **Memory channels using Trio**: Understanding that start_soon doesn't return anything and how to use dictionaries, lists and other memory channels to collect data when running multiple tasks in parallel. 6. **Opening and closing a Trio nursery**: Understanding asynchronous context managers and how to use them. … 7. **Sequential vs concurrent execution**: The expected behavior of concurrent tasks."
- Pre-registration, q15, the outcomes as registered: "Outcomes: Time of completion (minutes per task) - Half-completed tasks will be counted as 0.5 tasks. Quiz Score: A post-task quiz is administered that takes 30 minutes. The quiz grading is described in the appendix."
- Pre-registration, q18, the scoring rule: "The main transformation to note in our task is the calculation of quiz scores, a main outcome in our study. Based on the answer key attached, 1 point for multiple choice questions, 2-3 points for open answer questions. The total score on the quiz is 27 points."
- PDF p. 14, the two annotated timing events that define AI interaction time: "We capture the time invested in interacting with AI by labeling the time between when users start to type a query (annotated as an AI Interaction event) and when an answer from the AI assistant is produced (annotated as an AI Query event)."

**Skill formation, as the paper models it**

- Abstract, p. 1: "We conduct randomized experiments to study how developers gained mastery of a new asynchronous programming library with and without the assistance of AI. We find that AI use impairs conceptual understanding, code reading, and debugging abilities, without delivering significant efficiency gains on average."
- PDF p. 4 (§3 Framework, "Professional Skill Acquisition"): "The 'learning by doing' philosophy has been suggested by many learning frameworks such as the Kolb's experiential learning cycle, and the Problem-Based Learning (PBL) [Kolb, 2014, Schmidt, 1994]. The frameworks connect the completion of real-world tasks with the learning of new concepts and the development of new skills. … In its simplest form, we model AI tool assistance as taking a different learning path than without AI. We hypothesize that using AI tools to generate code in the development process effectively amounts to taking a shortcut to task completion without a pronounced learning stage."
- Figure 2 caption, p. 5: "With AI assistance becoming more ubiquitous in the workplace, novice workers may complete tasks without the same learning outcomes. Our experiments aim to investigate the process of task completion requiring a new skill to understand the impact of AI assistance on coding skill formation."
- PDF pp. 4–5, the research questions: "RQ1: Does AI assistance improve task completion productivity when new skills are required?" and "RQ2: How does using AI assistance affect the development of these new skills?"
- Pre-registration, q2, the registered hypotheses in full: "Hypotheses RQ1: Does using AI assistance reduce the learning of a skill? RQ2: Is there a tradeoff between skill acquisition and productivity?"
- PDF pp. 2, 5, the learning setting: "We run randomized experiments that measure skill formation by asking participants to complete coding tasks with a new library that they have not used before. This represents one way in which engineers acquire and learn new skills, since new libraries are frequently introduced in languages such as Python. We then evaluate their competency with the new library." And on why these tasks: "These tasks are designed to parallel the process of learning to use a new library or new software tool through a brief self-guided tutorial. For example, in software engineers' on-boarding materials, there is often a description of how to use an internal library and small tasks to build skills with the new library" (p. 6).
- PDF p. 5, why Trio: "We designed an experiment around the Python Trio library, which is designed for asynchronous concurrency and input-output processing (I/O). This library is less well known than asyncio (according to the number of StackOverflow questions) and involves new concepts (e.g., structured concurrency) beyond just Python fluency. It is also explicitly designed to be easy to use – making it particularly suitable for a learning experiment."
- PDF p. 6, the two tasks: "After several pilot studies, we used the first two tasks in our main study; each task took 10 - 20 minutes during initial testing. The first task is to write a timer that prints every passing second while other functions run. This task introduces the core concepts of nurseries, starting tasks, and running functions concurrently in Trio. The second task involves implementing a record retrieval function that can handle missing record errors in the Trio library. This task introduces concepts such as error handling and memory channels to store results. These two tasks are standalone; we provide sufficient instructions and usage examples so that participants can complete one task without the other."

**Sample and randomisation rules**

- PDF p. 6, the design: "We use a between-subjects randomized experiment to test for the effects of using AI in the coding skill formation process. Each participant first completed a warm-up coding task on a coding platform, where they needed to add a border around a list of strings. This Python coding question takes an average of 4 minutes to complete among users of this coding platform. There are no asynchronous concepts in this coding question."
- PDF p. 7, the warm-up's purpose: "No participants have access to AI while completing the warm-up stage. We use this stage to calibrate the Python familiarity of the participants and to help participants familiarize themselves with the interface."
- PDF p. 7, the sample and inclusion rule: "In our main study, 52 participants completed the task, 26 for each of the control and treatment groups. For all our pilot studies and the main study, we only recruited participants who self-reported having more than one year of Python experience, code in Python at least once a week, have tried AI coding assistance at least a few times, and have never used the Trio library before (Table 1)."
- PDF p. 8, burden and pay: "Together, these tasks take a maximum time of 1 hour and 15 minutes with an average duration of 58.5 minutes. Participants were recruited through a third party crowd-worker platform and paid a flat rate of 150 USD for the task."
- PDF p. 9, recruitment and balance: "To recruit 50 participants, we sent our study to 58 crowd workers. Participants were balanced across the following attributes (recorded through a separate recruitment survey): years of coding experience, years of Python experience, prior usage of the Python Asyncio library, frequency of Python use in the past year, and an asynchronous programming familiarity score (a 5-question, multiple-choice concept check). The demographic breakdown of the participants, collected after the completion of the task to avoid the threat of stereotypes, is summarized in Figure 17. Most participants in our study hold a bachelor's degree, are between 25 and 35 years old, and work either as freelance or professional software developers."

## Data and methods

In the wiki author's words, with references.

**Design.** A two-arm, between-subjects randomised controlled trial with a single session per participant, run on a third-party coding platform with screen recording. Three stages: (i) a warm-up Python task with no Trio content and no AI for either arm; (ii) a 35-minute main stage with two Trio tasks, in which the treatment arm has a sidebar chat assistant and the control arm has documentation and web search only; (iii) a quiz with no AI for either arm, followed by a survey (PDF pp. 6–7; Figure 4, p. 7). Total session up to 1 h 15 min, mean 58.5 minutes, flat payment 150 USD (p. 8).

**N and arms.** Main study n=52, 26 per arm (p. 7; Table 1, p. 7). 58 crowd workers were invited to reach a target of 50; 53 completed all three parts; 1 was disqualified under the pre-registered criteria (p. 9). Qualitative analysis covers 51 screen recordings, one treatment recording being unavailable (p. 12 and footnote 3). Four pilots preceded the main study: A n=39 and B n=107 on platform P1, C n=20 and D n=20 on platform P2 (Table 2, p. 8).

**Randomisation.** The paper states only that participants "were balanced across" five recruitment-survey attributes (p. 9) and that the design is "between-subjects randomized" (p. 6); it never describes the allocation procedure, the unit of randomisation, or who performed it. The pre-registration supplies the missing rule: stratified randomisation across years of Python experience, frequency of Python coding in the last year, the pre-test knowledge quiz and prior asyncio use (pre-registration q7). Participants knew whether they had AI but were not told another condition existed (pre-registration q5). Table 1 (p. 7) reports counts by arm on five covariates with a "Difference (%)" column, and no test statistics.

**Treatment.** A chat assistant in the coding environment, built on **GPT-4o**, prompted "to be an intelligent coding assistant", with read access to the participant's current code and able to emit the full correct solution on request (p. 6). Control participants pledged not to use generative AI and were told the platform can detect AI use (Figures 21 and 23, p. 29). Both arms were permitted web search "only for language documentation and syntax reference" (Figures 21–22, p. 29). Compliance was verified by watching screen recordings — the reason for moving off platform P1, where 25–35% of control participants used AI anyway (p. 8).

**Outcomes.** Two pre-registered outcomes: total task completion time in minutes (censored at 35) and the quiz score in points out of 27 (pre-registration q15, q18; PDF p. 6). The quiz has 14 questions covering 7 Trio concepts, in three question types — debugging, code reading, conceptual — with code-writing questions deliberately excluded (p. 6). Five quiz versions were tested in pilots using item-response-theory criteria, and the grading rubric was filed in the pre-registration before the main study (p. 6; the rubric appears in the registration record as q23, with per-question point allocations). An attention check is one of the 14 questions (pre-registration q23, "Q7: Attention Check").

**Estimators.** Difference in group means with t-tests, reported as Cohen's d with p-values (p. 9; pre-registration q17). One covariate-adjusted specification is reported, controlling for warm-up task time (p. 9). Error bars in Figures 5–8 are 95% CIs. No regression table, no standard errors in points, and no multiple-comparison adjustment is reported anywhere in the paper, although the pre-registration commits to false-discovery-rate scaling at 5% for multiple hypotheses (q19). The subarea decomposition is labelled "exploratory data analysis (not pre-registered)" (p. 11), as is the task-experience analysis (p. 11).

**Qualitative method.** Manual annotation of 51 screen recordings against a ten-event codebook (Table 5, p. 25), from which four axes are derived — AI interaction time, query types, errors encountered, active coding time (p. 12) — and a typology of six interaction patterns "based on query types, number of queries, queries per task, and active time" (p. 12). The paper is explicit that this is descriptive: the web page states "Our qualitative analysis does not draw a causal link between interaction patterns and learning outcomes, but it does point to behaviors associated with different learning outcomes." No inter-annotator agreement statistic is reported, and the paper does not say how many people annotated.

**Pre-registration.** https://osf.io/w49e7, registered 2025-06-09, public, OSF Preregistration template. It carries the hypotheses, sample-size target, stopping rule, stratification, disqualification criteria, outcome definitions, grading rubric, inference threshold and analysis plan quoted above. One internal oddity in the record: the "existing data" item (q8) is answered "Registration following analysis of the data", while the explanation (q9) reads "Registration prior to creation of data: As of the date of submission of this research plan for preregistration, the data have not yet been collected, created, or realized. We did conduct a pilot study (n=20) and updated a bug in the coding task." The two answers point in opposite directions; the q9 text and the 2025-06-09 registration date are consistent with registration before the main study, which is also what the paper claims ("We submitted the grading rubric for the quiz in our study pre-registration before running the experiment", p. 6). Recorded here as an observation, not a finding.

**Released data and code.** The GitHub repository releases task instructions, starter code, model solutions and the annotated transcripts (p. 24 §B.2). It does not release the quiz items with responses, the participant-level outcome table, the survey data, the screen recordings or the analysis code; the OSF registration reports no attached data, analytic code or materials. None of the numbers in the Results section can therefore be recomputed by a reader from the released files. The paper's own commitment is narrower than replication: "We make these annotated transcripts publicly available for future studies" (p. 12).

## Limitations (verbatim)

The paper has no section headed "Limitations"; its limitations are stated in §7.1 "Future Work" and are quoted in full here, followed by every other passage in which the paper or the web page bounds its own findings.

**§7.1 Future Work, pp. 19 (preamble and six bullets)**

- Preamble: "Our work is a first step to understanding the impact of AI assistance on humans in the human-AI collaboration process. We hope that this work will motivate future work that addresses the following limitations:"
- "**Task Selection**: This study focuses on a single task using a chat-based interface. This should be a lower bound for cognitive offloading since agentic AI coding tools would require even less human participation. In our work, users who relied on AI without thinking performed the worst on the evaluation; a completely agentic tool would create a similar effect. Future work should investigate the impacts of agentic coding tools on learning outcomes and skill development."
- "**Task Length**: Ideally, skill formation takes place over months to years. We measured skill formation for a specific Python library over a one-hour period. Future work should study real-world skill development through longitudinal measurement of the impacts of AI adoption."
- "**Participant Realism**: While participants in our study were professional or freelance programmers, there was not the same incentive to learn the library as if it were required for their actual job. Future studies should aim at studying the skill acquisition fro novice workers within a real company."
- "**Prompting Skills**: We collect self-reported familiarity with AI coding tools, but we do not actually measure differences in prompting techniques. An extension to our would also involve testing the level of prompting fluency beyond self-report."
- "**Evaluation Design**: Our study measures skill formation through a comprehensive quiz. Other studies could use the completion of another task or design coding as alternative evaluation strategies."
- "**Human Assistance**: We do not include the counterfactual of how skill formation would be impacted by receiving assistance from humans. Since human assistance and feedback takes place in a diverse settings (e.g., classroom, pair programming, code review), future work can compare the effect of feedback from AI vs humans in all these settings on skill formation."

**Bounds stated elsewhere in the paper**

- p. 11, on the experience heterogeneity: "While we do observe a slightly lower average completion time in the AI group among novice programmers, due to the small group size of the 1-3 year participant group (n=4), the difference in task time was not significant."

It is possible that AI both accelerates productivity on well-developed skills and hinders the acquisition of new ones, though more research is needed to understand this relationship."

## What it did not test

The wiki author's inference from the fetched paper (v1 and v2), the web page, the OSF registration record and the GitHub repository; not the authors' own statements.

1. **It did not test Claude.** The assistant was GPT-4o (p. 6). No Claude model, no Claude Code, no agentic or autocomplete surface was in the experiment, and the paper's claim that agentic tools would show a larger effect (p. 14; web footnote 1) is a conjecture with no evidence in the study. Anything our programme builds on this paper must say "an AI assistant" for the treatment, and cannot say "Claude" of any finding here — the opposite direction to our usual naming rule, and a point a referee will raise.
2. **It did not measure durable skill.** The quiz follows the task within one session (total mean 58.5 min, p. 8). Retention, transfer to a second task, or any measurement at a later date is absent, and the paper concedes "Ideally, skill formation takes place over months to years" (p. 19). The construct measured is immediate post-task comprehension of one library.
3. **It did not vary the incentive to learn.** Participants were paid a flat 150 USD (p. 8) and instructed to work "as fast as possible" (Figures 23–24, p. 29) while being told a quiz would follow. No arm was told the quiz mattered for pay, and no arm was told to learn rather than finish. The treatment instructions additionally differ from the control's in their stated *goal* ("utilize the AI assistant to **speed up task completion**" against "work with a new library and complete tasks as fast as possible"), so instruction framing is bundled with AI access; the paper never flags this as part of the treatment.
4. **It did not handle the censored time outcome.** Four control participants hit the 35-minute cap without finishing task 2 while all treatment participants finished (p. 11); their times enter the mean at the cap, which biases the measured time difference *towards* the AI group and still leaves it insignificant. The pre-registration's rule for partial completion ("Half-completed tasks will be counted as 0.5 tasks", q15) is not mentioned in the paper, and no survival, Tobit or per-task analysis is reported.
5. **Several pre-registered analyses are not reported.** The registration commits to quartile-based skill-level heterogeneity, age subgroups, a usage-pattern split by self-reported generation share, causal forests for heterogeneous effects (q17), and false-discovery-rate scaling at 5% for multiple hypotheses (q19). The paper reports none of these: heterogeneity appears only as three experience bands in one figure, and no multiplicity adjustment is mentioned despite the subarea decomposition testing five additional contrasts. No deviation log is published.
6. **The interaction typology is not tested against random assignment.** The six patterns are defined post hoc on treatment-group behaviour (pp. 12–14), with n between 2 and 7 and no control-side counterpart. The comparison that would license the web page's framing — that explanation-seeking use preserves learning — is a selected-subgroup comparison, never instrumented, matched on baseline covariates, or compared with a principal-stratification or complier analysis. Whether the high scorers would have scored highly without AI is untestable in this design.
7. **It did not measure the quality of the code produced.** Task completion is recorded as "Correct output is achieved" (Table 5, p. 25) and completion counts are reported, but no grading of the submitted code, no test-suite pass rate, no style or design assessment, and no comparison of code quality across arms appears. A trial about whether people can supervise AI-written code never scores the code.
8. **It did not separate the mechanism it proposes.** Errors encountered, active coding time, query composition and interaction time all differ by arm, and the paper attributes the learning gap to error resolution (pp. 3, 17). No arm isolates the channel — for example, a no-AI arm with errors suppressed, an AI arm forced to type its own code, or a learning-mode assistant — so error exposure, active time and cognitive effort remain confounded with the treatment.
9. **It did not test a learning-mode assistant, although it recommends one.** Both the paper (p. 20) and the web page point to ChatGPT Study Mode and Claude Code Learning/Explanatory mode. Neither was an arm, so the study offers no evidence that such modes recover the gap.
10. **No human-assistance arm.** The counterfactual to AI help is unaided work, not a mentor, pair programmer or reviewer (conceded, p. 19). The economic question of whether AI substitutes for the *mentoring* input to junior skill formation is therefore untouched.
11. **One library, one language, one skill.** All external validity beyond Python asynchronous concurrency is asserted. Trio was chosen partly because it "is also explicitly designed to be easy to use" (p. 5); whether an equally unfamiliar but harder tool, or a non-coding skill, shows the same effect is untested — as is the web page's extension to "any other industry".
12. **The web page's "mostly junior" framing is not what the balance table shows.** 29 of 52 participants are in the 7+ years of coding experience band and only 4 in the 1–3 year band (Table 1, p. 7); most participants hold a bachelor's degree and are 25–35 (p. 9). The paper's own novice-specific claims rest on that n=4 cell, and no analysis restricted to genuinely junior developers is reported. The literature the paper builds on (Peng et al., Cui et al., Brynjolfsson et al.) finds the *largest* productivity gains for novices, so the sample is the wrong one for testing the paper's own motivating scenario.
13. **Compliance in the main study is asserted, not quantified.** Pilot arms on platform P1 showed 25–35% control-side AI use (p. 8), and the fix was screen recording on P2. The paper says recordings "verified that participants did not use AI in the control group nor for the quiz" (p. 8) for the two P2 pilots, but reports no compliance rate, no excluded-for-non-compliance count and no web-search usage statistics for the main study, even though "Websearch" is one of the annotated events (Table 5, p. 25). Differential web-search intensity between arms is never reported.
14. **No baseline quiz on the outcome instrument.** Participants took a 5-question asynchronous-programming concept check at recruitment (p. 9; Table 1), but not the 27-point Trio quiz, so there is no within-participant measure of learning — only a post-treatment cross-sectional difference. Gain scores, and any test of whether the arms started level on Trio specifically, are unavailable.
15. **The results are not reproducible from the release.** The repository publishes tasks and annotated transcripts only; no outcome data, no quiz responses, no analysis code (§B.2, p. 24). Every number in Results is therefore uncheckable by a reader, including the group means that the web page states and the paper does not.
16. **No connection to the Economic Index data.** Although the web page frames the study against Anthropic's own observational 80%-time-savings estimate, nothing in the paper links the experimental setting to observed Claude usage: no mapping of the task to an O*NET task or Index request category, no check of how common "learning a new library" is in the Claude Code or API data, and no estimate of what share of real usage looks like the delegation pattern the study finds most harmful to learning. That gap is the natural opening for our programme.
17. **The trade-off is asserted at the individual level and never aggregated.** The paper's policy conjecture — that firms adopting AI aggressively may erode the supervisory capacity they will need — has no quantitative model attached: no estimate of how much learning is lost per unit of time saved, no cohort or pipeline arithmetic, and no comparison against the productivity gains reported elsewhere in Anthropic's work.
