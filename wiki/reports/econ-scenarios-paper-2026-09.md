## Claims

Numbered; each carries its page and table/figure reference, the number as published, and the comparison it rests on. **Framing**

1. **The paper's object is a mapping, not a forecast: five parameters in, six macro paths out, 2026–2030.** "We build a simple, integrated economic framework that converts a small set of parameters — chiefly, what fraction of tasks in the economy are affected by AI, how widely AI is used, how large the productivity gains per task are, and how much AI automates versus augments labor — into paths for productivity, growth, wages, the labor share, job reallocation, and unemployment from now until 2030" (p. 3). Comparison: scenarios against one another and against the no-AI path; explicitly not against a probability-weighted forecast — "The scenarios are not predictions, and we attach no probabilities to them" (p. 3).

2. **Three scenarios are placed against the published literature.** Modest (GDP +1.6% in 2030) is "only slightly higher than the estimate of Acemoglu (2025) and of the same order as the OECD figures of Filippucci, Gal, and Schief (2024, 2025)"; substantial (+8.3%) "corresponds to the forecasts that circulated from financial and consulting institutions in 2023 – roughly 1.5 percentage points of annual labor-productivity growth in Briggs and Kodnani (2023), and comparable magnitudes in McKinsey Global Institute (2023), Baily, Brynjolfsson, and Korinek (2023), Aghion and Bunel (2024), and the Penn Wharton Budget Model (Arnon and Smetters, 2025)"; extreme (+32%) "lies well beyond these and is best benchmarked against the literature on AGI-driven growth accelerations" (Korinek 2023; Korinek and Suh 2024; Davidson 2023; Erdil et al. 2025; Kokotajlo et al. 2025) (pp. 3–4). Comparison: this model's 2030 GDP gap against other authors' published estimates.

**The scenarios read one at a time**

8. **Modest change (§4.1, p. 32).** "In this scenario in 2030, just 4 percent of the tasks in the economy have been impacted by AI, in part because of modest capabilities and in part because of slow diffusion (m_2030 = 0.20 and diffusion is d_2030 = 0.20). AI raises productivity by around 35% on tasks that it impacts (a_2030 = 0.30)." GDP +1.6 percent is glossed as "an extra ten months of ordinary growth accumulated over four years"; the unemployment rate is "3.9 percent against a normal level of 3.8: the modest change scenario has only small labor market effects." Comparison: 4 percent = m×d = 0.20×0.20; 35% = exp(0.30) − 1.

9. **Substantial change (§4.2, pp. 32–33).** "In this scenario in 2030, 12 percent of the tasks in the economy — roughly 20 percent of the tasks performed by cognitive workers in 2025 — have been impacted by AI (m_2030 = 0.30 and diffusion is d_2030 = 0.40). On tasks that it impacts, AI raises productivity by approximately 57% (a_2030 = 0.45), and three quarters of the affected instances are automated (ψ_2030 = 0.75)." Two comparisons are drawn to history: "For comparison, the fastest GDP growth rate during the 1990s dot-com boom was 4.7 percent in 1999" (against this scenario's 5.4 percent, footnote 12 citing FRED GDPC1, p. 33); and the four-point fall in the labour share over four years is "only slightly less than the entire decline in the U.S. labor share over the four decades after 1980 (Karabarbounis and Neiman, 2014)" (p. 33). The report also states "the unemployment rate of cognitive workers rises from 2.9 percent in mid-2026 to 4.5 percent in 2030, more than a 50 percent increase" and that employment in all other occupations "has risen by 4.6 percent" (p. 34).

10. **Extreme change (§4.3, pp. 34–35).** "By 2030, 30 percent of the tasks in the economy — roughly half of the tasks performed by cognitive workers in 2025 — have been impacted by AI (m_2030 = 0.50 and diffusion is d_2030 = 0.60). AI is a powerful technology in this scenario, more than doubling productivity on the tasks it affects (a_2030 = 0.80 and exp(0.8) ≈ 2.2). Moreover, 90% of the affected instances are automated (ψ_2030 = 0.90) while just 10% are augmented. Finally, the reinstatement effect … is turned off (ρ = 0)." Outcomes as stated in the text: GDP "40 percent higher than in mid-2026, putting it 32 percent above the no-AI path"; growth of 15.4 percent a year, which "if this rate were sustained, per capita incomes would double every five years, compared with doubling every 35 years in the US over the past century"; cognitive wages 11.5 percent below the no-AI path, which "means that cognitive wages are slightly lower in 2030 than in mid-2026"; "A full 15 percent of GDP moves from being paid to labor to being captured as a return on investment"; the net return rising 6.5 → 8.3 percent, "an increase of about 28 percent"; and cognitive unemployment of 17.9 percent, "far above any postwar rate in the United States … annual unemployment rates peaked at slightly below 10 percent in the U.S. after the global financial crisis and at about 8 percent during the Covid-19 recession."

11. **Who gains and who loses, in the extreme scenario (p. 35).** "Total labor income in 2030 in the extreme scenario is almost exactly what it would have been without AI: the labor share falls by a quarter while GDP rises by a third, and 0.45 × 1.32 ≈ 0.60. All of the increase in GDP therefore accrues as capital income, which is 81 percent above its no-AI path." The cognitive wage bill is "31 percent below its previous path as wages are 11.5 percent lower paid on 21.5 percent fewer jobs", and "The economy's gain is thus almost three times the cognitive occupations' loss, so in the textbook sense, the gains could compensate the losses: a transfer of about 9 percent of GDP, roughly the size of Social Security and Medicare combined, would hold cognitive workers' income at its no-AI level and leave the rest of the economy more than 20 percent ahead." Comparison: the aggregate GDP gain against the cognitive occupations' wage-bill loss; and the required transfer against an existing programme of known size. The paper adds that "Transfers of that scale in response to technological change have no precedent" (p. 35).

**The innovation channel**

12. **The growth speedup from AI in research is small in every scenario, and this is flagged as a surprise.** "One surprising finding of our model is that the growth speedup from AI in innovation is relatively minor, even in the extreme change scenario. … Labor productivity rises through this channel by well under one percent in all three scenarios, and measured TFP by less" (p. 4). Comparison: the ideas channel against the level channel within the same scenario (Table 3 panel D: ideas stock 0.61 percent above the no-AI path in extreme against measured TFP 13.4 percent).

13. **The mechanism and its magnitude.** "On the extreme path, the uplift Δ ln R_t reaches 0.28 in 2030, so the ideas stock is headed for a level 10 percent above its no-AI path (and even less in TFP units). That is the most the channel delivers for a one-off change of that size. The realized values by 2030 are smaller still because the long-run gains take decades to cumulate. On the extreme path, the ideas stock is just 0.6 percent above its no-AI path in 2030" (p. 17). Comparison: the long-run elasticity `γ ≈ 0.35` applied to the research uplift, against what has cumulated by 2030.

**Mechanism results inside the model**

14. **The factor-price frontier: the two factor prices absorb the whole TFP gain, whatever the automation share.** "That is, between them the two factor prices absorb the entire TFP gain, whatever the values of ψ_t, ρ, and σ. If the rental rate does not move, as at ε = ∞, the wage rises by the full TFP gain" (p. 12, Equation (10), citing Caselli and Manning, 2019). Comparison: wage growth against rental-rate growth, share-weighted.

15. **Worked illustration at the substantial scenario's 2030 values (p. 12).** "Δ ln TFP_2030 ≈ 0.6 × 0.30 × 0.40 × 0.45 = 0.032, about 3 percent. (The exact solution is 0.029.)" With a pegged rental rate the wage would be 5.5 percent above its no-AI path; "at ε = 3 the rental rate in 2030 is 4.6 percent above r̄, so capital's term in Equation (10) is 0.4 × 0.045 = 0.018, and the wage is 1.9 percent above its no-AI path rather than 5.5 percent." Comparison: the same scenario under two capital-supply assumptions.

16. **When the wage can fall.** "When the saving per automated task is small relative to the transfer and when little capital is forthcoming, the rental price has to rise by enough to rationalize the transfer. If this rise is more than the cost saving, the wage is below its no-AI path … This is the "so-so automation" of Acemoglu and Restrepo (2019) in which displacement outweighs a modest productivity gain. In 2030 at our baseline ε = 3, the wage rises in every scenario. However, the wage falls if the capital supply elasticity ε is sufficiently small" (pp. 13–14; the threshold is footnote 3's Equation (12), p. 14).

17. **Cognitive employment falls by less than GDP rises.** Key result box, p. 14: "When s_N/s_C < 1, as it is here, cognitive employment falls by less than N rises, and therefore by less than Y rises; the loss in cognitive employment is smaller than the gain in GDP." Comparison: the proportional change in each group's employment, scaled by the relative size of the two groups (`s_N,t0/s_C,t0 ≈ 0.6`, Table A.2, p. 44).

18. **Half of every augmentation gain is spent on more of the same task.** "Each augmented instance needs a_t log points less labor. But because tasks are gross complements, demand for those instances rises by σa_t because they are cheaper, so only the fraction 1 − σ of the labor it saves is released. At σ = 0.5, half of every gain is spent on more of the same task" (p. 15, Equation (19)). Comparison: labour released by net displacement against labour released by the gains on augmented instances.

20. **Capability expectations (Figure 1, p. 29, four of the eight tasks shown).** Cumulative shares saying AI already can / will by 2027 / will by 2030: routine business emails and documents 53 / 65 / 75, "never": 19%; a working software product 24 / 38 / 58, "never": 27%; a small online business, no employees 19 / 32 / 52, "never": 32%; a Nobel-level scientific discovery 12 / 22 / 39, "never": 40% `(figure label)`. **Calibration facts the paper measures and reports**

30. **Observed exposure, averaged over occupations, gives the mid-2026 affected mass.** "It averages 0.22 in the cognitive group, 0.01 in the other, and 0.14 overall, so m = 0.14. The figure is conservative, since assistive use counts half, thinly used tasks count zero, and the data are from late 2025" (p. 27). Comparison: cognitive occupations against all other occupations on the same measure. This is the paper's only Claude-derived parameter anchor.

31. **Firm AI use gives the diffusion anchor.** "In late 2025, 18 percent of firms used AI (32 percent employment-weighted), and in 23 percent of firms workers used generative AI in their own tasks (Bonney et al., 2026). Firm shares overstate the share of instances done with AI, because adopters use it on few tasks and on only some instances of each. We therefore set the initial d = 0.10" (p. 27). Comparison: survey firm-adoption shares against the model's instance-level diffusion, with a downward adjustment that is asserted rather than estimated.

32. **The per-instance gain is set well below Anthropic's own conversation-based estimate.** "The productivity gain per instance starts at 0.30–0.45 in logs, reflecting estimates from field trials and frontier firms (Brynjolfsson et al., 2025; Huang et al., 2025), about a quarter of the gain of 1.6 estimated from conversations with Claude (Tamkin and McCrory, 2025)" (p. 27). Comparison: field-trial estimates against the Claude-conversation estimate; the paper takes the former.

33. **The automation share is anchored on the Economic Index.** "The Anthropic Economic Index classifies conversations by mode of use (Appel et al., 2025): about half of use on Claude.ai and about three quarters on the API looks like automation (the user delegates and the model executes). So 0.5 describes chat use today, and 0.9 a world in which agentic use is the norm" (pp. 27–28). Comparison: Claude.ai against the first-party API, as the two ends of the scenario range.

34. **Normal-times labour-market flows, from the CPS (§3.2, p. 26).** "Each month, on average, 22 percent of unemployed workers found a job, and 0.84 percent of employed cognitive workers and 1.84 percent of workers employed in all other occupations became unemployed." From the switching matrix: "19 percent of job-finders from cognitive occupations took a job in the all-other group, and 11 percent of job-finders from all other occupations took a cognitive job. Overall, 14.3 percent changed group." The implied quit rate is "0.219 × 3.84/96.16 = 0.875 percent of employment a month, or 0.105 a year", rounded to 0.11 a year (0.92 percent a month), splitting into "0.63 percent a month for cognitive workers and 1.40 for all other workers". **The task-based model: tasks and instances**

- Output (p. 8): "Output is produced by a constant-returns technology defined over task instances, in the task-based tradition of Zeira (1998), Autor et al. (2003), Acemoglu and Autor (2011), and Acemoglu and Restrepo (2018). Specifically, output is a CES aggregate over task instances," [Equation (1)] "where y_i,t is the output of an instance of task i and the weights ω_i, with sum_i ω_i = 1, are base-period expenditure shares."
- Task and instance (p. 8): "A task is a type of work, such as reviewing a contract or taking a patient's history. An instance is a single performance of a task, for example one contract review."
- Instances as separate goods (p. 8): "A task consists of a great many instances, and the sum in Equation (1) runs over instances, with the instances of a task sharing its weight equally. AI arrives instance by instance: some contracts are reviewed with AI while others are not. The two types of instances are separate entries in the production function, with their own prices and quantities, rather than a single aggregated composite."
- The elasticity (p. 8): "The parameter σ is the elasticity of substitution across instances, of the same task or of different tasks. We refer to it as the elasticity across tasks. In particular, an AI-performed instance and a human-performed instance of the same task are imperfect substitutes with the same elasticity σ."
- Its value (p. 8): "We set σ = 0.5, so that tasks are gross complements, the value assumed by Acemoglu and Restrepo (2022), which traces to the estimate of Humlum (2019) across three broad occupation groups. Estimates range from below one to above one (e.g. based on occupations, as in Burstein, Morales, and Vogel, 2019). Jones and Tonetti (2026) bound the task elasticity from evidence on the capital-labor elasticity of substitution and assume an even lower value."
- Task production and factor assignment (pp. 8–9): "Each task instance can be produced by labor or by capital:" [Equation (2)] "where l_i,t and k_i,t are labor and capital per instance, α_L,i,t and α_K,i,t are task-specific factor productivities, which AI shifts over time, and A_t is the stock of ideas, which augments labor on every task and is taken as given in this section. … Capital and labor are therefore perfect substitutes within a task and, since σ < 1, gross complements across tasks."
- The assignment rule (p. 9): "Markets are competitive, so each task instance is priced at unit cost, and cost minimization assigns it to the cheaper factor," [Equation (3)] "so that capital performs task instance i at date t if and only if α_K,i,t/(A_t α_L,i,t) > r_t/w_t. Ranking instances by capital's comparative advantage α_K,i,t/α_L,i,t, the equilibrium assignment is a threshold rule, as in Acemoglu and Restrepo (2018)."
- Shares and the wage identity (p. 9, Equation (5)): "The wage identity holds exactly at every date, and we use it repeatedly: any two of the wage, output per worker, and the labor share determine the third. We set s_L,t0 = 0.6."
- The mass of a task (p. 9): "In the base period, t0 = 2024, the last year before the use of large language models spread, we choose units so that all unit costs are equal and s_i,t0 = ω_i. We measure the importance of each task by its initial share of total labor payments: m_i ≡ s_i,t0/s_L,t0 = ω_i/s_L,t0. By construction, then, sum_i m_i = 1, where the sum is over the labor-performed tasks."

**The supply of capital**

- (p. 9): "The demand for capital follows from the capital share, K_t = s_K,t P_t Y_t /r_t. Its supply is an upward-sloping schedule in the rental rate: relative to the path the economy would follow without AI, on which the rental rate is constant at r̄, the capital stock is higher by ε percent for every percent by which the rental rate exceeds r̄," [Equation (6)].
- (p. 9): "The schedule is the reduced form of Moll, Rachel, and Restrepo (2022), whose households accumulate savings and hold more wealth in the long run the higher the return. As ε → ∞ the rental rate is pegged at r̄, the case of a small economy in world capital markets, and any amount of capital is forthcoming at an unchanged return. At ε = 0 the capital stock does not respond to AI at all within the horizon. The returns accrue to capital's owners. We report the rental rate as a net return r_t − δ, with r̄ = 0.115 and depreciation of δ = 0.05 a year, so that the net return is 6.5 percent without AI and the capital-output ratio is s_K,t0/r̄ = 3.5."
- Footnote 1 (p. 9), the mapping to Moll et al.: "Moll, Rachel, and Restrepo write their schedule with a semi-elasticity in the net return, Δ ln K_t = η Δ(r_t − δ), and set η = 50. With δ constant, Δ(r_t − δ) ≈ r̄ Δ ln r_t, so the two schedules agree to first order at r̄ when ε = η r̄, which is 50 × 0.115 ≈ 6. They call η = 50 conservative because much of the evidence points to a less elastic supply of capital, and their economy takes decades to reach the steady states it describes. We therefore set ε = 3, half their value. In their units, a rise of one percentage point in the net return then raises the capital stock by about 3/0.115 ≈ 26 percent."

**The economy without AI**

- (p. 10): "In the absence of AI, we assume the economy is on a balanced growth path. The stock of ideas A_t grows at rate g, and the labor force L_t at rate n. The rental rate is constant at r̄, and every expenditure share is then constant. The wage and output per worker therefore grow at g, capital at g + n, and measured TFP at s_L,t0 g. We set g = 1.67 percent a year, so that measured TFP grows at g_A ≡ s_L,t0 g = 1 percent, and we take the labor force to grow at n = 0.33 percent a year, within the range of the Bureau of Labor Statistics projections. GDP without AI therefore grows at g + n = 2 percent a year."

**The two groups of occupations**

- (p. 10): "The labor-performed tasks are sorted into two groups of occupations, which we think of as islands: the cognitive occupations C (management, professional, sales, and office work), whose tasks AI affects, and all other occupations N (manual and interpersonal work, say), whose tasks it does not. We refer to them as the two groups, the cognitive group and the all-other group."
- (p. 10): "Two facts about these groups are consequential for the model. First, AI affects C and not N, so some workers in C have to switch occupations as AI spreads. Second, moving between groups requires search, and that raises unemployment. With the labor force fixed, what AI changes therefore is the division of workers between the two groups and the fraction of workers who are between jobs."
- The occupational definition, footnote 10 (p. 25): "By 2018 SOC major group, the cognitive occupations are Management (11); Business and Financial Operations (13); Computer and Mathematical (15); Architecture and Engineering (17); Life, Physical, and Social Science (19); Community and Social Service (21); Legal (23); Educational Instruction and Library (25); Arts, Design, Entertainment, Sports, and Media (27); Healthcare Practitioners and Technical (29); Sales and Related (41); and Office and Administrative Support (43). All other occupations are Healthcare Support (31); Protective Service (33); Food Preparation and Serving Related (35); Building and Grounds Cleaning and Maintenance (37); Personal Care and Service (39); Farming, Fishing, and Forestry (45); Construction and Extraction (47); Installation, Maintenance, and Repair (49); Production (51); and Transportation and Material Moving (53). The scenario explorer labels the two groups "knowledge workers" and "all other workers.""

**The five scenario objects: capability, adoption, gain, automation, reinstatement**

- (p. 10): "An AI scenario is largely determined by a set of paths for five exogenous objects, which describe AI in the production of goods and services. At t, AI affects a subset A_t of the tasks in the economy, all of them cognitive tasks, and the affected mass is the mass of that set, m_t ≡ sum_{i in A_t} m_i: the fraction of the economy's tasks that AI affects."
- (p. 10): "On each affected task i, the diffusion share d_i,t is the fraction of the task's instances actually performed with AI, and the gain a_i,t is the log productivity gain per AI-performed instance, or equivalently the log decline in the instance's unit cost. In the aggregate, d_t ≡ sum_{i in A_t} m_i d_i,t /m_t is the mass-weighted diffusion share and a_t ≡ sum_{i in A_t} m_i d_i,t a_i,t / sum_{i in A_t} m_i d_i,t the average gain over AI-performed instances, so that sum_i m_i d_i,t a_i,t = m_t d_t a_t identically."
- (pp. 10–11): "All three objects are measured cumulatively from the pre-AI benchmark. The products in which they enter the rest of the paper are read the same way as m_t: m_t d_t is the fraction of the economy's task instances that AI performs, and m_t d_t a_t is that fraction times the gain on each."
- Automation and augmentation (p. 11): "Performing a task instance with AI takes one of two forms. Under augmentation, AI is assumed to raise a worker's labor productivity α_L,i,t on that instance by a_i,t log points. Under automation, an AI system performs the instance outright, so that the instance is performed by capital. In this case we assume that AI raises capital's productivity α_K,i,t to the point where capital, rented at the no-AI rate r̄, supplies the instance at unit cost r̄/α_K,i,t = e^{−a_i,t} w̄_t/(Ā_t α_L,i), that is, a_i,t log points below labor's unit cost without AI, where bars mark the no-AI path. In either form, the unit cost of the instance falls by a_i,t at no-AI factor prices."
- The automation share (p. 11): "The fourth scenario primitive, the automation share ψ_t ∈ [0, 1], is the fraction of AI-performed instances that are automated rather than augmented. The technology determines whether an instance is automated or augmented, and firms take this as given. The gain on an automated instance is available only if capital performs it. The gain on an augmented instance is available only if a worker performs it."
- The reinstatement ratio (p. 11): "The fifth scenario object, the reinstatement ratio ρ ∈ [0, 1], is the mass of newly created labor tasks as a fraction of the mass of automated tasks, in the spirit of Acemoglu and Restrepo (2019) and Autor et al. (2024). That is, a mass ψ_t m_t d_t of existing labor tasks is automated and a mass ρ ψ_t m_t d_t of new labor tasks is created, so the mass of labor tasks changes by −(1−ρ) ψ_t m_t d_t on net. The new tasks belong to the cognitive occupations, whose tasks were automated, and enter at the same unit cost as the remaining labor tasks. Reinstatement therefore offsets displacement one-for-one in the affected wage bill, adds no separate variety or weak-link term to output, and leaves the factor-price frontier unchanged. Labor is mobile across the tasks of a group, so a new task is staffed from the moment it enters."
- The functional forms (p. 11): "The affected mass and the diffusion share follow logistic curves, and the log gain follows a linear path:" [Equation (8): logistic in `m_t` with ceiling `m̄`, slope `κ_m` and midpoint `t_m`; logistic in `d_t` with ceiling `d̄`, slope `κ_d`, midpoint `t_d`; `a_t = a_0 + g_a (t − t0)`] "The automation share ψ_t and the reinstatement ratio ρ are held constant in the three scenarios."
- The slope rule, footnote 11 (p. 26): "The slope that takes the affected mass from its mid-2026 estimate m_2026 to its 2030 value m_2030, three and a half years later, as in equation (8), is" [Equation (8′)] "and likewise for κ_d."

**Measured TFP and the factor-price frontier**

- (pp. 11–12): "Following Acemoglu (2025), a growing literature uses Hulten's theorem (Hulten, 1978) to compute the resulting gain in aggregate productivity as the sum of these cost declines, each weighted by its Domar weight, which in our model is the task's expenditure share ω_i. Dividing the Domar weight ω_i by s_L,t0 gives the mass m_i, so measured TFP rises by" [Equation (9): Δ ln TFP_t ≈ s_L,t0 m_t d_t a_t] "This expression does not involve the automation share since the impact on aggregate productivity depends only on the decline in an instance's unit cost, which is a_i,t whether AI is used in an automated or augmented way."
- (p. 12): "Measured TFP growth equals its dual, the share-weighted growth of the two factor prices." [Equation (10)]
- Measured TFP as the Solow residual (p. 17): "The ideas stock A_t is not what an econometrician would measure as TFP. Measured TFP is the Solow residual, d ln TFP_t ≡ d ln Y_t − s_L,t d ln L_t − s_K,t d ln K_t, and in this model it combines the ideas stock with the level channel of Section 2.1."

**Employment targets**

- (p. 14): "Write l*_o,t for employment in group o when workers move freely between groups at no cost and all workers are paid a common wage. We call this allocation the groups' target, since it is what employment is adjusting toward."
- Key result box (p. 14): "AI does not affect the productivity of tasks of all other occupations, N, so the cost of each depends on the wage, not the automation gain. The demand for labor on those tasks is the ordinary CES demand for a good of price w_t: it rises one for one with output and falls with the wage, with elasticity σ. In other words, as long as AI raises the wage along with output, N's employment rises by less than output. Because labor supply is fixed, the workers N gains are the workers C loses, so C's proportional decline is N's proportional rise scaled by the relative size of the two groups, s_N,t0/s_C,t0."
- The decomposition (p. 15, Equation (19)): "The two terms that determine target employment in the "other" occupations are the same forces that push workers out of cognitive occupations. The first is net displacement: the tasks that automation moves to capital, net of the tasks it creates. The second is (1 − σ) times the gain on the augmented instances."

**The innovation block**

- Ideas production (p. 16): "Ideas are produced from research input R_t, in the tradition of Romer (1990) and Jones (1995):" [Equation (20)] "We set λ ≤ 1 and φ_R < 1: proportional gains become harder to find as the stock of ideas grows."
- Research funding (p. 16): "Research uses final output, as in the lab-equipment model of Rivera-Batiz and Romer (1991). The economy devotes a fraction ι_R,t of GDP to research, funded by a lump-sum tax on households; the government pays the researchers' salaries and their equipment and compute out of that budget."
- The AI channel (p. 16): "AI raises research input because the budget is a share of GDP and AI raises GDP: the level channel of Section 2.1 makes the economy more productive, and a given share of a bigger economy is a bigger research budget. … AI automates research by automating the goods that it uses to produce ideas."
- The long-run elasticity (p. 17, Equation (24)): "Δ ln A* = γ Δ ln R, γ ≡ λ/(1 − φ_R) = 1/2.86 ≈ 0.35, where the value for γ is based on Bloom et al. (2020)."
- What is hard-coded (p. 17): "A simplification in our setup is that there is no feedback from ideas to the gains from AI, a_t. Research raises the productivity of everything people do but does not by itself automate any of it. We instead chose to let a_t and ψ_t be parameters of the scenarios themselves. In that sense, the gains from recursive self-improvement (RSI) can be thought of as being hard-coded into a_t."
- The unit conversion (Appendix C.1, p. 49): "Bloom et al. (2020) measure idea output in units of TFP and research input as R&D spending deflated by the wage of skilled labor, that is, in researcher-equivalents. In those units, they estimate fishing out of 1 − φ = 3.1. Two conversions translate their estimate to the units of Equation (20). Measuring ideas by their effect on labor's productivity rather than on TFP multiplies the exponent by s_L,t0, since ln A_t is 1/s_L,t0 times log TFP on the balanced path. And a unit of output buys fewer researcher-equivalents as the wage grows, so measuring research input in goods adds λ:" [Equation (40): 1 − φ_R = s_L,t0 (1 − φ) + λ = 1.86 + 1 ≈ 2.86].
- The research share path (Appendix C.1, pp. 49–50): "which takes ι_R,t from 3.5 percent of GDP in 2024, the share the national accounts record as research and development, to 4.1 percent in 2030. In researcher-equivalents this is growth of research effort at g_ι + n = 3.1 percent a year, in line with its postwar growth. We assume ι_R,t follows this path whether or not AI arrives."

**The unemployment block**

- Separations (p. 18): "Workers in group o ∈ {C, N} experience two types of separations: quits, and layoffs caused by displacement. They quit at the rate" [Equation (27)] "where q^X_o is an exogenous base rate and the second term makes quits rise with job prospects, captured by the group's job-finding rate f_o,t−1 in the previous month relative to its steady-state value f̄_o."
- Overhang and shortfall (p. 18): "Each month, employers in group o compare their current employment with next month's target. On our scenario paths, the cognitive employment target only falls and the other only rises, so the cognitive occupations carry an employment overhang and the others an employment shortfall, both measured as log gaps," [Equation (28)] "The two sides of the market adjust differently. The expanding occupations post openings for a fraction θ_H of any shortfall per month. The shrinking occupations lay off the workers they do not demand at a slowly-adjusting wage, as discussed next. Unemployment is then the outcome of two frictions, a wage that falls too slowly to keep the displaced employed and a search process that takes time."
- The attached cognitive force (p. 18): "Let N_C,t be the cognitive labor force attached to the group, the employed plus the cognitive-origin unemployed above their normal pool,"[Equation (29)] "and let w^c_C,t be the wage that would clear the cognitive group at N_C,t, i.e. if no extra C workers were pushed into unemployment."
- Wage rigidity (p. 19): "We assume the cognitive wage is slow to adjust, so the cognitive labor market does not clear and there is unemployment. Specifically, the actual cognitive wage paid, w_C,t, closes only a fraction of its gap to w^c_C,t each month, in the manner of Blanchard and Galí (2007):" [Equation (30)] "where ξ ∈ [0, 1) is the rigidity of the cognitive wage per year. At ξ = 0 the wage clears the group every month, and as ξ → 1 it is the common wage. This rigidity occurs relative to the common wage w_t; what is rigid is the cognitive discount."
- Layoffs (p. 19): "Because of the search/matching structure of the model, employment is predetermined within the month, so firms reach their demand through separations. Let l^d_C,t denote the labor firms demand at w_C,t, and let E_t = max{0, l_C,t − l^d_C,t} be the excess employment they carry into the month. Quits from surplus positions are not replaced, and displacements D_C,t ("layoffs") remove the rest:" [Equation (31)].
- Job rationing (p. 19): "The layoffs are job rationing in the sense of Michaillat (2012): while the wage is above its clearing level, firms demand fewer cognitive workers than are attached to the group, and the difference is laid off rather than employed at a lower wage. - Job openings (p. 19): "Firms post openings when they need to replace quits and to close a fraction θ_H of their employment shortfall. … where π̄_o < 1 is the fraction of postings that employers expect to fill within the month, their normal-times filling rate, and v_o,t is a flow of postings per month. At θ_H = 1, the all-other group posts its entire shortfall at once. A θ_H below one spreads the postings over time and stands for the recruiting and training capacity of the expanding occupations."
- The search discount (pp. 19–20): "They search in both groups, but a worker searching outside the group of origin is at a disadvantage, reflecting occupation-specific human capital (Kambourov and Manovskii, 2009), which is otherwise outside the model: a software engineer does not become an electrician or a nurse at once. - The matching function (p. 20): "Hires in group j are given by a matching function combining search effort and openings, of the form proposed by den Haan et al. (2000)," [Equation (34)] "with the standard curvature ι = 1.27 and matching efficiency χ ≤ 1. The function has constant returns to scale, so the filling rate and hires per unit of effective search depend only on the ratio of openings to searchers, the tightness of the group's market, θ_j,t ≡ v_j,t/S_j,t. … Both lie between zero and χ whatever the tightness, so hires never exceed the searchers directed at a group or its openings."
- Normal times (p. 21): "In our normal times the pool is Ū = 3.8 percent of the labor force and the quit rates are q̄_C = 0.63 and q̄_N = 1.40 percent a month, the 0.92 of the economy as a whole split in the proportions of the two groups' separation rates in the Current Population Survey (CPS)."
- The normal-times search discount (p. 21): "The search discount behind these numbers is μ̄ = 0.17, the value at which the share of job-finders in this steady state who change groups equals one in seven, calibrated to this value in the CPS. The steady state is evaluated at μ̄ in every scenario, so these objects are common to the scenarios and Ū is unaffected by AI by construction."
- The two scenario frictions (p. 21): "Two key search parameters capturing how easily cognitive workers can switch occupations and how quickly firms post new vacancies are scenario objects. The modest, substantial, and extreme scenarios set μ = 0.17, 0.08, and 0.04 in Equations (33) and (35) and θ_H = 0.10, 0.25, and 0.50. The modest scenario keeps the normal-times discount and the other two put the displaced cognitive workers at a steeper disadvantage when aiming to switch occupations."

**The calibration, as the paper summarises its own parameters (p. 22, six bullets, in full)**

- "The product of the extent of capabilities, m_t, and the diffusion parameter, d_t, is the share of task instances performed with AI. These parameters always enter as a product."
- "The magnitude through which AI affects task productivity is a_t. The product m_t d_t a_t determines the increase in TFP and therefore the overall rise in GDP that results from AI."
- "The parameter ψ_t is the fraction of AI-performed instances that are automated and shift to capital, while 1 − ψ_t are augmented and remain with cognitive workers."
- "The reinstatement fraction ρ measures the rate at which new tasks arrive and shift work back from capital to cognitive workers. This is another way in which AI may augment cognitive labor."
- "The elasticity of capital supply governs how the productivity gains are split between capital and labor."
- "The rigidity of the cognitive wage governs whether the cost to cognitive workers takes the form of lower wages or of unemployment."
- And the data statement that follows (p. 22): "The labor market in normal times — how large the two groups are, how large the pool is, how the two groups' separation rates compare, and how often a job-finder crosses from one group to the other — is measured from the Current Population Survey. The level of quits is pinned down by the amount of unemployment and the job finding rate so that the steady state matches the U.S. labor market in 2025. The AI objects start from measurements of AI use and adoption in mid-2026, and their values in 2030 are scenario assumptions."
- Table 1's own note (p. 24): "A value spanning the three scenario columns is common to the scenarios. The ceilings of the logistic paths are their natural maxima (m̄ = s_C,t0/s_L,t0, d̄ = 1), and slope and midpoint follow from 2026 anchor, 2030 value, and ceiling by Equation (8′)."

**The Economic Index measures the paper uses (quoted in full)**

Claude is one of several AI assistants in use at work, so the measure is a proxy for AI use generally rather than a census of it. - Its reading and its ceiling (pp. 25–26): "Recall from Section 2.1.2 that the affected tasks must fit inside this group, so the ceiling of the affected mass is m̄ = s_C,t0/s_L,t0 in every scenario: in the long run all cognitive work is within AI's reach, and the scenarios differ in how much of it is within reach by 2030."
- Its value (p. 27): "The affected mass anchor is the observed-exposure measure of Section 3.1. It averages 0.22 in the cognitive group, 0.01 in the other, and 0.14 overall, so m = 0.14. - The productivity gain and Anthropic's own estimate (p. 27): "The productivity gain per instance starts at 0.30–0.45 in logs, reflecting estimates from field trials and frontier firms (Brynjolfsson et al., 2025; Huang et al., 2025), about a quarter of the gain of 1.6 estimated from conversations with Claude (Tamkin and McCrory, 2025). The scenarios also differ in whether that gap closes. In the modest scenario, the productivity gain per task is fixed, whereas in the extreme scenario it reaches 0.80 by 2030."
- Table 1's source lines for the three AI anchors (pp. 23–24): "affected mass, mid-2026 anchor … 0.14 … observed exposure, averaged over occupations (Massenkoff and McCrory, 2026)"; "diffusion share, mid-2026 anchor … 0.10 … firm AI use, Business Trends and Outlook Survey and its 2026 AI supplement (U.S. Census Bureau, 2026; Bonney et al., 2026)"; "log gain per instance, mid-2026 anchor … 0.30 / 0.35 / 0.45 … scenario assumptions: all-in gains in trials and at the frontier (Brynjolfsson et al., 2025; Huang et al., 2025; Demirer et al., 2026)"; "automation share, held constant … 0.50 / 0.75 / 0.90 … scenario assumptions: on the low end similar to current automation-like share of use (Appel et al., 2025)"; "reinstatement ratio … 0.50 / 0.25 / 0 … scenario assumptions: Acemoglu and Restrepo (2019) estimated ρ = 0.5"; "affected mass, 2030 … 0.2 / 0.3 / 0.5 … scenario assumptions: the low end, middle, and near the high end of rated feasibility (Eloundou et al., 2024)".

**The reinstatement anchor and the disruptiveness parameters (§3.4, pp. 27–29)**

- (p. 28): "The reinstatement ratio ρ is the mass of new labor tasks created per unit of tasks automated. In the decomposition of Acemoglu and Restrepo (2019), new tasks roughly kept pace with automated ones over 1947–87 and ran at about half of them over 1987–2017. The modest scenario's 0.5 is thus similar to recent history. The extreme scenario's 0 assumes that AI does not create any new tasks for humans to perform."
- (p. 28): "The search discount μ and the posting speed θ_H govern how quickly displaced workers are re-employed. Neither can be estimated precisely, given that AI has not yet displaced a significant share of workers. The re-employment of displaced workers in past episodes is the closest evidence. Using CPS data on employment–unemployment–employment episodes, we estimate the search discount to be approximately μ = 0.17 and use this value in the modest scenario."
- (p. 28): "The posting speed, θ_H, is an assumption: it closes a tenth, a quarter, and a half of the all-other group's shortfall per month in the three scenarios, respectively. Both frictions matter for the same reason: unemployment rises above its normal level by the number of workers the cognitive occupations have released less the number all other occupations have absorbed, and μ and θ_H set the pace of absorption."
- (pp. 28–29): "The rigidity ξ sets how fast the cognitive wage moves toward its clearing level: the gap between the two, both relative to the common wage, shrinks by the factor ξ a year. … We set ξ = 0.5 (a half-life of one year) in all three scenarios, motivated by the following reasons. The shock is large, visible, and permanent, and rigidity gives way in such episodes. … A stickier wage (higher ξ) means more unemployment and a smaller wage decline; we report ξ = 0.75 and 0.9 in Section 4.6."

**The three scenarios, as named and defined**

- (p. 22): "Our paper considers three main scenarios, ordered by the overall extent to which AI transforms the economy by 2030: modest change, substantial change, and extreme change. The three scenarios differ along two key dimensions. The first is how quickly AI capability and its use advance, captured by the 2030 values of m_t, d_t, and a_t. The second is how disruptive a given amount of AI is for workers, captured by the automation share, the reinstatement ratio, the search discount, and the posting speed. Table 1 lists every number with its source. The three scenarios are illustrative. Our framework allows one to set each scenario object and trace the paths that follow."
- Modest (p. 32): "In the modest change scenario, AI is a small technology. The economy continues on a "normal" path with AI making changes around the margins."
- Substantial (p. 32): "In the substantial change scenario, AI is a pivotal technology, more significant than the internet. It has substantial macroeconomic effects, both on GDP as a whole and on the cognitive occupations."
- Extreme (p. 34): "In the extreme scenario, AI transforms the economy. The macroeconomic consequences go well beyond any event in history, both in terms of magnitudes and in terms of the speed at which change happens."
- The conclusion's restatement (p. 38): "In the modest change scenario, AI acts like a "normal technology" over the next five years and has small macroeconomic effects. In the extreme scenario, AI is transformative: by 2030 GDP is 32 percent above its no-AI path and nearly one in five cognitive workers is unemployed."

**The survey: instrument and coding rules (Appendix B)**

- Sample (p. 45): "Morning Consult fielded the questionnaire to its online panel of US adults in eight daily samples of about 2,000, August 11–23, 2026. Some respondents answered more than once and we kept each respondent's first response. The sample contains 10,980 unique people whose responses are weighted to the adult population on age, gender, education, race, region, and income. Morning Consult screens responses for speed and attention (a minimum timer, an unlikely-event screener, a semantic-association check). "Not sure" answers are excluded item by item, so the number of respondents on each question differs. The questionnaire was developed in partnership with Morning Consult over three pilots in July and August 2026, each with about 2,000 respondents. The respondents were asked about their expectations for either 2027 or 2030. The year was chosen at random and used throughout a respondent's questionnaire. Figure 1 depicts results for both years, but when estimating the implied values of the parameters, only the 2030 data is used, since the scenarios are specified by their 2030 values."
- Question 1, capability (pp. 45–46): "When do you think AI software will be able to do each of the following as well as a skilled professional? Focus on capability — regardless of whether people or companies will actually use AI to do it." with the eight tasks "in the order respondents rank their difficulty": "1. Write routine business emails and documents / 2. Do a small business's monthly bookkeeping / 3. Prepare an accurate personal tax return from someone's documents / 4. Draft and negotiate a routine commercial contract / 5. Build, ship and maintain a working software product / 6. Design, run and write up a scientific study that gets published / 7. Run a small online business end to end profitably, with no human employees / 8. Independently make a scientific discovery important enough to win a Nobel Prize".
- Question 2, adoption (p. 46): "Even when AI is technically capable of performing a task, businesses and people don't always choose to use it that way. Of the tasks AI is capable of performing, what share do you think people will regularly use it for at work in 2030? We offered five bins: less than 10 percent, 10–24, 25–49, 50–74, more than 75 percent."
- Question 3, automation (p. 46): "Now imagine it is 2030 and AI is useful for each of the following tasks. For each task below, do you think AI will have taken over the task from workers, or will workers be doing it with AI's help? Workplaces will differ — answer for the arrangement you expect to be most common in 2030." with four options: "(a) AI does it alone, (b) AI does most of it, with workers checking, (c) workers do most of it, with AI helping, (d) workers do it alone."
- Question 4, productivity gain (p. 46): "From your own experience: think about a task at your work that today's AI is well suited for, like summarizing a document or drafting an email. How long do you think it takes on average to finish the task using AI — counting the time spent checking and fixing the AI's work — compared with not using AI? Six options: (a) it takes longer, (b) about the same time, (c) a little less time, (d) about half the time, (e) about a quarter of the time, (f) about a tenth of the time or less. Respondents who have not used AI at work are asked the same question about "a worker" they have seen or heard of."
- Question 5, re-employment (pp. 46–47): "In normal times, someone who loses a job typically takes about 3 months to find a new one. Now think about a person who loses their job in 2030 due to AI automation and needs to switch into a different occupation. How long do you think it would take them to find a new job? Eight bins: (a) less than 1 month, (b) 1–2 months, (c) 3–4, (d) 5–6, (e) 7–12, (f) 13–24, (g) 2–3 years, (h) more than 3 years, or would never find a new job."
- Capability mapping (p. 47): "To map a respondent's survey response to the parameter, we count the number of the eight tasks they expect AI to do by 2030, as a share of the rows they answered, multiplied by 95 percent to capture that a fraction of knowledge work will continue to be done in person. Finally, we multiply by the knowledge-work share of the wage bill (0.62). For example, seven tasks out of eight corresponds to m_2030 = 7/8 × .95 × .62 = .52. The three scenarios' values of m_2030 correspond to about three, four, and seven tasks performed by AI."
- Adoption mapping (p. 47): "Adults answered in five bins and we code a respondent's answer as the bin's midpoint, so "25–49 percent" is d_2030 = 0.37."
- Automation mapping (p. 47): "We set ψ so it corresponds to the share of the five listed tasks that AI can perform on its own. We code the answers that respondents could select from as follows: "AI does it alone" counts as 1, "AI does most of it, with workers checking" counts as 1/2, and "workers do most of it, with AI helping" counts as 0 automation. A row where the respondent selects "workers do it alone" is a task AI does not perform and is dropped from both numerator and denominator. … Respondents who answer "not sure" on more than two rows are left out."
- Productivity mapping (p. 47): "Each option is read as a time ratio — "about a quarter of the time" is 0.25 — and the gain is the log of the speed-up, a_2030 = ln(1/0.25) = 1.39. "It takes longer" and "about the same time" are both coded as a gain of zero, as productivity losses could be avoided by not using AI."
- Re-employment mapping (p. 49): "The survey tells respondents that in normal times a job search takes about three months and asks how long a worker displaced by AI in 2030 would take to find a job in a new occupation. The answer, in months, is converted to the model's search discount by μ = 0.17 × 3/months. Three months, the normal-times answer, gives μ = 0.17, the value at which the normal-times steady state is solved in every scenario. "More than 3 years, or would never find a new job" is read as never, μ = 0."
- Interpolation (p. 49): "We therefore report interpolated medians and quartiles, which treat respondents as spread evenly within their bin: the cumulative distribution is linear inside each bin, and the quantile is where it reaches one half (one quarter, three quarters). For adoption and re-employment time the bins are taken directly from the questionnaires. For the time ratio and for the automation parameter (ψ), whose codes are single values, a bin reaches halfway to the neighboring code. The count of tasks is not binned and keeps the plain quantile."
- From answers to outcomes (p. 49): "We run the model on each respondent's five values and report the quantiles of each outcome across respondents. This requires all five answers from the same person, so Table 4 uses the 3259 respondents who answered all five items, while the medians of Table 2 use everyone who answered each item. We take those medians item by item, so no single respondent need give all five median answers. Respondents were not asked to forecast the outcomes in Table 4. Those are the model's implications of their answers. For parameters we did not ask about in the surveys — the reinstatement ratio, the posting speed, the wage rigidity, and the elasticity of capital supply — we use the values from the substantial change scenario."

**The simulation (Appendix A)**

- (p. 40): "The model is simulated on a monthly grid. Rates quoted as fractions per period are converted to continuously compounded rates. For example, a quit fraction q̂ enters as q = − ln(1 − q̂). At t0 the AI objects are near the start of their paths, so that the GDP gap they imply is at most a quarter of a percent, and the flow block starts from the steady state (38), evaluated at the normal-times search discount μ̄ in every scenario. The scenario's own μ enters Equations (33) and (35) from t0 on."
- (p. 41): "Table A.1 displays the full model in the form in which it is evaluated: 44 equations in 44 unknowns per month, plus the steady state solved once at the start. Where a variable has both an exact and a first-order form the table gives both, the exact one on the variable's own row and the first-order one, marked ≈, on the row beneath it; the simulation uses the exact equations; the approximations are provided only for intuition."
- (p. 41): "With the exact set, two rows of the table are approximate: measured TFP, which is the base-price index, and the ideas update, which is a monthly step on an exact recursion (on the extreme path it is within 0.02 percentage points of the closed form in 2030). Every other row holds exactly given its right-hand side."
- Exactness convention (p. 11): "Throughout the remainder of this paper, we report exact solutions to our model equations with equality = and first-order approximations with the symbol ≈, which often give better intuition. The tables in Appendix A give the full recipe for solving the model."

## Data and methods

In the wiki author's words, with references.

**What kind of object this is.** A calibrated deterministic simulation model, not an empirical estimate. There is no sample, no estimation, no standard errors and no hypothesis test anywhere in the paper. Every number in Tables 3–6 is a model output at an assumed parameter vector; the only statistical objects in the document are the survey quantiles of Tables 2 and 4, and those are quantiles across respondents' beliefs, not sampling distributions of an estimate.

**Model structure.** Three blocks. (i) A static-within-period task-based production block (§2.1, pp. 8–15): CES over task *instances* with elasticity σ = 0.5; each instance produced by labour or capital, perfect substitutes within an instance; cost minimisation assigns each instance to the cheaper factor; AI lowers the unit cost of an affected, diffused instance by `a_i,t` log points, via labour productivity if augmented and via capital productivity if automated; capital supplied along Δ ln K = ε Δ ln r. (ii) A semi-endogenous ideas block (§2.2, pp. 15–17, Appendix C): research input is a fixed share of GDP, so AI raises research only through the GDP level; fishing out means the long-run elasticity is γ ≈ 0.35 and by 2030 almost nothing has cumulated. (iii) A monthly search-and-matching labour block (§2.3, pp. 18–22): two occupation groups, a slow-moving cognitive relative wage (ξ), layoffs as job rationing, a den Haan–Ramey–Watson matching function with curvature ι = 1.27, a cross-group search discount μ, and a posting speed θ_H. Everything is reported as a gap (Δ) against a no-AI balanced growth path on which GDP grows 2 percent a year.

**Solution.** A monthly recursion, 2024 to 2030, 44 equations in 44 unknowns per month plus a steady state solved once (Table A.1, pp. 42–43). Nine steps per month, listed on pp. 40–41: scenario paths; the rental-rate root by bisection; wage, shares and targets; gaps and the sticky cognitive wage; separations and openings; matching; stock updates; reporting; the ideas step. Two rows are approximations even in the exact set (measured TFP and the ideas update, p. 41). The paper gives both exact and first-order forms and says the simulations use the exact ones.

**Calibration sources, by parameter.**
- σ = 0.5 from Acemoglu and Restrepo (2022), tracing to Humlum (2019); `s_L,t0` = 0.6 as "conventional value for the U.S."; ε = 3 as half the Moll–Rachel–Restrepo (2022) long-run wealth elasticity, argued up because "the capital that performs automated cognitive work is disproportionately compute, financed in a world market and built in a year or two" (p. 26); r̄ = 0.115 and δ = 0.05 from the same source.
- `s_C,t0/s_L,t0` = 0.624 from the 2025 CPS annual averages, defined in Table 1 as the "share of employees in SOC major groups 11–29, 41, 43".
- Ideas: 1 − φ = 3.1 from Bloom et al. (2020), converted to 1 − φ_R = 2.86; λ = 1 ("no duplication"); g = 1.67 percent and g_A = 1 percent from "postwar TFP growth"; n = 0.33 percent; research share 3.5 percent of GDP in 2024 from the national accounts, rising at 2.8 percent a year to 4.1 percent in 2030.
- Labour market in normal times: CPS 2025 annual averages (BLS Tables 11 and 25b) for group employment shares and the pool; IPUMS-CPS matched monthly files 2010–19 (Flood et al., 2025) for separation and finding rates; the Carrillo-Tudela and Visschers (2023a,b) replication files and their correction method for the occupational switching matrix; JOLTS 2001–19 for the quits elasticity (0.53, rounded to 0.55) and JOLTS 2010–19 with the Davis, Faberman and Haltiwanger (2013) daily model for the 0.65 monthly filling rate; ι = 1.27 from den Haan et al. (2000).
- AI objects: `m` = 0.14 in mid-2026 from the observed-exposure measure (Massenkoff and McCrory, 2026); `d` = 0.10 in mid-2026 from the Census BTOS and its 2026 AI supplement; `a` = 0.30–0.45 in mid-2026 from field trials and frontier-firm studies (Brynjolfsson, Li and Raymond, 2025; Huang et al., 2025; Demirer, Musolff and Yang, 2026); ψ from the Economic Index's collaboration classification (Appel et al., 2025); ρ from Acemoglu and Restrepo's (2019) historical decomposition; the 2030 values of `m` from the rated-feasibility range of Eloundou et al. (2024); the 2030 values of `d`, `a`, θ_H and ξ are stated assumptions.

**The Economic Index inputs, precisely.** Three Anthropic measurements enter, all as parameter anchors and none as data the paper analyses:
1. **Observed exposure** (`labor-market-impacts-2026-03`) supplies the single most consequential number in the calibration, the mid-2026 affected mass `m` = 0.14, with the cognitive/all-other split 0.22 / 0.01 that also motivates the two-group structure. The paper reports it as an average over occupations and does not say whether that average is employment- or wage-bill-weighted, though `m_i` is defined as a share of total labour payments.
2. **The Index's collaboration classification** (`economic-index-2025-09-report`, Appel et al. 2025) supplies the automation share: "about half of use on Claude.ai and about three quarters on the API" (pp. 27–28). Those are the September 2025 report's V3 figures (49.1 percent automation on Claude.ai, 77 percent of API transcripts; see `wiki/reports/economic-index-2025-09-report.md`, claims 10, 27 and 76).
3. **The conversation-based productivity estimate** (`productivity-gains-2025-11`) appears only as a benchmark the paper declines to use: the calibrated gain is "about a quarter of the gain of 1.6 estimated from conversations with Claude" (p. 27). Huang et al. (2025) (`work-at-anthropic-2025-12`) is cited alongside field trials as a source for the gain's range, without a number.

No Economic Index release, file or column is opened, and no Index number is reproduced.

**The public survey.** Commissioned from Morning Consult, fielded 11–23 August 2026 in eight daily samples of about 2,000, 10,980 unique respondents after keeping first responses, weighted to the adult population on age, gender, education, race, region and income, with three pilots of about 2,000 in July and August 2026. Five items (capability over eight tasks, adoption, automation over five tasks, productivity gain, re-employment time). Each respondent was randomly assigned the 2027 or the 2030 horizon; only 2030 answers enter the parameter mapping. "Not sure" is dropped item by item. Table 2's medians use everyone who answered each item; Table 4's distribution of implied economies uses the 3,259 respondents who answered all five. The coding rules are Table B.1 (p. 48) and the four mapping paragraphs (pp. 47–49). No microdata, questionnaire file, weights or replication code accompany the paper.

**Code and data availability.** None stated. The paper names no repository, no dataset, no replication package and no licence; the word "replication" appears only in reference to Carrillo-Tudela and Visschers's files. The only public implementation of the model is the scenario explorer (`econ-scenarios-explorer-2026-09`), which the paper describes as letting "anyone set the parameters and see the paths they imply" (p. 38) but does not claim to be identical to the paper's own simulation. The survey data are not released.

## Limitations (verbatim)

The paper has no section headed "Limitations". It has a subsection of the Introduction headed "Caveats" (pp. 4–5), quoted here in full, followed by every other passage in which the paper bounds its own findings.

**The Caveats subsection (pp. 4–5), in full**

- "Like every model, our framework is a stark simplification of a complex reality: it isolates a few key forces and omits many others that may become relevant and important in the coming years. Several caveats should be made explicit. First, our framework omits many potentially relevant forces, for example, catastrophic risks, political economy considerations, business cycles, and possible financial market disruptions. All of these could be important in any of our scenarios."
- "Other omissions may be examined in future extensions to this framework. We do not consider price rigidities and the associated demand effects (beyond the slow adjustment of real wages), so the model cannot generate the negative feedback in which disruption depresses demand and amplifies its own labor-market consequences. We do not assign a value to AI's use in household production, which lies largely outside GDP (Nguyen et al., 2026). Worker heterogeneity is very coarse: workers differ only in employment status and in which of two groups of occupations they belong to; within a group all earn the same wage. A displaced worker who moves to the other group earns that group's wage at once, with no discount for lost tenure or occupation-specific skills (Jacobson, LaLonde, and Sullivan, 1993; Huckfeldt, 2022; Braxton and Taska, 2023). In future versions, it may be desirable to include heterogeneity by tenure and skill, across firms by adoption capacity, across regions by industrial composition, and across demographic groups in mobility and retraining costs, which would permit a richer discussion of inequality."
- "The factor structure is also deliberately simple: labor and a single capital good supplied along an exogenous schedule with fixed elasticity, which does not distinguish compute from other capital and does not explicitly model saving decisions. While we allow AI to improve the productivity of newly automated tasks over time, we do not currently capture productivity growth of tasks already performed by capital before 2024. This omits a potentially important channel for both growth and for sustaining the labor share."
- "We also consider only the effects of AI on cognitive tasks, and do not allow for rapid advances in robotics and their effects on physical tasks in the coming years. Largely for this reason, we do not extend the analysis beyond 2030."

**Caveats stated elsewhere**

- On what the scenarios are (p. 3): "The scenarios are not predictions, and we attach no probabilities to them; their purpose is to make the consequences of different assumptions comparable."
- On the innovation channel (p. 4): "Our model, focusing on changes by 2030, also omits some important forms of feedback between research and automation. As a result, the model cannot generate the singularities of Aghion, Jones, and Jones (2019) or the explosive research-driven acceleration of Davidson, Halperin, Houlden, and Korinek (2026), so the innovation effects here are likely a lower bound."
- On the task-bundle literature (p. 6): "A recent literature emphasizes that occupations are bundles of tasks, and workers differ in their productivity across them (Autor and Thompson, 2025; Freund and Mann, 2026; Althoff and Reichardt, 2026). We do not currently incorporate this richness. These models generate earnings losses for displaced workers and wage gains or losses within an exposed occupation."
- On first-order approximations (p. 12): "Equations (9) and (10) hold to first order, but a benefit of Hulten's theorem is that they do so for any constant-returns technology in an efficient economy. The gains from AI may be large so a first-order approximation may not be accurate enough for the more extreme scenarios we consider. We use exact solutions in our simulations but describe some of the intuition using first-order approximations."
- On gross versus net occupational flows (p. 21): "Historically, gross occupational flows have dwarfed net flows with much of the adjustment mediated by entry to and exit from the labor force (Pilossoph, 2012; Carrillo-Tudela and Visschers, 2023b; Autor et al., 2014; Cortes et al., 2017). Our focus is on scenarios where displaced workers must cross through the unemployed pool."
- On the scenarios' status (p. 22): "The three scenarios are illustrative."
- On the two-way occupational split (p. 25): "Of course, any two-way split is a simplification, since exposure varies widely and many occupations involve some cognitive work."
- On the exposure measure (p. 25): "Claude is one of several AI assistants in use at work, so the measure is a proxy for AI use generally rather than a census of it."
- On the exposure anchor's direction of error (p. 27): "The figure is conservative, since assistive use counts half, thinly used tasks count zero, and the data are from late 2025."
- On the diffusion anchor (p. 27): "Firm shares overstate the share of instances done with AI, because adopters use it on few tasks and on only some instances of each."
- On the diffusion assumption for 2030 (p. 27): "Diffusion reaches 0.2, 0.4, or 0.6, below the recent rapid adoption of frontier LLMs (Bick, Blandin, and Deming, 2024) but also potentially reflecting the fact that historically diffusion has been much slower (Griliches, 1957; David, 1990; Comin and Hobijn, 2010)."
- On the two search frictions (p. 28): "Neither can be estimated precisely, given that AI has not yet displaced a significant share of workers."
- On the posting speed (p. 28): "The posting speed, θ_H, is an assumption".
- On wage rigidity (p. 28): "There is no agreed value of this parameter in the literature, and it may depend on circumstances."
- On the survey's own internal inconsistency (p. 47): "Among workers in cognitive occupations the median answer is 27 percent, against 71 percent from the task grid: respondents expect more of the listed tasks to be within AI's reach than of their own work, a pattern common in surveys about new technology."
- On the return to capital, footnote 13 (p. 35): "The current version of our model possesses an upward-sloping supply of capital but does not feature an Euler equation connecting the return to capital to the growth rate of the economy."
- On distribution (p. 35): "The question of what institutions could lead to shared prosperity in this scenario is beyond our framework."
- On what the model can settle (p. 38): "Today, none of the three can be ruled out."
- On approximation in the simulation (p. 41): "With the exact set, two rows of the table are approximate: measured TFP, which is the base-price index, and the ideas update, which is a monthly step on an exact recursion".

## Open questions, conjectures and promised follow-ups (verbatim)

**Promised follow-ups**

- (p. 5): "Other omissions may be examined in future extensions to this framework."
- (p. 5): "In future versions, it may be desirable to include heterogeneity by tenure and skill, across firms by adoption capacity, across regions by industrial composition, and across demographic groups in mobility and retraining costs, which would permit a richer discussion of inequality."
- Footnote 6 (p. 17), on recursive self-improvement feeding back into the gain: "This does not have to be the case of course, and this channel merits further exploration in the future."
- Footnote 13 (p. 35), on the missing Euler equation: "This connection could raise the return to capital substantially and will be included in a future version of the model."
- (p. 39): "We are developing this framework and the accompanying scenario explorer to inform the debate over how society can best manage the transition to powerful AI."

**Open questions posed**

- (p. 35): "With GDP this much higher, transfers could in principle leave everyone better off. How much redistribution would be required, and what would it need to look like?"
- (p. 39): "The questions the framework sharpens are questions about transitions."
- (p. 39): "But whether and how those resources reach the people who bear the cost is not something growth delivers by itself. The mechanisms through which people could share in the gains of a much richer economy — retraining, income support, universal basic capital or universal basic income, and others not yet designed — then become a central question of economic policy."
- (p. 4): "AI is likely to reshape the U.S. and global economies in profound ways in the coming decade, but how, and by how much, is extraordinarily uncertain." (Introduction's opening, p. 3, sets the question; this sentence is its statement.)
- Conclusion's programme (p. 38): "Each parameter underpinning our scenario explorer is something we can potentially measure: the share of tasks AI can do, the share of firms using it, the productivity gain per task, how long a displaced worker takes to find a new job. The scenarios therefore make statements about data we will observe over the coming years, and those data will tell us which scenario we are in. Today, none of the three can be ruled out. The lasting contribution, we hope, is the framework itself: a common way to state disagreements about the economic effects of AI as disagreements about a few measurable parameters."

**Conjectures and hedged interpretations**

- (p. 4): "One surprising finding of our model is that the growth speedup from AI in innovation is relatively minor, even in the extreme change scenario."
- (p. 4), on the muted macro data: "Two interpretations are consistent with this fact. One is that we are in a modest change scenario and the economic effects will remain small. The other is that we are only at the very beginning of a more extreme scenario that will reshape the role and earnings of labor. The two interpretations have very different policy implications."
- (p. 4): "If instead AI substantially raises output while displacing work, the mechanisms by which people receive resources from an immensely more prosperous economy may themselves need to change. The range of reasonable options, from worker retraining to new forms of insurance and income support, is wide. The framework can represent either case and shows what would have to be true of AI in each."

## What it did not test

The wiki author's inference from the fetched PDF, read in full; not the paper's own statements. Items 1–7 concern places where Anthropic's own Economic Index measurements could have disciplined a parameter and were not used.

1. **The automation share is anchored to one Index wave and then frozen, and the Index's own series contradicts the direction.** ψ enters as a constant per scenario, anchored to "about half of use on Claude.ai" from the September 2025 report (p. 28). That wave is the single wave in which automation exceeded augmentation on Claude.ai; the Index's published series of collaboration-mode shares runs 41 / 42 / 49 / 45 / 44 percent automation across the five report windows to February 2026 (`wiki/reports/economic-index-2026-03-report.md`, claim 8, Fig. 1.3, p. 7). The paper neither uses the later waves nor tests a declining ψ path, though ψ is the parameter that moves the labour share one-for-one in Equation (11). Nothing in the paper acknowledges that the anchor is the peak of a five-point series.
2. **The Index's automation construct and the model's ψ are different objects, and the mapping is asserted.** The model's ψ is the fraction of AI-performed *task instances* performed by capital outright, with the wage bill of the instance moving to capital. The Index's automation share is the fraction of *conversations* classified as directive or feedback-loop. A directive conversation ("translate this paragraph") need not transfer a task's wage bill to capital, and the Index publishes a separate AI-autonomy primitive precisely because delegation and autonomy are distinct. No validation, no crosswalk and no sensitivity to the mapping is reported.
3. **Diffusion is calibrated from firm-adoption surveys, with the adjustment to instance-level diffusion made by assertion.** "Firm shares overstate the share of instances done with AI … We therefore set the initial d = 0.10" (p. 27). No bound is placed on the adjustment, and no use is made of the Index's own task-level usage shares or of the observed-coverage series released with `labor-market-impacts-2026-03`, which measure how concentrated Claude use is across tasks. The 0.10 anchor sets the level of every scenario path; nothing tests it.
4. **Diffusion is assumed uniform across affected tasks in the simulations, although Proposition 1 permits heterogeneity and the Index measures it.** The closed form is written over task-specific `m_i`, `d_i,t`, `a_i,t`, `ψ_i,t`, and the paper then says "With uniform parameters the two sums are …" (p. 15). The observed-exposure data are occupation- and task-level; no run with the empirical distribution of exposure across tasks is reported, so the paper never shows whether concentration of AI use in a minority of tasks changes the labour-share or unemployment path relative to the uniform case.
5. **The affected-mass anchor's weighting is not stated and not tested.** `m_i` is defined as the task's share of total labour payments (p. 9), while the anchor is "observed exposure, averaged over occupations" (Table 1, p. 23) reported as 0.22 / 0.01 / 0.14. Cognitive occupations are paid above average, so an employment-weighted average and a wage-bill-weighted average differ. The paper does not say which it used, nor how much `m` = 0.14 would move under the other.
6. **The cognitive group's wage-bill share is set equal to its employment share.** `s_C,t0/s_L,t0` is defined as a base-period wage-bill share (Table A.2 panel B, p. 44) and calibrated at 0.624, "share of employees in SOC major groups 11–29, 41, 43" (Table 1, p. 23). Since the cognitive occupations are the higher-paid ones, this understates their wage-bill share and therefore `s_N/s_C`, the factor by which Equation (13) converts the all-other group's employment gain into the cognitive group's loss. No alternative is reported.
7. **The gap with Anthropic's own productivity estimate is noted and then left.** The calibrated per-instance gain is "about a quarter of the gain of 1.6 estimated from conversations with Claude" (p. 27). A factor-of-four disagreement between the paper's central parameter and the Index programme's own published measure is neither reconciled nor bounded: no scenario is run at a = 1.6, and `a` has no sensitivity table, although `m d a` is stated to determine the whole TFP and GDP gain (p. 22).
8. **Only two parameters get a sensitivity table.** Tables 5 and 6 vary ε and ξ. σ, ρ, ψ, θ_H, μ, γ, λ, φ_R, n and g are never varied one at a time, and the three scenarios move six parameters together, so the paper never reports which parameter the results are most sensitive to. σ in particular is acknowledged to be contested ("Estimates range from below one to above one", p. 8) and appears in three of the four first-order solutions.
9. **No uncertainty of any kind is attached to a scenario outcome.** No confidence intervals, no probability weights ("we attach no probabilities to them", p. 3), no Monte Carlo over parameter draws, no partial-identification bounds. The only dispersion in the paper — Table 4's quartiles — is dispersion of beliefs among survey respondents, run through the model one respondent at a time.
10. **The model is never back-tested.** It is anchored at mid-2026 by construction and the three paths coincide there (p. 27). The paper does not run it over 2024–2026 to check that it reproduces the observed labour share, cognitive employment, unemployment or GDP, even though it states as a motivating fact that "the labor market effects of AI have been relatively muted" (p. 4). There is therefore no evidence that the frictional block generates the right magnitude of adjustment at observed levels of AI use.
11. **The survey mapping rules are not varied.** The 95-percent in-person ceiling, the 0.62 knowledge-work multiplier, the bin midpoints, the coding of "AI does most of it, with workers checking" as ½, and the coding of "it takes longer" as zero gain all shape Table 2's medians. No alternative coding is reported, and the paper's own finding that respondents rate the listed tasks far more automatable than their own work (27 percent against 71 percent, p. 47) is noted but never used to adjust or bound the capability mapping.
12. **The survey measures beliefs, and the paper does not test whether those beliefs track anything.** No comparison with expert forecasts, prediction markets, the firm surveys used for `d`, or respondents' own AI use; no question validation; no test for anchoring on the "three months" prompt in the re-employment item, which mechanically sets the μ scale.
13. **Labour supply never responds.** The labour force grows at `n` "with or without AI" (p. 10), so participation, retirement, discouragement and hours are all outside the model, and the unemployment rates in Table 3 are therefore not comparable to published rates in which participation moves. Entry and exit are named as the main historical channel of occupational adjustment (p. 21) and then excluded by assumption, with no variant reported.
14. **Reallocation is two-way and frictionless within each group.** Workers inside the cognitive group move between tasks costlessly; the only friction is crossing to the all-other group. No within-cognitive reallocation cost, no retraining cost, no wage scarring on re-employment (the paper cites the displaced-worker literature in the caveats and then sets losses at zero), and no capacity constraint on the receiving occupations beyond θ_H.
15. **The data-centre buildout is absent.** The scenarios raise the capital stock by up to 56 percent above the no-AI path with no investment dynamics, no financing constraint, no construction lag and no aggregate-demand consequence; the caveats mention "possible financial market disruptions" but the paper nowhere discusses the current AI capex cycle, which is the main channel by which AI is visible in 2026 macro data.
16. **Prices, monetary policy and the business cycle are absent** (stated in the caveats for price rigidities and business cycles), so the unemployment paths are pure reallocation unemployment. No variant with a demand channel is run, even at the level of an illustration.
17. **Geography and the rest of the world are absent.** The model is one closed US economy with a world capital market implied only by ε. The Index publishes usage by country and US state; the paper's own affected-mass measure is US-occupation-based; no regional variant, no trade, no offshoring channel and no international comparison is attempted, although the introduction motivates the paper with "the U.S. and global economies" (p. 3).
18. **The explorer is not validated against the paper.** The paper implements the model and also points readers to an interactive implementation (p. 38) without stating that the two solve the same equations, without a version number for the explorer's model, and without reporting any check that the explorer reproduces Tables 3–6. The two documents' published survey-implied GDP figures differ (see `## Verification`).
19. **Nothing is reproducible.** No code, no parameter file, no survey microdata, no output series behind Figures 2–4. A reader cannot recompute any number in the paper, and the only route to the model — the explorer — is a black box on a web page.
