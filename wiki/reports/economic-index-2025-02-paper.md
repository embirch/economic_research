## Definitions (verbatim)

Quotations only; no paraphrase. Page references in brackets.

**Clio and facets**
- "To understand how AI systems are being used for different economic tasks, we leverage Clio [Tamkin et al., 2024], an analysis tool that uses Claude [Anthropic, 2024] to provide aggregated insights from millions of human-model conversations." [p.4]
- "To conduct our analysis, we use Clio [Tamkin et al., 2024], a privacy-preserving framework for analyzing language model conversations." [p.17]
- "Clio uses facets to extract specific attributes or characteristics from conversations. Facets can capture both direct questions like "What task is the assistant performing?" as well as metadata such as the AI model used to fulfill a request." [p.17]
- "we measure real-world **usage patterns** using Clio [Tamkin et al., 2024], a recent system that enables privacy-perserving [sic] analysis of millions of human-AI conversations on a major model provider." [p.4]

**The sample and what it excludes**
- "Using Clio on a dataset of one million Claude.ai Free and Pro conversations, we analyzed each interaction to map it to its most relevant task category in the O\*NET database." [p.4]
- "Because we focus on studying patterns in individual usage, the results shared in this paper exclude activity from business customers (i.e., Team, Enterprise, and all API customers)." [p.4, footnote 4]
- "All analyses draw from conversation data collected during December 2024 and January 2025." [p.4]

**Task mapping**
- "Our approach consists of three main components: 1) creating a hierarchical taxonomy of the O\*NET occupational tasks, 2) mapping from summarized, privacy-preserved, conversations to O\*NET tasks with Clio, 3) connecting O\*NET tasks to occupations." [p.17]
- "Because there are nearly 20,000 unique task statements in O\*NET, we create a hierarchical tree of tasks using Clio, and perform the assignment by traversing the tree." [p.4]
- "However, a key challenge is the size of the O\*NET task database we use (∼ 20K tasks), which makes direct classification via zero or few-shot prompting impossible because the full list of tasks does not fit in the model's context window. We instead construct this as a classification over a hierarchy of task labels (Figure 9), inspired by Morin and Bengio [2005], Mnih and Hinton [2008]." [p.17]
- "To map conversations to specific tasks, we use this generated hierarchy to perform a tree-based search through our task hierarchy. For each conversation, we first used Claude to determine if the conversation was occupationally relevant. We screen conversations using Claude 3.5 Haiku (claude-3-5-haiku-20241022). The prompt we use for screening is provided in Appendix F. If the conversation is deemed relevant, we traverse the hierarchy from top to bottom, with Claude selecting the most appropriate task at each level based on the conversation content. Through this process, Clio calculates the cumulative number of conversations assigned to each task in the O\*NET database." [p.18]
- "Our hierarchy contains three levels: 12 top-level tasks, 474 middle-level tasks, and 19530 base-level (O\*NET) tasks." [Figure 9 caption, p.18]
- Hierarchy construction steps, verbatim [pp.17–18]: "**Embed task names.** Embeds tasks names using the all-mpnet-base-v2 [Reimers and Gurevych, 2022] sentence transformer to obtain 768-dimensional vector representations of each task"; "**Generate neighborhoods.** Group these embeddings into k neighborhoods using k-means clustering, where k is chosen so that the average number of tasks per neighborhood is 40."; "**Propose new tasks for each neighborhood.** For each neighborhood, use Claude to propose candidate higher-level task descriptions by examining both the tasks within the neighborhood and the nearest m tasks outside it."; "We require the final number of tasks at the level l to be nl ± 1.5nl, where nl is chosen such that the ratio between successive levels follows nl/nl−1 = (ntop/nbase)^1/(L−1) for L total levels."; "**Deduplicate across neighborhoods.** Deduplicate and refine the proposed tasks across all neighborhoods using Claude to ensure distinctiveness while maintaining coverage of the underlying data distribution."; "**Assign to new best fit higher-level tasks.** Assign each lower-level task to its most appropriate parent task using Claude. We randomly shuffle the order of the higher-level tasks when sampling from Claude to avoid biasing assignments based on the order of the list."; "**Rename higher-level tasks.** Once all tasks at level l have been assigned to a higher-level task, we regenerate a new name and description for the parent task based on the lower-level tasks that were assigned to it."
- "The following details are reproduced from Appendix Section G.7 of Tamkin et al. [2024]." with footnote: "The key difference here is that rather than generating clusters from conversations, we are generating clusters from O\*NET tasks." [p.17]

**Occupation and occupational category**
- "Each task in O\*NET is associated with one or more occupations. To conduct our occupation-level analysis, we map from individual tasks to their associated occupations. To calculate occupational use, we aggregate the number of conversations associated with tasks for a given occupation. In the case that a single task maps to multiple occupations, each occupation's conversation count is incremented by the number of conversations assigned to that task divided by the number of occupations associated with that task." [p.18]
- "To understand broader patterns, we group these tasks according to O\*NET's occupational framework—first mapping them to specific occupations (such as Computer Network Architects) and then to broader occupational categories (like Computer and Mathematical Occupations)." [p.5]
- "It is worth noting that the occupational classification of a conversation does not necessarily mean the user was a professional in that field. For instance, while some nutrition-related queries might come from dietitians, others likely come from individuals seeking personal dietary advice." [p.5]
- "U.S. representation is computed by the fraction of workers in each high-level category according to the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics [U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, 2024]." [Figure 3 caption, p.6]

**Task usage (the depth measure)**
- "Task usage is defined as occurrence across five or more unique user accounts and fifteen or more conversations." [Figure 4 caption, p.7]
- "Cumulative distribution showing what fraction of occupations (y-axis) have at least a given fraction of their tasks with AI usage (x-axis)." [Figure 4 caption, p.7]
- "To assess the depth of AI integration across occupations, we examined what fraction of each occupation's tasks appeared in our Clio run." [p.7]

**Privacy thresholds**
- "Clio operates with a set of privacy measures that protect the activity of individual users from appearing in Clio outputs. In this work, we only examine tasks that have accumulated at least 15 separate conversations spread across a minimum of five different user accounts—ensuring that a task must appear multiple times across different users to appear in our dataset." [p.7, footnote 5]
- "For privacy reasons, tasks with less than 5 unique accounts or 15 conversations are excluded from our analysis. This also serves to reduce statistical noise." [p.18]
- "Anthropic enforces strict internal privacy controls. Our privacy policy enables us to analyze aggregated and anonymized user conversations to understand patterns or trends. We continue to manage data according to our privacy and retention policies, and maintain our approach of not training our generative models on user conversations by default." [p.4, footnote 4]
- "In our primary analyses above, we used Clio to classify conversations into their best-fit O\*NET tasks; these tasks were treated as clusters in Clio to maintain our privacy assurances (importantly, aggregation thresholds and removal of personally identifiable information.)." [p.27]

**Automation and augmentation**
- "A key distinction in the economics literature is between automation—where technology substitutes for human labor—and augmentation—where technology complements and enhances human capabilities [Autor, 2015]." [p.9]
- "To understand which pattern is more prevalent, we used Clio to classify conversations into one of five different collaboration patterns grouped into automative vs. augmentative behaviors, listed in Table 1." [p.9]
- "**Automative Behaviors** / AI directly executes tasks with minimal human involvement" [Table 1, p.10]
- "**Augmentative Behaviors** / AI enhances human capabilities through collaboration" [Table 1, p.10]
- "Table 1: **Taxonomy of Human-AI Collaboration Patterns.** We classify conversations into five distinct patterns across two broad categories based on how people integrate AI into their workflow." [p.10]
- "automative modes include Directive and Feedback Loop, while augmentative modes are comprised of Task Iteration, Learning, and Validation." [Figure 7 caption, p.10]
- "57% of usage suggests augmentation of human capabilities (e.g., learning or iterating on an output) while 43% suggests automation (e.g., fulfilling a request with minimal human involvement)." [abstract, p.1]

**The five collaboration patterns** (Table 1, p.10; the illustrative examples are the paper's own)
- "**Directive:** Complete task delegation with minimal interaction — *Illustrative Example:* "Format this technical documentation in Markdown"" [p.10]
- "**Feedback Loop:** Task completion guided by environmental feedback — *Illustrative Example:* "Here's my Python script for data analysis – it's giving an IndexError. Can you help fix it? … Now I'm getting a different error…"" [p.10]
- "**Task Iteration:** Collaborative refinement process — *Illustrative Example:* "Let's draft a marketing strategy for our new product. … Good start, but can we add some concrete metrics?"" [p.10]
- "**Learning:** Knowledge acquisition and understanding — *Illustrative Example:* "Can you explain how neural networks work?"" [p.10]
- "**Validation:** Work verification and improvement — *Illustrative Example:* "I've written this SQL query to find duplicate customer records. Can you check if my logic is correct and suggest any improvements?"" [p.10]
- The classifier's own definitions, from the prompt [Appendix F.3, p.25]: "- Directive - Human delegates complete task execution to AI with minimal interaction"; "- Feedback Loop - Human and AI engage in iterative dialogue to complete task with human mainly providing feedback from the environment"; "- Task Iteration - Human and AI engage in iterative dialogue to complete a task with the human refining the AI outputs"; "- Learning - Human seeks understanding and explanation rather than direct task completion"; "- Validation - Human uses AI to check or validate their own work"
- "If you are unsure or there is not enough context to determine the most representative pattern, return 'None' as your answer. Use 'None' liberally---for only some conversations will this task be possible." [Appendix F.3, p.26]
- "To understand the nature of users' interactions with Claude, we construct a Clio facet to identify the collaboration pattern present in each conversation. … We intersect this facet with the previously-mentioned Clio facet mapping from conversations to O\*NET tasks. Thus, for each O\*NET task, Clio provides an associated breakdown across collaboration patterns." [Appendix B.5, p.20]

**Occupational skills**
- "The O\*NET database contains 35 occupational skills that identify the essential abilities workers need in order to perform tasks across different jobs. These include skills such as Critical Thinking, Complex Problem Solving, Persuasion, and Equipment Maintenance." [p.7]
- "Similar to our analyses on Automating vs. Augmenting Users and Usage by Model Type, we create a Clio facet which assigns conversations to the most relevant occupational skills exhibited by the model. Each conversation is assigned to all skills exhibited by the assistant within the conversation. Assignments to multiple skills are allowed. There is also an option to return None if none of the skills apply to the conversation. In our analyses, we filtered out these classifications as only 0.1% of conversations were labeled None. For each conversation, the order of skills are shuffled before querying for an assignment to avoid any biases due to ordering." [Appendix B.4, p.20]

Your job is to identify ALL skills exhibited by the assistant in the following human-AI assistant conversation. You can select multiple skills if appropriate. Select all that apply."

**Wage**
- "Occupations in O\*NET contain associated data on the median wage of workers in that occupation. To analyze occupational usage of AI relative to occupations' wage, we mapped each occupation's conversation count to the median salary of that occupation." [Appendix B.3, p.19]
- "This information is included on the O\*NET website but at the time of writing this paper, we did not find it readily available in a downloadable format. **Job Zone (barrier to entry)**
- "Occupations in the O\*NET database are placed in Job Zones ranging from one to five, categories defined by the amount of preparation needed for a human to perform the duties of a given occupation. Occupations requiring higher levels of education, experience, and training are placed in higher Job Zones." [p.8]
- "Occupations in O\*NET are placed into one of five Job Zones which describe the amount of preparation a person would need for a given occupation. O\*NET delineates the Job Zones as: / Job Zone 1 - occupations that need little or no preparation / Job Zone 2 - occupations that need some preparation / Job Zone 3 - occupations that need medium preparation / Job Zone 4 - occupations that need considerable preparation / Job Zone 5 - occupations that need extensive preparation" [pp.19–20]

## Data and methods

In the wiki author's words, with page references.

**Platform and product.** Claude.ai Free and Pro conversations only. Team, Enterprise and all API traffic are excluded (p.4, footnote 4). Claude.ai in this period produced text output only (a limitation the authors name, p.12). Two models are named: Claude 3 Opus (released March 2024) and Claude 3.5 Sonnet (new) (released October 2024) (p.11), in a mix of about 46% Opus / 54% Sonnet (p.20).

**Sample windows and sizes** (Appendix E, p.24 — the paper's only statement of exact windows):

| Analysis | Conversations | Time span |
|---|---|---|
| Usage Across Tasks and Occupations (§3.1) | 1M | 2024-12-16 to 2024-12-23 |
| Usage by Wage and Barrier to Entry (§3.3) | 1M | 2024-12-16 to 2024-12-23 |
| Usage by Skills (§3.2) | 500K | 2025-01-10 to 2025-01-17 |
| Automating vs. Augmenting Users (§3.4) | 1M | 2024-12-16 to 2024-12-23 |
| Usage by Model Type (§3.5) | 1M | 2024-12-15 to 2025-01-04 |

Appendix G's cluster-reconstruction run uses a separate random sample of about 2.8M conversations, 2024-11-28 to 2024-12-18, with abusive/harmful conversations excluded and no occupational-relevance screen (p.27). *Wiki author's note:* the abstract's "over four million" is the sum of the rows above (4.5M analysis-runs) across three overlapping windows, not a count of distinct conversations; §3.1, §3.3 and §3.4 appear to run on the same 1M seven-day sample. The paper never states how many distinct conversations underlie the paper.

**Classification pipeline** (§3.1, Appendix B.1, pp.4, 17–18).
1. A screening step asks Claude 3.5 Haiku (`claude-3-5-haiku-20241022`) whether a conversation "possibly involve[s] an occupational task"; irrelevant conversations are dropped (p.18; prompt at p.24).
2. O\*NET's ~20,000 task statements are organised by Clio's hierarchy-generation step into a three-level tree: 12 top-level, 474 middle-level, 19,530 base-level O\*NET tasks (Figure 9, p.18). Construction: embed task names with `all-mpnet-base-v2` (768-dim), k-means into neighbourhoods averaging 40 tasks, Claude proposes parent labels using the neighbourhood plus its nearest outside tasks, deduplicate with Claude, assign children to parents with shuffled option order, then regenerate parent names from their children (pp.17–18).
3. Each screened conversation is assigned by top-down tree traversal, Claude choosing one option per level; the assignment prompt forces a single "single-most pertinent" task (p.18; prompt at p.25). A multi-label variant with k=3 was run and reported as qualitatively similar (p.18).
4. Tasks are mapped to O\*NET occupations; where a task belongs to several occupations, the conversation count is divided equally among them (p.18). Occupations are then grouped into O\*NET high-level categories (p.5).

**Other facets.** Occupational skills: a multi-label facet over O\*NET's 35 skills, asking which skills the *assistant* exhibited, with shuffled option order and a 'None' option (0.1% of conversations) (p.20; prompt pp.26–27). Collaboration pattern: a single-label facet over the five patterns with a liberal 'None', intersected with the task facet so each task carries a pattern breakdown (p.20; prompt pp.25–26). Dataset composition: a facet scoring each conversation on work, coursework and personal-use dimensions with four confidence levels each (p.20).

**Thresholds.** A task enters the analysis only with at least 15 conversations across at least 5 unique accounts (p.7, footnote 5; p.18). The authors describe this as a privacy control that also reduces statistical noise (p.18). The same threshold defines "task usage" in the depth measure (Figure 4 caption, p.7) and is applied to the Appendix G cluster run (p.28).

**Wage and Job Zone data.** Occupation median wages come not from an O\*NET download but from a third-party scrape of the O\*NET website, Kilbourne-Quirk (2019) (p.19, footnote 10). Figure 6 marks the U.S. median wage at $60,070, sourced to US Census Bureau (2022) (p.9 and caption). Job Zones are O\*NET's five preparation categories; Table 2 compares each zone's share of usage with its share of occupations (pp.19–20, 22, 24).

**Validation (Appendix B and C).** Three kinds. (i) Human validation on 150 examples each for the task-hierarchy path (95.3% / 91.3% / 86% acceptable at top / middle / base level) and for the collaboration-pattern label (90.7% optimal) (p.21) — drawn from conversations where users submitted feedback, which the authors flag as a non-representative distribution. (ii) Internal robustness: conversation counts against unique-account counts, "remarkably stable" (p.19, Figure 10); k=3 multi-label variant (p.18); dataset composition showing 23% non-work and 5–10% coursework after screening (p.21). (iii) Cluster reconstruction (Appendix G): re-running the pipeline on Clio clusters at nine aggregation levels and correlating with direct assignment — strong at category level (≥0.95 Pearson), moderate at occupation level (≥0.70 Pearson), weak at task level (≥0.47 Pearson) (pp.28–30).

**Statistical methods.** Descriptive shares and rank orderings throughout; a representation ratio (usage share / baseline share) for Job Zones (p.22); Pearson, Spearman, Kendall's Tau-b correlations and mean squared error for the cluster-reconstruction comparison (pp.28–35). No regression, no standard errors, no confidence intervals and no significance tests are reported anywhere in the paper; percentage-point differences between models (Figure 8, p.11) are reported without uncertainty.

**Released data and code.** "We provide relevant data at https://huggingface.co/datasets/Anthropic/EconomicIndex/" (p.2, footnote 2). The paper states no code release and gives no repository; the prompts in Appendix F (pp.24–27) are the reproducible artefact for the classifiers. O\*NET is cited under CC BY 4.0 (p.15, references).

## Limitations (verbatim)

All from §4.1 (p.12) unless another page is given.

1. **Data sample.** "We use snapshots of Claude.ai Free and Pro conversations over 7-day periods. It is possible that this sample is not representative of usage on Claude.ai across longer time windows, and quite likely that our sample differs in important ways from API data or data from other AI model providers due to differing model capabilities, product features, and user bases. Additionally, Claude.ai only outputs text as opposed to other modalities. This removes key potential users who may rely on image or video outputs (e.g. fashion designers). For these reasons our findings should be interpreted as an imperfect snapshot of AI usage across the labor market, while noting that a broader understanding of model interaction patterns will emerge as more researchers and organizations are able to share usage data from diverse deployment contexts." [p.12]
2. **Reliability of model-driven classification.** "Our use of Claude to classify user conversations may also introduce potential inconsistencies if the model's understanding of tasks differs from the intended reading in the O\*NET database. While we conduct human validation of our pipeline (Appendix C), rely upon past validations of Clio [Tamkin et al., 2024], and corroborate our results with cluster-level analyses (Appendix G), it is important to note that these classifications likely contain some inherent noise." [p.12]
3. **Varying complexity of users' queries.** "While we make efforts to exclude conversations without relevance to any occupational tasks (Appendix B), our method does not account for the complexity of user queries—for example, providing instructions for a basic omelette does not indicate culinary expertise. Consequently, we may overestimate usage rates for certain tasks by classifying conversations from novice users." [p.12]
4. **Limitations of the O\*NET database.** "While the O\*NET database offers valuable insights into current economic sectors, its static nature presents key limitations for analyzing AI's impact on the labor market. The database cannot capture emerging tasks and occupations that AI systems such as Claude may create or transform. Additionally, while O\*NET captures a very large number of tasks, it cannot contain all tasks in the economy. Furthermore, these tasks are often written in general terms, leading to inherent ambiguity when classifying conversations—many tasks are similar across multiple different occupations. Finally, as a U.S.-centric classification system, O\*NET may overlook significant occupational categories and tasks from other regions, potentially skewing our distributional analysis of global Claude.ai usage. This limits our analysis as AI usage patterns can vary across international contexts Gmyrek et al. [2023]." [p.12]
5. **Lack of full context into user workflows.** "Although our work analyzes conversation data on Claude.ai, our methods are not able to capture how users are using the outputs of Claude.ai conversations. For example, we cannot observe whether users are copying code snippets into development environments, incorporating writing suggestions into documents, fact-checking responses against other sources, or using outputs as inspiration rather than verbatim content. Thus, it remains out of reach to make definitive judgments as to how much Claude's outputs are actually incorporated by users in their tasks." [p.12]
6. Named in the introduction: "we acknowledge multiple key limitations (discussed in Section 4.1); for example, our usage data cannot reveal how Claude's outputs are actually used in practice, and our reliance on O\*NET's static occupational descriptions means we cannot account for entirely new tasks or jobs that AI might create." [p.3]
7. On inferring impact: "While our methods are imperfect, they provide a systematic way to track usage patterns and identify leading indicators of economic effects across different sectors." [p.3]
8. On who the user is: "It is worth noting that the occupational classification of a conversation does not necessarily mean the user was a professional in that field. For instance, while some nutrition-related queries might come from dietitians, others likely come from individuals seeking personal dietary advice. This widespread access to AI assistance for traditionally professional tasks—even if the assistance is not perfect—could have significant implications for these fields, though analyzing such impacts lies outside the scope of this study." [p.5]
9. On multiple valid task labels: "Although there are often multiple valid tasks that a single conversation could be mapped to, we observed qualitatively very similar results when mapping a single conversation to multiple tasks." [pp.4–5]
10. On the skills measure: "However, our analysis captures only whether a skill was exhibited in Claude's responses, not whether that skill was central to the user's purpose or was performed at an expert level. For instance, while Active Listening appears as the second most common skill, this likely reflects Claude's default conversational behaviors—such as rephrasing user inputs and asking clarifying questions—rather than users specifically seeking out listening-focused interactions." [p.8]
11. On the augmentation share: "That said, we note an important caveat that users might edit and adjust the response they receive from Claude outside the chat window, suggesting that the true proportion of augmentative conversations may be even higher. In addition, even automation of simple tasks can serve to enhance human capabilities when embedded within broader human-directed workflows." [p.9]
12. On the validation sample: "While we acknowledge the distribution of conversations for which users submitted feedback likely differs from regular Claude.ai usage, we consider this distribution to be more reliable than alternative open-source datasets." [p.21]
13. On classification accuracy falling with specificity: "As tasks become more specific, classification becomes more difficult. Thus, accuracy of our method predictably decreases as we traverse the hierarchy. Furthermore, there is likely some irreducible noise in the assignment process, given that the O\*NET database, while extensive, does not encompass all possible tasks in the economy." [p.21]
14. On task-level reconstruction: "The relatively weak correlation at the task level can be attributed to two key factors. First, there is intrinsic noise in the O\*NET task dataset itself, with many tasks being highly similar or overlapping. … Second, there exists a many-to-many relationship between conversations and tasks (a single conversation might involve multiple tasks) and between tasks and occupations (a single task might map to multiple occupations). This means that uncertainty expands at the task level but naturally narrows as we aggregate upward to occupational categories". [p.33]
15. On what cluster-based analysis supports: "This pattern suggests that while cluster-based analysis may not perfectly reconstruct task-level assignments, it remains reliable for analyzing broader occupational trends." [p.33]
16. On task coverage: "Notably, only a small fraction (<20%) of the ∼20K O\*NET tasks are recovered." [Figure 25 caption, p.36]
17. On inferring long-run consequences: "While our data reveals where AI is being used today, inferring long-term consequences from these early usage trends poses significant empirical challenges [Acemoglu et al., 2022]. … While our present results cannot definitively map these relationships". [p.13]
18. On the platform: "While our data and methods face important limitations and only paint a picture of AI usage on a single platform". [abstract, p.1]

## What it did not test

**Wiki author's inference — not the paper's own words.** Adjacent questions for which the paper visibly had the data, and constructs it used without validating.

*Cuts the pipeline could have produced but the paper does not report*
1. **Automation share by wage or Job Zone.** Appendix B.5 (p.20) says the collaboration-pattern facet was intersected with the task facet, "for each O\*NET task"; the wage and Job Zone attributes are attached to occupations (pp.19–20). A table of augmentation share by wage quartile or Job Zone was therefore one join away and is never shown. The paper asserts "most occupations exhibited a mix of automation and augmentation across tasks" (p.3) and "this ratio varies across occupations" (p.3) without a single number for that variation.
2. **Automation share by occupational category.** Same join; §3.4 (pp.9–10) reports only qualitative task examples per pattern.
3. **Collaboration patterns by model.** Model metadata is a Clio facet (p.17) and the model split is known (54/46, p.20), but the paper never asks whether Sonnet conversations are more automative than Opus conversations — the obvious test of whether capability gains shift the delegation/collaboration mix.
4. **Free versus Pro.** Every sample is described as "Claude.ai Free and Pro" (pp.4, 27) and the tier is a product attribute, yet no result is split by tier; nothing is said about whether the paying population's task mix differs.
5. **Time stability inside the paper's own data.** §3.1/§3.3/§3.4 use 2024-12-16 to 2024-12-23 and §3.2 uses 2025-01-10 to 2025-01-17 (p.24); the model analysis spans 2024-12-15 to 2025-01-04. Running the task facet on the January window and comparing would have tested the "7-day snapshot" limitation the paper states (p.12). It is not done; the two windows are never compared.
6. **Geography.** The paper argues O\*NET is "U.S.-centric" and that this may skew "our distributional analysis of global Claude.ai usage" (p.12), which presumes non-US traffic in the sample, but no country or region share is reported, and no comparison of task mix between US and non-US conversations is attempted.
7. **The work / coursework / personal cut as a result.** Appendix B.7 (pp.20–21) ran a three-dimensional composition facet, but reports only two aggregate numbers (23% non-work; 5–10% coursework) and anecdotes. The occupational distribution conditional on "explicitly work" is never shown, so the paper cannot say how much of the 37.2% Computer & Mathematical share (p.6) is work rather than study or hobby — which is exactly the interpretive question the headline invites.
8. **Threshold sensitivity of the depth curve.** "Task usage" is defined by 5 accounts and 15 conversations (p.7). The Figure 4 depth statistics (∼4% / ∼11% / ∼36%, and the ∼57% at the 10% threshold on p.13) are mechanically sensitive to that cut and to how many tasks O\*NET lists for an occupation, yet no sensitivity analysis over the threshold is reported and the correlation between an occupation's task count and its measured depth is never examined.
9. **Any uncertainty quantification.** The samples are large random draws (1M, 500K, 2.8M), so sampling error is computable for every share, and the 150-example validations have wide binomial intervals. No interval, standard error or test appears in the paper, including for the Figure 8 model differences and the Table 2 representation ratios.
10. **A wage analogue of Table 2.** Job Zones get a representation ratio against a baseline (p.24); wages get a scatter with no aggregation (p.9). No usage share by wage quartile or decile, no correlation coefficient, no elasticity — so the repeated claim that "usage peaks in the upper quartile" (pp.3, 8, 9) rests on a visual reading of Figure 6.
11. **Concentration of usage across accounts.** Appendix B.2 (p.19) shows account-level measurement was available, so the distribution of conversations per account (how much of the 37.2% comes from a small number of heavy users) was computable and is not reported; the comparison shown is only of category shares.
12. **Prompt-order and model sensitivity of the classifiers.** Option order is shuffled to avoid ordering bias (pp.18, 20), but no ablation reports how much assignments move under shuffling, under a different classifier model, or under the Haiku-versus-Claude choice at the screening step.
13. **The excluded tail.** The share of conversations dropped by the occupational-relevance screen, and the share dropped by the 5/15 privacy threshold, are never stated; nor is what those dropped conversations look like. The paper therefore cannot say what denominator its shares are shares of.

*Constructs used without validation*
14. **Task-to-occupation mapping with equal splitting.** "each occupation's conversation count is incremented by the number of conversations assigned to that task divided by the number of occupations associated with that task" (p.18). This uniform-split assumption drives every occupation- and category-level number, including 37.2%, the wage scatter and Table 2; it is never validated, and no alternative weighting (e.g. by employment) is tried. Appendix G.3's moderate occupation-level correlation (≥0.70 Pearson, ≥0.46 Tau-b, p.30) is the closest thing to a check and is reported as reassuring rather than as a bound.
15. **The occupational-skills facet.** Human validation covers the task hierarchy and the collaboration pattern only (p.21); the 35-skill multi-label facet has no reported accuracy, and its target — skills exhibited by the assistant — is not the construct the economics reading wants (skills demanded by the work). The paper names this gap in prose (p.8) but does not measure it.
16. **The occupational-relevance screener.** No precision or recall is reported for the Claude 3.5 Haiku screen (p.18), although it determines the analysis population.
17. **The work/coursework/personal facet.** No validation reported (pp.20–21), though the 23% figure is used to reassure the reader about the dataset's occupational content.
18. **Automation and augmentation as economic constructs.** The five conversational patterns are mapped onto Autor's (2015) substitution/complementarity distinction (p.9) with no external validation against any measure of labour substitution, task time saved or output kept; the only caveat offered is out-of-chat editing (p.9). The classifier's own pattern definitions (p.25) are about who structures the dialogue, not about whether human labour was displaced.
19. **Wage data provenance.** Median wages come from a 2019 third-party scrape of O\*NET (p.19, footnote 10) and are compared with a US Census 2022 median (p.9); neither the vintage mismatch nor the scrape's fidelity to current O\*NET or BLS values is checked.
20. **O\*NET's occupation-to-category assignment.** Category shares (Figures 2 and 3) inherit O\*NET's grouping — which places Bioinformatics Technicians in Office & Administrative (p.5) while the prose calls them a software-engineering role (p.2). No robustness check on alternative groupings is reported.
21. **"Nearly half of all total usage" for software development and writing** (p.1) is stated in the abstract but never given as a computed number with its constituent tasks in the body; the reader must assemble it from Figures 2, 11 and 12.
