Those two sections feed `programme/LEDGER.md` and `programme/THREADS.md`.

## Claims

Programme and product pages make few empirical claims. Each is numbered within its page, with the section or field it appears in, and the comparison or authority it rests on. Where a page asserts an Index result without publishing a number, that is said.

### economic-policy-responses-2025-10

1. **Delegation of full tasks to Claude is rising and collaboration falling.** (Second framing paragraph.) "Since launching the [Anthropic Economic Index](https://www.anthropic.com/economic-index), we've observed an important shift in AI use. Users are becoming [increasingly likely to delegate full tasks to Claude](https://www.anthropic.com/research/anthropic-economic-index-september-2025-report), 'collaborating' with Claude less." Authority: the hyperlink to `economic-index-2025-09-report`. No number, window or sample appears on this page; the claim is asserted by link. This is the framing on which the whole post rests.
2. **This trend is expected to accelerate.** (Same paragraph.) "As AI models continue to work independently for longer periods of time, and as more employers adopt AI to improve their productivity, we expect this trend to accelerate." A forecast, not a measurement; immediately followed by "The implications for the workforce are uncertain."
3. **Nine categories of ideas, sorted into three scenario tiers.** (§"Matching policies to scenarios" and the three following H2 blocks.) Nine numbered H3 ideas: (1) workforce training grants, (2) tax incentives for retention and retraining, (3) closing corporate tax loopholes, (4) permitting and approvals for AI infrastructure, (5) trade-adjustment-style assistance for AI displacement, (6) taxes on compute or token generation, (7) national sovereign wealth funds with AI stakes, (8) adopting or modernising VATs, (9) new revenue structures for AI's share of the economy. Ideas 1–4 are "nearly all scenarios", 5–6 "moderate acceleration", 7–9 "faster-moving scenarios".
4. **Workforce Training Grant subsidy scale.** (§1.) "governments would provide substantial annual subsidies (Ball and Cass suggest $10,000 per year in the US) directly to employers who create formal trainee positions with structured training programs." Attributed to Abigail Ball and Oren Cass, American Compass.
5. **The US tax code favours physical over human capital.** (§2.) "Businesses can immediately expense AI systems through bonus depreciation, yet face numerous restrictions when deducting worker training costs." Named reform targets: "eliminating the $5,250 cap on tax-free educational assistance, and extending full and immediate expensing to all job-related training." Attributed to Revana Sharfuddin, Mercatus Center.
6. **US permitting timescales.** (§4.) "state regulatory reviews for transmission projects can cause buildouts of new lines to last 10 years or more"; "approvals to interconnect facilities to the electric grid typically take 4-6 years for generation resources." No source is cited for either figure.
7. **An Automation Adjustment Assistance programme could start at TAA scale.** (§5.) "funding AAA at levels similar to TAA—approximately $700 million annually—could be an initial option, with mechanisms built in to increase or decrease the size of the program in line with the pace and scale of AI-driven displacement." Attributed to Suchet Mittal and Sam Manning.
8. **VAT coverage across the G7 and OECD.** (§8.) "Six out of the G7 countries have national value-added taxes (VATs), as do 37 out of 38 OECD countries. The United States is the exception." No source cited.
9. **A $10M commitment to scale the Economic Futures Program, and two symposia.** (§"Continuing the conversation".) "Earlier this fall, Anthropic announced a $10 million commitment to scale up the Economic Futures Program. This investment will support rigorous empirical research on AI's economic impacts and policy ideas, as well as expand our Symposia series—beginning with an event in London this November, which follows our September event in DC." Note for the ledger: this $10M is superseded by the $200M Research Fund of `economic-futures-research-fund-2026-07`.
10. **Anthropic endorses studying taxes that would cost Anthropic.** (§6.) "We believe taxes in this broader category deserve serious study, even though they would directly impact Anthropic's revenue and profitability."

### economic-policy-framework-2026-06

1. **Entry-level workers in the most AI-exposed occupations have seen weaker employment growth.** (PDF p.1.) "We're still early in AI's adoption, but evidence from the US already suggests that entry-level workers in occupations most exposed to AI have seen weaker employment growth in recent years." Footnote 1 gives the authority: "Brynjolfsson et al (2025), 'Canaries in the Coal Mine'; Massenkoff and McCrory (2026), 'Labor market impacts of AI: A new measure and early evidence'". This is the framework's only empirical claim about AI's labour-market effects, and one of its two sources is `labor-market-impacts-2026-03`.
2. **Published forecasts span modest growth to collapsing wages.** (PDF p.1.) "Economists' models of the future range from modest productivity-driven growth to scenarios where economic output doubles but wages collapse." Footnotes 2–3: Acemoglu (2024), "The Simple Macroeconomics of AI"; Korinek and Suh (2024), "Scenarios for the Transition to AGI".
3. **Claude is used more in the US than in any other country.** (PDF p.4, with fn 5 "Appel et al (2026), 'Anthropic Economic Index report: economic primitives'".) "We have started with the US because Anthropic is headquartered in San Francisco, and because Claude is used more in the US than any other country." Authority: `economic-index-2026-01-report`. No number reproduced.
4. **Three tiers, indexed to the unemployment rate.** (Web page and PDF pp.7, 10, 12.) "Tier 1: Baseline economy (~5% unemployment with churn)"; "Tier 2: Recession-level disruption (~10% unemployment)"; "Tier 3: Transformative disruption (unemployment exceeding historical peaks)".
5. **Social protection coverage worldwide.** (PDF p.6, fn 10 International Labour Organization (2024), "World Social Protection Report 2024-26".) "Only 52% of the world's population is covered by any social protection scheme, and in many countries the delivery systems that do exist cannot absorb a fast-moving labor market shock."
6. **US licensing prevalence.** (PDF p.9, fn 13 Bureau of Labor Statistics (2026).) "Roughly 24% of US workers hold a certification or license and evidence shows licensing requirements reduce employment and raise prices without corresponding net welfare gains."
7. **Two new investments totalling $350M.** (Web page.) "alongside the framework we're making two further investments, totaling $350 million: A $200 million commitment to an **Economic Futures Research Fund** … A $150 million national fellowship program designed to help early-career people extend the benefits of AI to communities across America."
8. **UI delivery infrastructure is not ready.** (PDF pp.5–6, fnn 8–9.) "Many states still run UI on legacy systems that buckled under pandemic claim volumes, took months to implement emergency extensions, and made billions of dollars worth of improper payments."
9. **Evidence positions taken on each Tier 1 and Tier 2 instrument.** (PDF pp.9–11, in the intervention tables.) Wage insurance: "US evidence suggests wage insurance decreases nonemployment and increases employment rates such that it is funded by avoided UI payments and increased income tax. Evidence from studies in Canada and Germany is less encouraging." Retention tax incentives: "Such incentives can reduce distributional harm and preserve employment during shocks, but can also prop up low-productivity firms." Workforce training grants: "Evidence is mixed on traditional government retraining, but shorter-term programs connected to specific employers and sectors have shown significant income gains for participants." Job matching infrastructure: "Rigorous evaluations of job recommendation systems in a number of countries find modest effects on reemployment." Sector-specific transition support: "Evidence suggests that intensive reemployment services, including career counseling and job search assistance, produce modest positive effects on earnings." Basic needs relief: "Research shows consumption drops sharply when workers exhaust UI benefits, and existing safety net programs only partially fill the gap." Each is a claim about the external literature, each footnoted; none is Anthropic's own estimate. Note for the ledger: the retraining position matches `worker-retraining-2026-08`, which is not cited in the PDF's footnotes.
10. **Extended UI did not materially discourage job search.** (PDF p.11, fnn 23–24 Chodorow-Reich et al (2019); Ganong et al (2021).) "during the Great Recession and COVID pandemic, extended benefits did little to discourage people from looking for work, while helping families keep up with essential spending."
11. **Effective tax rates on labour exceed those on capital, so a shift to capital shrinks the base.** (PDF p.13, fn 28 Korinek and Lockwood (2025).) "Because effective tax rates on labor substantially exceed those on capital, a shift in national income from labor toward capital means the current tax system captures a shrinking share of a growing economy, even as the cost of sustained income support rises."

### economic-policy-framework-2026-06

- **What the framework is** — PDF p.3, §"About this framework": "This document sets out an initial framework for US policy responses, organized by how severe the impact on jobs turns out to be. It separates the steps that make sense now in almost any scenario from the responses worth having ready if conditions worsen, and from the harder questions where research and dialogue should inform decisions."
- **What capability level the recommendations are calibrated to** — PDF p.3: "The recommendations here are calibrated to what we expect from the broad diffusion of AI capabilities that exist today or will arrive within roughly the next year."
- **The aim** — PDF p.3: "In the face of this uncertainty, our aim is to buy time—month by month, and year by year—by making the economy more flexible and resilient as AI accelerates, so that workers, firms, and governments have room to adapt."
- **The three foundations** — PDF p.5, §"The foundation": "The framework that follows depends on three foundations: the ability to see AI-driven labor market disruption as it develops, the analytical capacity to interpret what we see, and the delivery infrastructure to act on it."
- **Measurement, in full** — PDF p.5 (**the key passage for this corpus**): "**Measurement.** Governments' statistical infrastructure was built for slower-moving economies; it does not yet track AI adoption or its labor-market effects with the speed or granularity this moment requires. We need expanded investment in our statistical agencies, sustained funding for AI-specific survey instruments, and reporting requirements for AI labs and firms—including Anthropic—on deployment patterns and workforce effects. Instruments should focus on directly observable metrics like AI usage rates and intensity of use, and worker productivity indicators by level of AI adoption."
- **What the Economic Index is, in Anthropic's policy voice** — PDF p.5: "Anthropic is contributing to this effort through the Anthropic Economic Index, which uses Claude usage data to track how AI is being used across occupations and industries. We believe transparency about AI's economic effects is a precondition for informed policymaking, but data from a single company cannot tell the whole story."
- **The government analytical unit** — PDF p.5: "**A government unit focused on tracking AI's effects.** Visibility is necessary but not sufficient. Governments also need a small, dedicated unit to track how AI is moving through the economy sector by sector, stress-test the institutions that will have to absorb the shock, and flag the early signals that should trigger a policy response."
- **Delivery infrastructure** — PDF pp.5–6: "The delivery infrastructure for Unemployment Insurance (UI), which provides workers time to navigate job loss and transition, is not sufficiently prepared for a large labor market shock."
- **The trigger variable, and the ones that are not the trigger** — PDF p.6: "We suggest calibrating responses to the severity of AI-driven labor market disruption, using the unemployment rate as the primary trigger because it is widely understood, regularly measured, and directly captures labor market health. But the unemployment rate is not the only thing that matters. People may keep their jobs while the pay, security, and quality of that work erode. Governments should therefore watch a broader set of signals alongside unemployment: labor force participation, underemployment, wages, and labor's share of national income."
- **Temporary shock versus enduring restructuring** — PDF p.7: "One possibility is a temporary shock, after which the labor market re-stabilizes. The other is an enduring restructuring, in which the demand for human labor is significantly and persistently lower. These are different problems. A temporary shock calls for temporary supports withdrawn as the labor market recovers; an enduring restructuring calls for more permanent responses."
- **Stability and adaptability** — PDF p.7: "Our suggested interventions serve one or both of two aims: stability and adaptability. Some help keep the macroeconomy stable, through income support and transfers that cushion the shock and sustain demand. Others build the economy's adaptability, by moving people toward more durable work, easing transitions between jobs, and, where possible, slowing the pace of displacement. Neither is enough on its own … As disruption increases, the balance shifts from adaptability toward income support and redistribution."
- **The three tiers, as headed** — PDF pp.7, 10, 12: "**Tier 1: Baseline economy (~5% unemployment with churn)** — Prepare for disruption and ensure families share in AI's upside"; "**Tier 2: Recession-level disruption (~10% unemployment)** — Expand temporary income support and retraining"; "**Tier 3: Transformative disruption (unemployment exceeding historical peaks)** — Broaden and deepen existing support while building new mechanisms for redistribution".
- **What Tier 1 feels like** — PDF p.7: "At 5% unemployment, the headline numbers look normal. But underneath, the labor market may be churning faster than workers can adapt. AI is automating tasks across occupations simultaneously, steadily compressing the value of skills that workers spent years acquiring."
- **What Tier 3 is** — PDF p.13: "not merely high unemployment, but a structural decoupling of productive contribution from income."
- **Pre-distributive capital accounts** — PDF p.8, with its three sub-definitions: "Phased eligibility expansion: beyond children, accounts should be extended to young adults entering the workforce and to incumbent workers in occupations most exposed to AI-driven displacement, with a defined path to coverage for all working-age adults."; "Flexible withdrawal for workforce transitions: account holders should be able to draw on funds for retraining, relocation, credentialing, and other transition expenses."; "Equity funding: policymakers should expand the mechanisms by which these accounts can be funded, including with equity in AI companies, so that beneficiaries share directly in nearer-term gains from AI-driven growth."
- **Wage insurance** — PDF p.9: "Time-limited, capped supplemental income for workers who take lower-paying jobs after displacement, funded by the government."
- **Occupational licensing reform** — PDF p.9: "Federal funding for states that make it easier to enter licensed occupations, for example by recognizing licenses issued in other states or shortening the path to qualification, without weakening the rules on what licensed workers may do or who supervises them."
- **Retention tax incentives** — PDF p.9: "Multi-year tax credits for companies that retain and redeploy workers into substantially different roles when adopting AI."
- **Workforce training grants** — PDF p.10: "Grants for employer-connected sectoral training programs that place workers into roles with strong current demand."
- **Job matching infrastructure** — PDF p.10: "Invest in AI-powered skills-based labor market platforms, Learning and Employment Records, and skills wallets."
- **Sector-specific transition support** — PDF p.11: "Targeted assistance for workers in industries facing acute AI-driven displacement. Could include extended income support, subsidized retraining, job search allowances, and relocation assistance."
- **Basic needs relief** — PDF p.11: "Monthly payments to displaced workers who have exhausted UI benefits or labor market entrants unable to secure employment after some period of documented search, ensuring continued access to food, housing, and healthcare."
- **Steering or sequencing the rollout** — PDF p.4: "When technological progress causes large disruption and the available redistributive tools are imperfect, economic theory suggests the technology's rollout should be steered, or sequenced. … if disruption happens beyond the economy's ability to adapt, at the levels described in Tier 2, we would support government regulations and incentives at the firm level that manage the pace of displacement. Uniform application across the field is essential: any firm that slows down alone simply cedes the market to those that do not."
- **What AI can be used for, as a firm-level choice** — PDF p.3: "AI can be used to produce the same output with fewer people, or to accomplish more with the same number of people: building new products, serving markets that were previously out of reach, and raising the capability of less-experienced workers."
- **Anthropic's own four values** — web page and PDF p.2, quoted identically in both; the fourth is "Whatever happens, we are on the side of people. We are trying to solve these problems. We take no satisfaction in contributing to them, and we are not working to make them more likely." The second is the one that bears on any post about worth of work: "Supporting people financially is necessary, and it is the primary focus of this document because it is the more tractable of the two problems. It is not sufficient. There is dignity in work. We should help people find work wherever we can, and society should keep searching for ways to remain at full employment."
- **The value judgement Anthropic names as a value judgement** — PDF p.14: "We recognize the second rationale rests on a value judgment—that work confers something income alone does not—which not everyone shares and which future generations may weigh differently. The case here does not depend on it."

## Data and methods

In the wiki author's words, with references. For programme pages this section records what data (if any) the page rests on and how it could be checked — not a methodology, because these pages have none.

### economic-policy-responses-2025-10

No data, no method, no appendix. The page's single empirical premise — rising delegation, falling collaboration — is imported by hyperlink from `economic-index-2025-09-report` and is not restated with a number, a window, a sample or a facet. Anyone wanting to check the framing must go to that report and, behind it, to the released `release_2025_09_15` files; whether the released aggregates support "increasingly likely to delegate full tasks … 'collaborating' with Claude less" over a stated interval is a data-steward question and is **not** confirmed here. Terminology check against `/mnt/memory/standards/terminology.md`: the page's "delegate full tasks" corresponds to Anthropic's *automation* (Directive + Feedback Loop) and its "collaborating" to *augmentation*; it does not use the collaboration facet's names, and it must not be read as a statement about the AI-autonomy primitive, which did not exist until `economic-index-2026-01-report`. All other numbers on the page are external (American Compass, Mercatus, NBER, DOL, SSRN, Centre for British Progress) and are attributed to their proposers.

### economic-policy-framework-2026-06

No original data. The PDF is a policy document with 30 footnoted references to external literature and two to Anthropic's own: fn 1 to `labor-market-impacts-2026-03` (as "Massenkoff and McCrory (2026)") and fn 5 to `economic-index-2026-01-report` (as "Appel et al (2026)"), plus fn 7, "Anthropic (2026), 'Anthropic Economic Index'", i.e. the hub page. Fn 6 is the source of its measurement agenda: "The American Economic Association Committee on Economic Statistics (2025), 'Measuring the Economic Effects of Artificial Intelligence'". Method, such as it is: assemble the external evidence for each candidate instrument, state where it is thin, and assign the instrument to a tier keyed to the unemployment rate. The one methodological commitment that bears on our own posts is the trigger choice — unemployment as primary, with labour force participation, underemployment, wages and labour's share as the watch list (p.6) — because it tells us which outcome variables Anthropic would accept as evidence of disruption. Nothing in the PDF is reproducible; there is no dataset, no code and no table of estimates.

No data. Method is programme design: three pillars, three participation routes, two symposia, one grant instrument. The only methodologically relevant sentence is pillar 3's promise to create "longitudinal datasets tracking AI's economic usage and impact" — a claim about the future shape of the released data, not about any file that existed in June 2025.

### economic-futures-uk-europe-2025-11

This is the one page of the twelve that reports results, and it reports them with no method at all. Five usage claims (Claims 1–5) are attributed to "the Anthropic Economic Index" with: no sample size, no data window, no release identifier, no surface (Claude.ai vs first-party API vs Claude Code), no per-capita denominator for "especially high per capita", no comparison set for "most often used", and no taxonomy label for the categories described in prose ("calibrating, troubleshooting, and repairing equipment"; "information about dining, shopping and the nightlife"). ### economic-futures-research-fund-2026-07

**The five priority areas and every fundable direction, verbatim.** This is the densest ledger source of the twelve pages.

*Priority 1 — "Shaping AI's impact on workers at the firm and workplace level".* Rationale: "AI's impact on the labor market depends on the systems, workplaces, training protocols, and institutional choices that are built around it. The existing evidence on AI's integration in the workplace is observational and short-term. Field experiments can help us understand which collaborative patterns develop human expertise alongside AI, how organizational design choices affect both productivity and who captures the gains, and what difference worker voice makes in those design choices." Fundable directions:
- "Field experiments randomizing AI systems and AI integration designs at the firm or team level, including comparisons of designs co-developed with workers and worker organizations against top-down approaches."
- "Estimates of the impact of organizational choices around AI workplace integration and usage on the incidence of AI productivity gains."
- "Evaluations of retention tax credits and employer co-investment requirements."

*Priority 2 — "Equipping people to navigate AI-driven transitions".* Rationale: "The evidence on retraining and job placement is mixed, and it may not generalize to AI-induced economic disruption and rapid structural transformation." Fundable directions:

- "Field experiments on the early-career and professional pipeline, e.g., what apprenticeship, mentorship, or rotational models can build expertise if junior tasks are absorbed by AI."
- "Evaluations of curriculum and educational delivery models in K-12 and higher education that aim to prepare students for a transformed labor market, including longitudinal pilots that link educational interventions to later labor market outcomes."
- "Tests of ambitious mobility instruments, for example paid leave tied to retraining programs and portable benefits that follow workers across employers."
- And the scale-up question: "We want to find out whether promising programs could scale quickly across a broader population. For example, a large-scale 'fire drill' where selected programs are scaled up rapidly for job seekers in a given state could provide evidence on how well these programs work in the face of major disruption."

*Priority 3 — "Modernizing income support for AI-driven displacement".* Fundable directions:
- "Unemployment Insurance (UI) reforms suited to AI-driven displacement, including alternative eligibility thresholds, automatic extension triggers linked to industry or occupation, and integration of UI with wage insurance, retraining, or other transition supports."
- "Basic needs relief for workers who exhaust UI, never qualified, or are persistently underemployed."
- "Longer-duration unconditional income pilots at livable levels, designed to speak to scenarios where income and work are decoupled for sustained periods of time, with analyzed outcomes spanning not only labor supply and consumption but also wellbeing, family stability, child development, civic participation, and how recipients structure their time."

*Priority 5 — "Generating new evidence on public investments".* Fundable directions:
