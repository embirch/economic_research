## Claims

Each claim gives the number as published, the page and figure or table, and the comparison the number rests on.

**Chapter 1: what changed since November 2025**

1. **Claude.ai task concentration fell.** "the top 10 most common O\*NET tasks went from 24% of conversations to just 19%" (p. 5, Fig. 1.1). Comparison: the Nov 2025 sample (previous report) versus the Feb 5–12 2026 sample, same platform, same top-10-task measure. Fig. 1.1's full Claude.ai series: 21% (Jan 2025), 24% (Mar 2025), 23% (Aug 2025), 24% (Nov 2025), 19% (Feb 2026).
2. **1P API task concentration rose over the longer horizon and was flat over the short one.** Fig. 1.1's API series: 28% (Aug 2025), 32% (Nov 2025), 33% (Feb 2026) — first plotted point Aug 2025 (p. 5). The Discussion states it as "Since August 2025, 1P API usage has become more concentrated, with the top 10 O\*NET tasks now accounting for 33% of traffic, up from 28%" (p. 19). Over Nov→Feb the body says "task concentration in the API remained roughly flat despite the influx of coding activity" (p. 6). Comparison: Aug 2025 versus Feb 2026 on the API for the 33/28 figure; Nov versus Feb for "roughly flat".
3. **Coding is still the largest single domain on Claude.ai.** "tasks associated with Computer and Mathematical occupations accounting for 35% of conversations on Claude.ai (see Appendix)" (p. 5). Footnote 2 (p. 11) states this number uses 2019 O\*NET-SOC codes while previous reports used the 2010 vintage; no cross-vintage comparison figure is given in the body. Evidence is in the appendix.
4. **The mechanism offered for the concentration divergence is Claude Code's architecture.** "Claude Code's agentic architecture splits coding work into smaller API calls, which are labeled as distinct tasks. So while coding's overall share of API traffic has grown, it is spread across many task categories rather than concentrated in a few" (p. 6). This is a stated mechanism, not a measured decomposition; no number is attached to it.
5. **Use-case mix shifted on Claude.ai.** "Coursework fell from 19% to 12% of conversations, while personal use rose from 35% to 42% of conversations" (p. 6, Fig. 1.2); Fig. 1.2 also plots work at 46% (Nov 2025) and 45% (Feb 2026). Comparison: two Claude.ai samples, Nov 2025 and Feb 2026, shares of conversations classified work / personal / coursework.
6. **Part of the coursework fall tracks academic calendars.** "The drop in coursework conversations was 5 percentage points in countries where the school term was active and 12 percentage points in the countries where most students were on break" (footnote 3, p. 11). Comparison: term-active countries versus break countries, Nov→Feb change in coursework share.
7. **Cumulative task coverage of jobs barely moved.** "About 49% of jobs have seen at least a quarter of their tasks performed using Claude" (p. 2–3); "In our previous report, we noted that 49% of jobs had seen at least a quarter of their tasks performed using Claude. In this data pull, that cumulative estimate barely changed (Appendix Figure A.2). Our data from this report showed many fewer novel O\*NET tasks than in our previous report" (p. 7). Comparison: cumulative across samples, current versus previous report; the "many fewer novel tasks" claim is stated without a number in the body.
8. **Augmentation rose slightly on Claude.ai.** Fig. 1.3 (p. 7) plots augmentation 55% (Jan 2025), 55% (Mar 2025), 47% (Aug 2025), 52% (Nov 2025), 53% (Feb 2026) and automation 41%, 42%, 49%, 45%, 44% over the same five report windows — i.e. Aug 2025 is the one window where automation exceeded augmentation. Body: "Figure 1.3 shows that augmentation in Claude.ai increased slightly. This was driven by small bumps in validation and learning patterns. In Appendix Figure A.3, we show that automation decreased sharply in the 1P API data" (p. 7). Comparison: Nov 2025 versus Feb 2026 shares of conversations by interaction type, grouped. The API side is evidenced only in the appendix.
9. **The platforms are diverging in coding share.** "Since August 2025, the share of tasks in this category has increased by 14% in the API and decreased by 18% in Claude.ai" (p. 7), "this category" being Computer and Mathematical. Comparison: Aug 2025 versus Feb 2026, within-platform proportional change (not percentage points).
10. **Management tasks grew on Claude.ai.** "The increase in tasks associated with Management occupations in Claude.ai, which went from 3% to 5% of its traffic, comes from a mix of both analytical tasks (e.g., preparing an investment memo) and responding to customer questions" (p. 8). Comparison: Nov 2025 versus Feb 2026, share of Claude.ai traffic.
11. **Average task value on Claude.ai fell; on the API it rose.** "This estimate of the value of tasks in Claude.ai has dropped slightly from $49.3 to $47.9, mostly due to an increase in simple factual questions (e.g., sports outcomes, weather) and a decrease in coding as it shifts to the API" (p. 8, Fig. 1.4). The $49.3 is the Jan 2025 (first report) point, not November: Fig. 1.4's Claude.ai series is $49.3 (Jan 2025), $48.5 (Mar 2025), $48.9 (Aug 2025), $48.3 (Nov 2025), $47.9 (Feb 2026); the 1P API series is $50.5 (Aug 2025), $50.4 (Nov 2025), $50.7 (Feb 2026). The figure marks "US avg. hourly wage: $37.3" as a reference line and its y-axis is labelled "Volume-weighted mean wage (Feb 2026 $/hr)". The Discussion restates the comparison as "has declined on Claude.ai since our first report, while rising among API users" (p. 19). Comparison: report-window means of an occupation-wage-weighted task value, across five windows and two platforms, against the US average wage.
12. **Five primitives moved between November 2025 and February 2026 on Claude.ai** (Table 1.1, p. 9): Human education (yr) 12.21 → 11.92, published difference ▼ -0.29; AI autonomy (1–5) 3.38 → 3.41, published difference ▲ +0.02; Human time (min) 185.53 → 183.77, ▼ -1.76; Human and AI time (min) 15.35 → 14.30, ▼ -1.05; Human can't do alone (%) 12.09 → 12.24, ▲ +0.15. Caption: "All differences are statistically significant with p<0.001, except Human-only time with p<0.05." Body gloss: "The average years of education required for the human inputs declined from 12.2 to 11.9 years, users granted more autonomy to the AI, and the time required for the human to do the task alone fell by about 2 minutes. One change goes ostensibly in the opposite direction: the tasks performed by Claude were judged to be slightly less possible for a human without access to AI" (pp. 8–9). Comparison: two Claude.ai samples, means of each primitive.
13. **Two API workflow families at least doubled.** "We highlight two API workflows that appeared more frequently in February as compared to three months prior, with their shares at least doubling in our latest sample: Business sales & outreach automation: sales enablement generation, B2B lead qualification research, customer data enrichment, cold-email drafting. Automated trading & market ops: monitor markets or positions, propose specific investments, inform traders of market conditions, and related tasks" (p. 9). The selection rule is footnote 6 (p. 11): tasks appearing ≥ 300 times in the current data and showing ≥ 2× growth against the previous report. No share levels are published for either family.
14. **Customer Service Representatives' exposure is restated from an earlier report.** "In a previous report, we highlighted that customer service tasks, including, for example, automated support for payment and billing issues, are prevalent in the API data. These contributed to a higher observed exposure for Customer Service Representatives—Claude was recorded doing a high share of their tasks in automated workflows, so these jobs may be more likely to change as AI diffuses" (p. 9). No new number in this report.
15. **US state convergence continued but slowed, and the horizon was revised.** "From August 2025 to February 2026, the share of per-person usage going to the top five states has decreased from 30 to 24%. The Gini coefficient has fallen since August 2025, though the pace of convergence has slowed. When we update our estimates from the previous report, we find that at this rate states would arrive at roughly equal usage per capita in 5-9 years, rather than 2-5" (p. 10). Fig. 1.5 (left panel) gives state AUI Ginis: 0.37 (Aug 2025), 0.31 (Nov 2025), 0.29 (Feb 2026). Footnote 7 (p. 11): "The range is given to reflect the different estimates from running the model in our previous report with (5 years) or without (9 years) weights." Comparison: the January 2026 report's own convergence model, re-estimated on data through Feb 2026, against its published 2–5 year horizon.
16. **Cross-country concentration rose.** "Across countries (right panel), the pattern is reversed: usage has become slightly more concentrated, with the Gini rising over the same period. The countries using Claude the most (per capita) now account for a larger share of overall usage, with the top 20 countries going from 45% to 48% of usage adjusted for population" (pp. 10–11). Fig. 1.5 (right panel) country AUI Ginis: 0.48 (Aug 2025), 0.46 (Nov 2025), 0.50 (Feb 2026) — i.e. the Aug→Feb rise runs through a fall at November. Comparison: Lorenz curves and Ginis of the AUI across countries, three windows.
17. **A second, differently-cut state statistic appears in the introduction.** "Claude usage per capita continued to converge within the United States: the share of usage accounted for by the 10 highest usage states decreased from 40% to 38% since our last report" (p. 3). Comparison: top 10 states, Nov 2025 versus Feb 2026 — distinct from the top-five, Aug-to-Feb cut on p. 10.

**Chapter 2: model selection**

18. **Opus is over-selected for higher-wage occupational domains by paying Claude.ai users.** "for paid Claude.ai accounts, which have access to all model classes, 55% of Computer and Mathematical tasks (like coding software) use Opus, compared to 45% of Educational tasks" (p. 13). Fig. 2.1 caption: "In this sample, 51% of overall usage is Opus, and 55% of Computer and Mathematical usage (+4.4pp) is Opus." The bars, in the figure's order: Educational instruction and library -6.5 pp; Arts, design, entertainment, sports, and media -5.7 pp; Sales and related -1.6 pp; Office and administrative support -0.6 pp; Life, physical, and social science +0.9 pp; Management +1.9 pp; Business and financial operations +2.3 pp; Computer and mathematical +4.4 pp. Comparison: each occupational domain's Opus share against the 51% all-domain Opus share, paid Claude.ai accounts only, Feb 2026.
19. **The Opus share rises with the occupation's wage, roughly twice as fast on the API.** "on Claude.ai, 34% of Software Developer tasks involve Opus compared to just 12% of Tutor tasks. Overall, for every additional $10 of hourly wage for a task, the share of conversations using Opus increases by 1.5 percentage points for Claude.ai users. The 1P API traffic shows much more response to the complexity of the task. Its slope is about twice as large, with the Opus share increasing 2.8 percentage points for every $10 in task value" (p. 14). Fig. 2.2 panel titles give the slopes to two decimals: "Claude.ai: slope = +1.48pp per +$10/hr" and "API: slope = +2.79pp per +$10/hr"; each point is an occupation, wage on the x-axis (plotted to $180/hr), Opus share on the y-axis (0–70%). Comparison: cross-occupation regression of Opus share on occupation hourly wage, run separately on the two platforms.
20. **The introduction states the same result in over/under-representation terms.** "among paying Claude.ai users, Opus is used 4 percentage points more than average for coding tasks and 7 percentage points less than average for tutoring-related tasks. This model switching is about twice as stark for API users" (pp. 3–4). Comparison: same as claim 18, with the Fig. 2.1 bars rounded (coding +4.4 pp; the -7 pp for "tutoring-related" is not a bar in Fig. 2.1, whose Educational instruction and library bar is -6.5 pp).

**Chapter 2: tenure and learning-by-doing**

21. **High-tenure users differ from low-tenure users across every characteristic tabulated** (Table 2.1, p. 15; high tenure = signed up ≥ 6 months before the data pull, low tenure = everyone else). Shares of conversations, low → high (published difference): directive 38.1% → 29.4% (▼ -8.7 pp); feedback loop 11.7% → 12.1% (▲ +0.5 pp); task iteration 24.5% → 28.2% (▲ +3.6 pp); validation 4.4% → 5.6% (▲ +1.3 pp); learning 21.3% → 24.7% (▲ +3.4 pp); work 41.6% → 48.9% (▲ +7.3 pp); personal 44.3% → 40.3% (▼ -4.0 pp); coursework 14.1% → 10.8% (▼ -3.3 pp); task success rate 66.7% → 73.1% (▲ +6.4 pp); top 10 tasks' share of usage 22.2% → 20.7% (▼ -1.6 pp). Numeric facets (means): AI autonomy (1-5) 3.42 → 3.40 (▼ -0.6%); Human education (yr) 11.5 → 12.3 (▲ +6.6%); AI education (yr) 11.7 → 12.4 (▲ +6.0%). Body gloss: "High tenure users are more likely to use Claude to iterate on their work, and much less likely to delegate greater responsibility through directive use patterns. They are 7 percentage points more likely to be using Claude for work, and use Claude for tasks that tend to require higher levels of education. Finally, their usage is less concentrated in certain tasks. The top 10 O\*NET tasks account for a slightly lower (20.7% compared to 22.2%) share of usage for the high tenure group" (p. 15). Comparison: two groups of users in one cross-section, split at six months since signup, unconditional means. Note that under the report's own grouping (automation = directive + feedback loop) the table implies automation 49.8% for low tenure against 41.5% for high tenure; the report does not print that pair.
22. **Both education and personal use trend monotonically in tenure.** "the years of schooling needed to understand the human prompt increases by almost 1 year for every additional year of Claude usage. In the panel on the right, we show that at the same time, personal use decreases: people who signed up a year ago devote 38% of their conversations to personal use cases, compared to 44% for the newest users" (p. 16, Fig. 2.3 — two binned scatterplots of human education years and personal-use share against days since signup, x-axis running 0 to 18 months). Comparison: binned means across signup cohorts within the single Feb 2026 sample.
23. **The request clusters with the highest and lowest mean tenure differ in sophistication.** "among request clusters, tasks with highest mean tenure included: AI research, git operations, revising manuscripts, and startup fundraising. The tasks with the lowest average tenure have more simple workflows like writing haikus, checking sports scores, and suggesting food for a party" (p. 17). No tenure values are published for these clusters. Footnote 3 (p. 18) attaches a caveat to the low-tenure end: the sampling period overlapped Anthropic's Super Bowl advertisements.
24. **The tenure–success association survives narrow within-task comparison** (Fig. 2.4, p. 17, and text pp. 17–18). Three specifications regressing the binary success measure on a high-tenure indicator: "(1) Raw", "(2) + task FE", "(3) + full controls". The text reports "long-tenure users are about 5 percentage points more likely to have a successful conversation" for (1); "This control moderates the effect somewhat, bringing it closer to 3 percentage points" for (2); and "a slightly higher impact of high tenure, suggesting a 4 percentage point higher success rate accounting for the full controls" for (3). Read off Fig. 2.4, the plotted coefficients are approximately 4.7 pp, 3.1 pp and 3.8 pp, with 95% confidence intervals that exclude zero in all three specifications (the figure prints no numeric labels). Caption: "'Task FEs' indicates fixed effects for O\*NET task and request cluster. Full controls adds model, use case, and country fixed effects." Comparison: high versus low tenure within the same O\*NET task and request cluster, then additionally within model, use case and country.
25. **The tenure cut is claimed to be robust to its threshold.** "These results are similar however we define high tenure" (footnote 2, p. 15). No alternative thresholds or estimates are published.
26. **The introduction states the tenure result in relative terms.** "people who have been using Claude for 6 months or more have 10% fewer personal conversations and a 6% higher education level reflected in their inputs. Most strikingly, people in this higher-tenure group have a 10% higher success rate in their conversations, an association that is not explained by their task selection, country of origin, or other factors" (p. 4). Comparison: high versus low tenure, proportional rather than percentage-point differences; the corresponding Table 2.1 rows are -4.0 pp on a 44.3% base, +6.6%, and +6.4 pp on a 66.7% base (see `## Verification` for the arithmetic).
27. **The report says what is new in it.** "This report revisited the core measures we use to track Claude usage, and analyzed model selection and success for the first time" (p. 19); "With this report, we can begin to trace out how various economic primitives have changed" (p. 19).
28. **The aggregate complexity of Claude.ai prompts fell.** "We also note a slight decrease in the aggregate complexity of prompts in Claude.ai, with conversations in Claude.ai exhibiting less sophisticated inputs and shorter estimated completion times" (p. 19). Comparison: Nov 2025 versus Feb 2026 primitive means (Table 1.1).
29. **Claude's use remains unrepresentative of the economy.** "Overall, Claude is used for high-value, complex work that is not broadly representative of the US economy. But as the user base has grown, less remunerated tasks have become a slightly larger share of traffic" (p. 19). Comparison: task-value distribution on Claude against the US average wage (Fig. 1.4), across report windows.

## Definitions (verbatim)

Quoted exactly; page references to the PDF. No paraphrase in this section.

**The Index and its instrument**
- "The Anthropic Economic Index uses our privacy-preserving data analysis system to track how Claude is being used across the economy. It's part of our effort to understand the economic impacts of AI as early as possible, so that researchers and policymakers have adequate time to prepare." (p. 2)
- "We use our privacy-preserving system, which allows us to describe behavior at an aggregated level without revealing the content of individual transcripts." (p. 5)
- "In this analysis, we use log-level data to estimate the models with the same privacy thresholds. See the Appendix for more on the methodology." (footnote 1, p. 18)

**Platforms**
- "We sample 1 million conversations from both Claude.ai, our consumer-facing web product, and our first-party API, the developer-facing interface for integrating Claude into products and workflows." (p. 5)
- "'First-party API' or 1P API refers to developer traffic routed directly through Anthropic's own programming interface, which is distinct from both Anthropic's consumer-facing Claude.ai application and third-party platforms such as Amazon Bedrock or Google Cloud Vertex." (footnote 1, p. 4)
- "This includes data from Claude Code." (footnote 1, p. 11)

**Interaction types, automation and augmentation**
- "Since our first report, we have classified conversations into one of five interaction types—directive, feedback loop, task iteration, validation, and learning—which we group into two broader categories: automation and augmentation." (p. 7)
- "See the Appendix for definitions of the interaction types." (footnote 4, p. 11)
- "Automation = directive + feedback loop" and "Augmentation = validation + task iteration + learning" (Fig. 1.3 axis note, p. 7)
- "the rate of augmentation, collaborative interaction where the AI complements the user's abilities" (p. 2)
- Figure title and caption: "Automation vs. augmentation over time (Claude.ai)"; "Figure 1.3: Collaboration mode share, Claude.ai / Collaboration mode frequencies across Anthropic Economic Index Reports in Claude.ai." (p. 7)

**Task concentration**

**Use case**

## Data and methods

In the wiki author's words, with page references. The report ships no code and describes its methods only in the body, the footnotes and the separate appendix.

**Samples and windows.** One million conversations are sampled from each of two platforms — Claude.ai and the first-party API — for the window 5–12 February 2026 (pp. 2, 5). The first-party API sample includes Claude Code traffic (footnote 1, p. 11) and excludes third-party routes such as Amazon Bedrock and Google Cloud Vertex (footnote 1, p. 4). The comparison window throughout Chapter 1 is the January 2026 report's November 2025 sample; Figures 1.1, 1.3 and 1.4 also carry the Jan 2025, Mar 2025 and Aug 2025 report windows, with the API series beginning at Aug 2025. The window sits three months after the release of Claude Opus 4.5 and coincides with the release of Claude Opus 4.6 (p. 2), and it overlaps Anthropic's Super Bowl advertising (footnote 3, p. 18) and winter school breaks in some countries (footnote 3, p. 11).

**Classification.** Conversations are classified with Anthropic's privacy-preserving system (Clio, cited at p. 2 and via arXiv 2412.13678 at p. 5), which reports only aggregates and never individual transcripts. Each conversation is mapped to an O\*NET task and a request cluster; to one of five interaction types (directive, feedback loop, task iteration, validation, learning), grouped into automation and augmentation (p. 7); to a use case (work, personal, coursework; Fig. 1.2); and to a set of numeric "primitives" (human education years, AI education years, AI autonomy 1–5, human time, human-and-AI time, human-can't-do-alone), whose definitions are in the appendix (Table 1.1 caption, p. 9). Occupational aggregation uses 2019 O\*NET-SOC codes for the 35% Computer and Mathematical figure, where earlier reports used the 2010 vintage (footnote 2, p. 11).

**Task value.** Each O\*NET task is priced at the average hourly wage of US workers who perform it, from the May 2024 BLS OEWS tables; where several occupations perform a task, wages are averaged with weights for employment and the fraction of time spent on the task (footnote 5, p. 11). Platform-level task value is the volume-weighted mean over conversations, plotted in February 2026 dollars per hour against a US average of $37.3 (Fig. 1.4, p. 8). The report does not state the deflator used to move May 2024 wages into February 2026 dollars.

**Concentration measures.** Task concentration is the share of sampled conversations falling in the ten most prevalent O\*NET tasks, computed within platform and within report window (Fig. 1.1, p. 5). Cumulative job coverage — the share of jobs for which at least a quarter of O\*NET tasks have been seen on Claude — is carried over from the previous report and updated in the appendix (p. 7, Appendix Figure A.2).

**Emerging API workflows.** Candidate tasks are filtered to those appearing at least 300 times in the February data and growing at least twofold against November, then grouped by hand into the two workflow families named on p. 9 (footnote 6, p. 11).

**Geography.** The unit is the AI Usage Index: a geography's share of Claude usage divided by its share of working-age population (p. 10). Inequality is summarised with Lorenz curves and Gini coefficients across US states and across countries for three windows (Aug 2025, Nov 2025, Feb 2026; Fig. 1.5, p. 10), plus top-five and top-twenty concentration shares. The convergence horizon is produced by re-running the January 2026 report's convergence model on data through February 2026; the 5–9 year range is the spread between the weighted (5 years) and unweighted (9 years) versions of that model (footnote 7, p. 11).

**Model selection.** Model class (Haiku, Sonnet, Opus) is observed per conversation. Figure 2.1 restricts to paid Claude.ai accounts, "which have access to all model classes" (p. 13), and expresses each occupational domain's Opus share as a deviation from the 51% sample-wide Opus share. Figure 2.2 is a cross-occupation scatter — one point per occupation, occupation hourly wage on the x-axis and Opus share on the y-axis — with a fitted slope per platform (+1.48 pp and +2.79 pp per $10/hr). The report does not state whether Figure 2.2's Claude.ai panel is restricted to paid accounts, nor the number of occupations plotted, nor standard errors on the slopes.

**Tenure.** Tenure is days since the user signed up for Claude (Fig. 2.3 axis, p. 16). The binary cut is at six months before the data pull; the low-tenure group is everyone else (p. 15). Table 2.1 reports unconditional group means of conversation-level characteristics. Figure 2.3 bins the same cross-section by days since signup out to 18 months. The report states, without supporting estimates, that results are similar under other definitions of high tenure (footnote 2, p. 15).

**Success regressions.** The outcome is a binary success measure, described as Claude's own assessment of whether the conversation was successful and defined in the January 2026 report (p. 18). The estimates use log-level data under the same privacy thresholds as the aggregate analysis, with the methodology deferred to the appendix (footnote 1, p. 18). Three specifications are reported: raw bivariate; plus fixed effects for O\*NET task and request cluster; plus fixed effects for model, use case and country (Fig. 2.4, p. 17). Whiskers are 95% confidence intervals. No sample sizes, standard errors, clustering choices or minimum detectable effects are reported anywhere in the report body.

**Statistical reporting.** The only inferential statements in the body are Table 1.1's blanket p-values (p<0.001 for all rows except human-only time at p<0.05, p. 9) and the confidence intervals in Fig. 2.4. Table 2.1 carries no standard errors or p-values. No sample counts are printed in the report beyond the one-million-conversation sample size per platform.

**Released data (no code).** The report points to the Hugging Face dataset `Anthropic/EconomicIndex` for the data behind it (p. 20). Per the steward's enumeration (`data/releases/INDEX.md`), the corresponding folder `release_2026_03_24` holds two raw CSVs for the 5–12 February 2026 window — one Claude.ai, one 1P API — plus `data_documentation.md`, and ships no replication code; the tenure, model-selection and success analyses run on log-level data that is not in the release.

## Open questions, conjectures and promised follow-ups (verbatim)

Quoted exactly; page references to the PDF. No paraphrase in this section.

**The framing question of the report**
1. "An important question is how inequality of adoption might determine where and to whom the benefits of AI will accrue. If, for example, effective AI use requires complementary skills and expertise—which we argued in our previous report—and if such skills can be acquired through use and experimentation, then the benefits from early adoption may be self-reinforcing." 2. "In our second chapter we investigate how users appear to shape the value that they get out of Claude: how they match model capability to the task at hand, and how usage patterns and outcomes shift with experience on the platform." (p. 3)

4. "How does one's tenure with Claude shape their experience with it?" (p. 15)

**Model selection**
5. "Little is known about how AI users choose between different models, navigating tradeoffs around speed, performance, and cost. If users are calibrating to the task at hand, we should see Opus concentrated on harder, higher-value work." (p. 12)

## What it did not test

This section is the wiki author's inference, not the report's. It lists adjacent questions the report appears to have had the data to answer but did not report, and constructs it used without validation. Feasibility of any of these on the public release is a question for the data steward (the tenure, success and model-class variables are log-level and, on the steward's enumeration of `release_2026_03_24`, are not obviously in the two published CSVs).

**Adjacent questions the data could have answered**
1. **No panel, no event study.** Tenure is measured in a single cross-section (5–12 February 2026). Anthropic has five report windows of Claude.ai data; it never follows a signup cohort across windows, which is the comparison that would separate learning-by-doing from cohort composition — the very distinction the report defers to the future (p. 20, quote 15 above).
2. **Tenure × geography.** Country fixed effects enter specification (3) but no heterogeneity is reported. Whether the tenure–success gap is larger in low-AUI countries or low-usage US states is the direct test of the report's own self-reinforcing-advantage conjecture (p. 3) and of its "poorer countries, earlier adopters" reading (p. 17).
3. **Tenure × platform.** Chapter 2 opens by noting that "the userbase on both Claude.ai and the API has grown rapidly" (p. 15), but Table 2.1, Figure 2.3 and Figure 2.4 never state which platform they cover, and no API tenure result is reported.
4. **Tenure × model selection.** Model fixed effects enter specification (3), so model class is observed for the tenure sample, yet the report never asks whether high-tenure users select Opus more often — the obvious bridge between its two chapters.
5. **Success decomposed by interaction type.** Success and collaboration mode are both classified per conversation. Whether the high-tenure success advantage comes from iterating rather than delegating (the mechanism the Discussion favours, p. 19) is testable and untested.
6. **Shift-share of the task-value fall.** Task shares and task wages are both in hand, so the $48.3 → $47.9 Claude.ai move could be decomposed into composition (coding leaving, personal queries arriving) versus within-task change. The report asserts the composition story ("mostly due to", p. 8) without decomposing it.
7. **Whether the O\*NET-SOC vintage change explains part of chapter 1.** The 2010 → 2019 recode (footnote 2, p. 11) is disclosed for one number but its effect on the top-10 concentration series, the occupational shares and the wage-based task value is never quantified, and no both-vintages comparison is shown.
8. **Whether the Feb-window shocks bias the headline comparisons.** Two confounds are named: winter break, stated in the body at p. 6 ("Some of the drop in coursework can be explained by academic calendars in countries where students were on winter break during our sample period") and quantified in **chapter 1 endnote 3, p. 11** ("The drop in coursework conversations was 5 percentage points in countries where the school term was active and 12 percentage points in the countries where most students were on break"); and Super Bowl signups, in **chapter 2 endnote 3, p. 18** ("Our sampling period overlapped with the release of our Super Bowl advertisements, which brought many first-time users"). Only the first is quantified, and only for coursework. The same reweighting — dropping or reweighting first-week signups — is never applied to the top-10 concentration fall, the task-value fall, the primitive shifts in Table 1.1, or the tenure contrasts, even though new signups mechanically inflate the low-tenure group. *(Citation made precise 2026-09-16 and re-verified against the PDF at pp. 6, 11 and 18. This item had read "winter break, footnote 3 p. 11", which `room/lead-2026-09-16-ledger-status.md` flagged as inconsistent with Limitations item 1's p. 6. Both are right: the report numbers its endnotes per chapter, the confound is named in the body at p. 6 and quantified in ch. 1 endnote 3 at p. 11, and ch. 2 endnote 3 at p. 18 is a different note. No error; the citation is now unambiguous.)*
9. **Whether the convergence horizon is robust to the new window.** The 5–9 year range comes from re-running the previous report's model with and without weights (footnote 7, p. 11). No alternative functional form, no confidence interval, and no test of whether February's signup surge (concentrated, on the report's own account, among casual users) drives the slowdown.
10. **Why the country Gini is non-monotonic.** Fig. 1.5 shows 0.48 → 0.46 → 0.50 across countries, but the text reports only the Aug-to-Feb rise "over the same period" (p. 10) and does not address the November dip, nor whether it is within noise.
11. **Small-cell noise in geography.** Neither the state nor the country Lorenz curves carry sample counts or uncertainty bands, so no reader can tell whether a Gini move from 0.31 to 0.29 is distinguishable from sampling variation.
12. **Opus gradient net of complexity.** Figure 2.2 regresses Opus share on the occupation's wage. The report's own primitives (human education years, AI autonomy, human time) measure task complexity directly, so wage and complexity could have been entered together; as published, the "demand for intelligence" reading (p. 12) cannot be separated from a pure wage gradient.
13. **Free versus paid model selection.** Figure 2.1 is paid Claude.ai accounts only. Whether free-tier users show the same gradient — and what that implies for the cost-and-limits mechanism the report proposes (p. 13) — is not shown.
14. **Claude Code as its own surface.** Claude Code is named as the driver of the API's task fragmentation (p. 6) and is inside the API sample (footnote 1, p. 11), but no Claude Code cut is reported anywhere, so the mechanism claim in claim 4 above is untested against the alternative that non-coding API traffic diversified.
15. **Third-party API traffic.** Bedrock and Vertex are excluded by definition (footnote 1, p. 4). The report's platform-migration story therefore cannot rule out migration to routes it does not observe.
16. **The two doubling API workflows.** Named, with a growth filter, but with no levels, no shares, no counts and no significance — and no statement of how many task families passed the filter and were not highlighted.
17. **Automation in the API.** The Discussion's summary that augmentation "increased slightly in both Claude.ai and API traffic" (p. 2) sits beside the body's "automation decreased sharply in the 1P API data" (p. 7), evidenced only in the appendix; the report does not reconcile "slightly" with "sharply" or test whether the API shift is composition (Claude Code call-splitting) rather than behaviour.
18. **MDEs and power.** No sample sizes, standard errors or minimum detectable effects are given for Table 2.1, Figure 2.1, Figure 2.2 or Figure 2.3, so the several near-zero published differences (feedback loop +0.5 pp, AI autonomy -0.6%) cannot be read as nulls or as noise.

**Constructs used without validation**
19. **Task success.** The headline construct of the report is "Claude's assessment of whether the conversation was successful" (p. 18), defined in the previous report. It is never validated against any human-judged or user-reported outcome, and the report does not consider that a model judging its own success may score sophisticated prompts as successful more readily — which would produce the whole tenure result without any learning.
20. **Tenure as days since signup.** No usage intensity, no gap between signups and active use, no account-sharing or multi-account behaviour, no distinction between a user who has used Claude daily for six months and one who signed up six months ago and returned in February. The report's own survivorship caveat (p. 16) is stated but not bounded.
21. **The primitives.** Human education years, AI education years, AI autonomy (1–5), human time, human-and-AI time and human-can't-do-alone are model-assigned ratings of a conversation; their definitions sit in the appendix and no validation of any of them appears in this report. Differences of 0.02 on a 1–5 autonomy scale and 0.29 years of education are reported as statistically significant without any statement of rating reliability.
22. **Wage-based task value.** Task value maps a conversation to US occupational wages via O\*NET, which assumes the task performed on Claude is the O\*NET task, that US wages price it, and (per the Fig. 1.4 axis) that May 2024 OEWS wages can be expressed in February 2026 dollars by an unstated deflator. Occupation in this data is inferred from the task, not from the user, so none of the occupational cuts is a statement about who was using Claude.
23. **Use case (work / personal / coursework).** The classifier carries the entire diversification story (p. 6) and the tenure story (Table 2.1) and is never validated; the three shares also do not sum consistently across figures (99% in Feb 2026 in Fig. 1.2 against 100% in Nov 2025).
24. **"Model class" as a proxy for demand for intelligence.** The report treats Opus selection as revealed demand for capability while acknowledging default-model effects and usage limits (p. 13); it does not observe which conversations began on a default and were never switched.
