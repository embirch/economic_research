## Claims

Numbers are transcribed as published. Each claim carries its section or chart reference and the comparison it rests on. Percentages taken from chart alt text are marked `(alt text)`.

1. **Scope: 11 of 24 framework behaviours are observable.** "Of these 24 behaviors, 11 (listed in the graph below) are directly observable when humans interact with Claude on Claude.ai or Claude Code. The other 13 (including things like being honest about AI's role in work, or considering the consequences of sharing AI-generated output), happen outside Claude.ai's chat interface, so they're much harder for us to track." (§Measuring AI fluency.) *Comparison: observable versus unobservable behaviours within a fixed 24-item taxonomy. No count of how many of the 24 fall in each of the four competencies is published.*
2. **Sample.** "we used our privacy-preserving analysis tool to study 9,830 conversations that included several back-and-forths with Claude on Claude.ai during a 7-day window in January 2026" (§Measuring AI fluency); the window is given in fn 2 as "a one-week sample (January 20–26, 2026)". *Comparison: none — this is the frame for every other claim. **Host discrepancy:** the anthropic.com rendering's fn 2 reads "January 20–26, 2025", a year earlier than its own body text; the live Academy page reads 2026. Treated as a typo in the anthropic.com copy.*
3. **The headline: the commonest expression of fluency is augmentative.** "In line with our recent Economic Index, we find that the most common expression of AI fluency is *augmentative*—treating AI as a thought partner, rather than delegating work entirely." (§introduction.) *Comparison: augmentative use against "delegating work entirely", and implicitly against the Economic Index's collaboration facet. **No measurement of automation or augmentation appears anywhere in the report** — the claim is carried entirely by the prevalence of iteration and refinement (Claim 5) and the report never maps a behaviour onto the Index's collaboration categories.*
4. **Augmentative conversations show more than double the fluency behaviours.** "In fact, these conversations exhibit more than double the number of AI fluency behaviors than quick, back-and-forth chats." (§introduction.) *Comparison: augmentative conversations against "quick, back-and-forth chats". The body's corresponding measurement (Claim 8) compares iterative against **non**-iterative conversations, so the introduction's contrast class — "quick, back-and-forth chats" — is a looser gloss than what was measured; back-and-forth is the defining feature of the high-fluency group, not the low one.*
5. **Prevalence of each of the 11 behaviours across the sample.** Ranked most to least common, all eleven `(alt text)`, Chart A: iterates and refines 85.7%; clarifies goal before asking for help 51.1%; provides examples of what good looks like 41.1%; specifies format and structure needed 30%; sets interaction mode 30%; communicates tone and style preferences 22.7%; identifies when AI might be missing context 20.3%; defines audience for the output 17.6%; questions when AI reasoning doesn't hold up 15.8%; consults AI on approach before execution 10.1%; checks facts and claims that matter 8.7%. Caption: "**Prevalence of each AI fluency behavioral indicator across 9,830 Claude.ai conversations, ranked from most to least common and color-coded by competency.**" *Comparison: the eleven behaviours against each other. Only two of these eleven numbers appear in the body prose — 85.7% (Claim 6) and 30% (Claim 12); the other nine are recoverable from the page only as alt text.*
6. **Iteration and refinement is present in 85.7% of conversations.** "85.7% of the conversations in our sample exhibited iteration and refinement: building on previous exchanges to refine the user's work, rather than accepting the first response and moving to a new task." (§Results/iteration.) *Comparison: within-sample share; and against the residual 14.3% (n=1,406) that did not iterate.*
7. **Every other behaviour is more common where the user iterates.** Caption: "**Behavioral indicator prevalence in conversations where the user iterates and refines (n=8,424) versus conversations without iteration and refinement (n=1,406). All behaviors are substantially more prevalent in conversations with iteration and refinement.**" Chart B pairs, iterating versus not `(alt text)`: clarifies goal 54.5% vs 30.9% (+23.6pp); provides examples 44.4% vs 21.8% (+22.6pp); identifies missing context 22.8% vs 5.7% (+17.1pp); specifies format 32.4% vs 16.1% (+16.3pp); questions reasoning 17.9% vs 3.2% (+14.7pp). *Comparison: iterative against non-iterative conversations, on five of the eleven behaviours. The caption says "All behaviors" are more prevalent; the chart shows five. The other five non-iteration behaviours are not published for this split.*
8. **Counts of behaviours: 2.67 against 1.33.** "On average, conversations with iteration and refinement exhibit 2.67 additional fluency behaviors—roughly double the non-iterative rate of 1.33." (§Results/iteration.) *Comparison: mean number of behaviours per conversation, iterative against non-iterative. The wording is ambiguous — "2.67 **additional**" against "the non-iterative **rate** of 1.33" — and only one reading reconciles with Chart A (see Verification, arithmetic check 4): 2.67 and 1.33 are both counts of non-iteration behaviours.*
9. **Ratios on the two evaluative behaviours.** "Conversations with iteration and refinement are 5.6x more likely to involve users questioning Claude's reasoning, and 4x more likely to see them identify missing context." 10. **12.3% of conversations involved artifacts.** "12.3% of conversations in our sample involved artifacts, including code, documents, interactive tools, and other outputs." (§Results/artifacts.) *Comparison: within-sample share; n=1,209 against n=8,621 non-artifact conversations (caption of Chart C).*
11. **In artifact conversations the directive behaviours rise.** "these conversations are more likely to see users clarify their goal (+14.7pp), specify a format (+14.5pp), provide examples (+13.4pp), and iterate (+9.7pp) compared to non-artifact conversations." ## Definitions (verbatim)

Every definitional statement on the page, quoted with its location. Where a construct is used without definition, the absence is recorded.

**Fluency / AI fluency.** The report never gives a one-sentence definition. These are the only definitional statements:

- "we wanted to explore this further, and to understand how people using AI develop "fluency" with this technology over time." (§introduction; the inner straight double quotes are the source's on the Academy page, curly on the anthropic.com rendering.)
- "In this report, we begin answering that question. We track the presence or absence of a taxonomy of behaviors that we take to represent AI fluency across a large sample of anonymized conversations." (§introduction.)
- "To quantify AI fluency, we use the 4D AI Fluency Framework, developed by Professors Rick Dakan and Joseph Feller in collaboration with Anthropic. This framework helps us define 24 specific behaviors that we take to exemplify safe and effective human-AI collaboration." (§Measuring AI fluency.)
- "As with all skills, AI fluency is a matter of degree—for most of us, it's possible to develop our techniques much further." (§Developing your own AI fluency.)
- Note the construction: fluency is *defined by* the taxonomy — "behaviors that we take to represent AI fluency", "that we take to exemplify safe and effective human-AI collaboration" — with no external criterion, no outcome and no validity claim. The framework is the definition.

**The 11 behavioural indicators.** The report's only full list of the eleven is the **alt text of Chart A**, so every label below is marked `(alt text)` and none is quoted as prose or caption. Labels, in published rank order: "iterates and refines"; "clarifies goal before asking for help"; "provides examples of what good looks like"; "specifies format and structure needed"; "sets interaction mode"; "communicates tone and style preferences"; "identifies when AI might be missing context"; "defines audience for the output"; "questions when AI reasoning doesn't hold up"; "consults AI on approach before execution"; "checks facts and claims that matter" `(alt text, all eleven)`. The body text glosses six of them, and those glosses are the only prose definitions on the page:

1. **Iterates and refines** — "building on previous exchanges to refine the user's work, rather than accepting the first response and moving to a new task" (§Results/iteration). Also referred to as "iterate" (§Results/artifacts) and "refines and iterates" `(alt text, Chart C)`.
2. **Clarifies goal before asking for help** — used in prose only as "clarify their goal" (§Results/artifacts) and "clarifies goal" `(alt text)`. No definition.
3. **Provides examples of what good looks like** — prose "provide examples" (§Results/artifacts). No definition. (The companion discussion guide glosses it as "examples of what good output looks like"; that is the guide's wording, not the report's.)
4. **Specifies format and structure needed** — prose "specify a format" (§Results/artifacts). No definition.
5. **Sets interaction mode** — "users tell Claude how they'd like it to interact with them", illustrated by "instructions like, "Push back if my assumptions are wrong," "Walk me through your reasoning before giving me the answer," or, "Tell me what you're uncertain about."" (§Developing your own AI fluency). This is the fullest behaviour definition on the page.
6. **Communicates tone and style preferences** — no prose mention anywhere; label only `(alt text)`.
7. **Identifies when AI might be missing context** — prose "identify missing context" (§introduction, §Results). No definition.
8. **Defines audience for the output** — no prose mention anywhere; label only `(alt text)`.
9. **Questions when AI reasoning doesn't hold up** — "question the model's reasoning by asking it to explain its rationale" (§Results/artifacts); also "questioning Claude's reasoning" (§Results/iteration). The operationalisation is therefore *asking for a rationale*, not disagreeing.
10. **Consults AI on approach before execution** — no prose mention anywhere; label only `(alt text)`.
11. **Checks facts and claims that matter** — prose "check facts" (§Results/artifacts), and in §Limitations "fact-checking Claude's claims against their own knowledge" is named as the *unobservable* version of it. No definition of what counts as a fact-check inside the conversation.

**The competencies (the "4 Ds").** The report names three competency groups and never lists their membership:

- "we found substantially higher rates of behaviors that fall within the broader themes of "description" and "delegation."" (§Results/artifacts.)
- "But this directiveness doesn't correspond with greater levels of evaluation or discernment." and "all three discernment behaviors decrease" (§Results/artifacts and Chart C caption) — fixing **discernment = 3 behaviours**: identifies missing context, checks facts, questions reasoning. The split of the remaining eight between description and delegation is not published.
- Chart A is "color-coded by competency" (caption), the three competencies being named in its alt text as "(Description, Delegation, Discernment)" `(alt text)`.
- The fourth D is not named in the report. The linked Academy collection page (fetched separately, 2026-09-16) describes the course as "the 4D framework: Delegation, Description, Discernment, and Diligence." **Diligence therefore contributes no observable indicator**, which is consistent with §Limitations: "All behaviors related to the responsible and ethical use of AI outputs occur outside of these conversations, and are not captured." That mapping is the wiki author's inference from two fetched pages, not a statement of the report.

**The Index — how it is constructed.** The report's only construction statement:

- "For this study, we focused on the 11 directly observable behaviors. … We then measured the presence or absence of the 11 behaviors; each conversation could display evidence of multiple behaviors. We assessed the reliability of our sample by checking whether our results were consistent across each day of the week, and across the different languages in our sample (we found that they were). This, finally, gave us the AI Fluency Index: a baseline measurement of how people collaborate with AI today, and a foundation for tracking how those behaviors evolve over time as models change." (§Measuring AI fluency.)
- **There is no composite score, no weighting, no scale and no aggregation formula.** "The AI Fluency Index" denotes the set of eleven prevalence rates, plus the mean count of behaviours per conversation used in Claim 8. No index value is published for any conversation, user, task, occupation or country. Nothing on the page is called a score.

**Artifacts.** Two non-identical descriptions, in the same report:

- "when AI produces artifacts—including apps, code, documents, or interactive tools" (§introduction).
- "12.3% of conversations in our sample involved artifacts, including code, documents, interactive tools, and other outputs." (§Results/artifacts.) *The introduction's list opens with "apps", the body's closes with "and other outputs"; neither is an exhaustive rule, and the product feature is not defined. On the anthropic.com rendering the word "artifacts" is hyperlinked to a claude.ai artifacts catalogue page; on the Academy page it is plain text.*

**Sample rules and classification (fn 1 and fn 2, quoted in full).**

- fn 1: "When researching how people use AI models, protecting user privacy is paramount. For this project, we used our privacy-preserving analysis tool, which enables bottom-up discovery of AI usage patterns by distilling user conversations into high-level usage summaries, such as "troubleshoot code" or "explain economic concepts." For this analysis, we ran 11 separate binary classifiers (one per behavioral indicator) using Claude Sonnet 4 for behavioral classification and Claude Haiku 3.5 for language detection. This means a single conversation could indicate multiple AI fluency behavioral indicators. Conversations were filtered to substantive exchanges with multiple back-and-forths using a screener that excluded greetings, single-word exchanges, test messages, and pure chitchat. Manual review of 200 chats that were screened out indicated that chats of this nature did not qualify for any AI Fluency indicators, so we feel confident that the screener did not influence the relative rankings of AI fluency behaviors observed in the study. - fn 2: "Behavioral indicators were calculated across a one-week sample (January 20–26, 2026) and held stable day-to-day, with most behaviors varying by only 1–5 percentage points. Rates were also consistent across six languages (English, French, Spanish, Chinese, Japanese, and German), with most behaviors varying by 3 percentage points or fewer across language groups. - Unit of analysis, stated only implicitly: "For each conversation in our sample, we classify each behavior as either present or absent." (§Limitations.) **The unit is the conversation. No user count, no conversations-per-user figure and no clustering rule appears anywhere.**

## Data and methods

In the wiki author's words, with references to the page.

- **Data.** 9,830 Claude.ai conversations from a seven-day window, 20–26 January 2026, screened to "substantive exchanges with multiple back-and-forths" (fn 1). Claude.ai only — Claude Code is named as a surface where the eleven behaviours *would* be observable (§Measuring AI fluency) but is excluded from the study and appears only as a preliminary, unquantified consistency remark (§Looking ahead). The first-party API is never mentioned. No data or code is released; there is no Hugging Face release, no repository and no appendix for this report.
- **Instrument.** Eleven independent binary classifiers, one per behavioural indicator, run with **Claude Sonnet 4**, plus **Claude Haiku 3.5** for language detection (fn 1), inside Clio, the privacy-preserving analysis system (`clio-insights-2024-12`). Classification is at conversation level and non-exclusive: "a single conversation could indicate multiple AI fluency behavioral indicators" (fn 1). Note that the classifier model (Sonnet 4) is older than the January 2026 window the conversations come from.
- **Taxonomy.** The 4D AI Fluency Framework of Dakan and Feller, "developed … in collaboration with Anthropic", which "helps us define 24 specific behaviors"; 11 are used, 13 are not (§Measuring AI fluency). The framework is external to the Economic Index taxonomy: no O\*NET task, no SOC occupation, no automation/augmentation collaboration pattern, no AI-autonomy primitive and no AI Usage Index appears in this report. The two frameworks are connected only rhetorically, by the "in line with our recent Economic Index" sentence (Claim 3).
- **Estimates.** Three quantities and nothing else: (i) the share of conversations exhibiting each behaviour, overall and within the two splits; (ii) the mean number of behaviours per conversation, for the iteration split only; (iii) pp differences and two risk ratios between split groups. All comparisons are between two unconditional subgroup means.
- **Splits.** Two, both defined by a classifier output rather than by anything exogenous: iteration present/absent (n=8,424 / n=1,406) and artifact present/absent (n=1,209 / n=8,621). The iteration split therefore uses one of the eleven index components as the explanatory variable for the other ten — the "strongest correlate" claim is a correlation among classifier outputs on the same conversation.
- **Statistics.** None. No standard error, no confidence interval, no significance test, no regression, no control variable, no fixed effect, no weighting, no clustering, no multiple-comparison correction, no power calculation and no sensitivity analysis appears anywhere in the report. The report is explicit that the design supports no causal reading (§Limitations, "Correlational findings").
- **Robustness.** Exactly two checks, both in fn 2 and both reported qualitatively: stability across the seven days (with one worked example) and stability across six languages. Neither is tabulated; no per-day or per-language rate other than the Saturday and weekday-peak pair is published.
- **Human validation.** One exercise: manual review of 200 conversations that the screener **excluded** (fn 1). No human audit of the eleven classifiers' positive or negative calls, and no agreement statistic of any kind.
- **Relation to the Economic Index public data.** There is none at the data level. This report's sample, window, instrument and taxonomy are all outside the Economic Index releases, and no Index file is used or cited. Whether a Claude.ai release window overlaps 20–26 January 2026, and whether any released Index file carries a turn count, an artifact flag or any conversation-level behavioural field, is **not settled from this page and is not assumed here**; it is a question for the data steward (`room/lead-2026-09-16-wiki-ai-fluency-index-2026-02.md` records that this file needs it). The programme lead has not opened any data file for this entry.

## Limitations (verbatim)

The report has a section headed `Limitations`, introduced "This research comes with important caveats:". Its five bullets, quoted in full:

- "**Sample limitations:** Our sample reflects Claude.ai users who engaged in multi-turn conversations during a single week in January 2026. Since we think this is still relatively early on in the diffusion of AI tools, these users likely skew towards early adopters who are already comfortable with AI—i.e., who may not represent the broader population. Our sample should be understood as providing a baseline for *this* population, not as a universal benchmark. Because the data comes from a single week, it is also unable to capture any seasonal or longitudinal effects. And because it's focused on Claude.ai, we don't capture how users interact with other AI platforms."

## What it did not test

The wiki author's inference, not the report's own statements. Each item is something a reader of this page cannot learn from it.

1. **The classifiers are never validated.** Eleven binary classifiers, run once, on Claude Sonnet 4, with no reported precision, recall, agreement rate, human audit of positive calls, inter-rater statistic, or sensitivity to the classifier prompt. The single human exercise reviewed 200 conversations the screener had **excluded** (fn 1) — it bounds the screener, not the instrument. Every number in the report is a classifier output, so the whole result set rests on unmeasured classification accuracy. This matters most where prevalence is lowest: "checks facts and claims that matter" at 8.7% `(alt text)` is the behaviour most exposed to false negatives, and it is one of the three that carry the artifact finding.
2. **No uncertainty on any number.** Not one interval, standard error or test appears. *Illustrative calculation by the wiki author, from the published n's and the alt-text rates, treating conversations as an independent simple random sample* (an assumption the report neither makes nor licenses): the artifact-gap standard errors are about 0.7pp for checks facts, 1.1pp for questions reasoning and 1.1pp for identifies missing context, so the published −3.7, −3.1 and −5.2pp gaps are roughly 5, 3 and 4.6 standard errors. The artifact finding is not fragile on sample size alone — but the report never says so, never states a minimum detectable effect, and the same arithmetic shows that a gap of 1–2pp on these behaviours would not have been separable from noise. Nothing in the report tells the reader which of its many small differences are distinguishable from zero.
3. **The unit is the conversation, never the user.** No user count, no distribution of conversations per user, no clustering, no repeat-use structure. So every result is silent on whether "85.7% of conversations iterate" means most users iterate most of the time or a heavy-using minority iterates constantly. The report's own framing question is about *individuals* developing skills; its evidence is about conversations. The promised cohort analysis is precisely the missing design.
4. **No trend, despite being called an Index.** One week. The word "Index" here denotes a baseline, not a series: there is no earlier wave, no comparison to the two prior Education Reports, and no attempt to place the eleven rates against any earlier measurement. Whether fluency is rising or falling is the report's stated future work, not its content.
5. **Iteration is not separated from conversation length or task complexity.** The iteration split compares conversations that, by construction, have more turns to work with — more turns mean more opportunities for any other behaviour to be observed and classified as present. No turn count is published, nothing is conditioned on length, and the report names task complexity as a possible common cause without measuring it. The headline "+23.6pp" style gaps (Claim 7) are consistent with a pure exposure-to-observation effect, and that alternative is never bounded.
6. **The artifact comparison holds nothing constant.** Artifact conversations differ from non-artifact conversations in task, domain, surface, user and length. The report's own second explanation — artifact tasks are ones "where factual precision matters less" — is a composition story, and no composition adjustment of any kind is attempted. Nor is the artifact effect shown separately for code against documents against interactive tools, which is the split that would discriminate the explanations.
7. **The three explanations of the artifact effect are not distinguished.** Off-conversation evaluation (running the code), lower stakes for precision, and a genuine polish-induced drop in scrutiny all predict the published pattern. Distinguishing them needs data the report does not use — outcomes, follow-up conversations, revisions, or user-level linkage — and the report says as much without bounding any of the three.
8. **No outcome is measured.** Nothing in the report establishes that any of the eleven behaviours produces a better answer, a faster task, a correct fact, a working artifact, or a higher-quality piece of work. "Fluency" is defined by the taxonomy, and the taxonomy is asserted to "exemplify safe and effective human-AI collaboration". The prescriptive section and the closing claim that evaluation skill "will become more valuable rather than less" therefore rest on a construct that the report never validates against any output, error rate, wage or productivity measure.
9. **The link to the Economic Index is rhetorical, not measured.** Claim 3 asserts augmentative use "in line with our recent Economic Index", but no automation/augmentation classification is run, no collaboration pattern is assigned, and no primitive is computed. Whether a conversation classified as iterating and refining would be classified as Task Iteration or Learning under the Index's collaboration facet — the mapping that would make the two literatures commensurable — is untested. (Terminology reminder from `/mnt/memory/standards/terminology.md`: collaboration is the name of the whole facet, and AI autonomy is a separate construct from automation; this report engages with neither.)
10. **No occupational, task or geographic cut.** No O\*NET task, no SOC group, no country, no US state, no income proxy, no sector. So the report cannot say whether fluency differs between software work and writing, between work and personal use, or between rich and poor countries — the dimensions every Economic Index release is organised around, and the dimensions an economics reader needs.
11. **No work / study / personal split.** The Index's own primitive for use context is absent, so "are individuals developing the skills to use it well?" is answered over a pooled sample of work and non-work conversations, and the economically relevant subset is never isolated.
12. **Plan tier and surface coverage are unstated.** Which Claude.ai plans are in the sample is never said — unusually, since every Economic Index release specifies its plan coverage. Claude Code is excluded from the measurement while being named as observable; the first-party API is not mentioned. The one Claude Code statement (Claim 17) carries no number, so "consistency" is unfalsifiable as published.
13. **The screener's magnitude is unpublished.** The number (or share) of conversations removed by the screener is never given, so the reader cannot tell whether 9,830 is most of the week's multi-turn conversations or a small residue. The sampling procedure behind 9,830 — random draw, stratification, population size — is not described at all.
14. **The 13 unobservable behaviours are untested by design, and the report calls them the most consequential.** So the Index measures the visible third-to-half of its own framework, Diligence contributes nothing, and no bound is offered on how the missing behaviours would move any published rate. The name "AI Fluency Index" nevertheless generalises over the whole framework.
15. **Binary coding is never stress-tested.** No graded or partial coding is tried, no threshold is varied, and no check is reported on whether behaviours overlap (a message asking for a rationale might trip both "questions reasoning" and "consults AI on approach"). The report flags the nuance loss and leaves it unquantified — while the mean-count statistic of Claim 8 sums eleven possibly overlapping indicators as if they were distinct.
16. **The robustness checks are reported without their numbers.** Day-of-week and language stability are asserted in fn 2 with one worked example. No per-day table, no per-language table, no per-language n, no share of the sample by language. And the one number given cuts against the summary: the Saturday-to-weekday spread on iteration (81.4% against 87.9%) is 6.5pp, wider than the stated "1–5 percentage points" band and wider than every artifact gap the report treats as a finding. Whether the artifact and iteration *effects* are stable across days or languages is not reported at all — only the levels.
17. **The model the users were talking to is not recorded.** The Index is offered as "a foundation for tracking how those behaviors evolve over time as models change", yet the report does not say which Claude models served the January 2026 conversations, nor how much of a future change would be attributable to the model rather than to users. The classifier is itself a model (Sonnet 4), and classifier drift across future waves is not addressed.
18. **No benchmark for what a good rate would be.** 8.7% fact-checking is presented as an improvement opportunity; no standard, no expert baseline, no task-conditional target and no comparison population establishes what the rate ought to be. Equally, 30% interaction-mode setting is called "only 30%" with no criterion.
19. **Selection is named but never bounded.** §Limitations conjectures an early-adopter skew; the report offers no comparison to the general population, to non-multi-turn users, or to any external survey, and no bound on how the skew would move the eleven rates. The direction of the bias on the artifact result in particular is not discussed.
20. **The two cross-references are never made commensurable.** The coding-skills study (an RCT on skill formation) and the fourth Economic Index report (complexity and Claude's failure rate) are invoked as corroboration without a shared measure, sample or window. Whether the artifact effect and the coding-skills pattern are the same phenomenon is untested.
