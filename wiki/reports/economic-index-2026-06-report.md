**Analysis windows inside the report** (these are *not* the released monthly aggregates):
- Chapter 1: "our new privacy-preserving telemetry, which continuously samples a slice of conversations every day" (p.4); the window is given only indirectly — "The sample period for this report covers tax filing deadlines for people in the United States" (p.7) and Figure 1.4 spans April 14–16 and "the average day in May".
- Chapter 2: "Data in this chapter cover chat and Cowork conversations sampled between April 10 and June 10, 2026." (p.18, ch.2 fn 1). Wages: "Wages are from the BLS OEWS, May 2025 release." (same footnote).
- Chapter 3: survey fielded from April 2026; "We link survey responses to usage data from mid-May to early June" (p.19).

**Sampling design change.** Hourly/daily continuous sampling replaces the fixed seven-day draws of the earlier waves: "in contrast to the seven-day samples each previous Economic Index report drew on" (p.4).

## Claims

Every substantive empirical claim, numbered continuously, with page and figure/table reference, the number **as published**, and the comparison the number rests on. Figure and table *data values* are not machine-readable in the PDF (all figures are images; see `## Verification`), so every number below is one Anthropic stated in body text, a caption or a footnote.

### Introduction (pp.2–3)

1. **Usage has shifted from chat to long-running agentic sessions.** "One year ago, most Claude usage took the form of a conversation between a user and an assistant. Today, with the rapid growth of Claude Code and Cowork, Claude sessions increasingly consist of long-running agentic tasks." (p.2). No number; comparison is one year earlier versus today, asserted, not measured in this report.
2. **Chat transcripts no longer suffice as a measurement instrument.** "A chat transcript no longer fully captures how people are using AI, and our methods for studying Claude's economic impacts have had to rapidly adapt." (p.2). Motivating claim for the three pipeline changes.
3. **Preview: work queries fall less on weekends in the highest-paid occupations.** "Work-related queries subside on the weekend, though less dramatically in the most highly paid occupations" (p.2). Comparison: weekend versus weekday, by occupation wage rank. Delivered as claim 15 below.
4. **Preview: sleep advice peaks around 5 a.m.** "people tend to ask for the news in the morning, and sleep advice peaks around 5 a.m." (p.2). **This conflicts with the chapter-1 text, which says 3 a.m. (claim 13).** Recorded as a discrepancy, not resolved by the report.
5. **Preview: compute scales with the value of the work.** "We also see that more compute is associated with more valuable artifacts; the tokens a given output consumes rise with the estimated value of the work." (pp.2–3). Delivered as claims 29–34.
6. **Preview: the most automated users are the most optimistic.** "people who use Claude in the most automated way expect AI to take on more of their tasks in the next year, yet feel the most optimistic about what that means for their work, anticipating positive impacts on pay, job security, and meaning." (p.3). Delivered as claims 69, 74–78.

### Chapter 1 — Cadences: hourly sampling and time-of-day (pp.4–8)

7. **Personal-use share rises from ~35% on weekdays to just under 50% on weekends.** "The share of chat and Cowork conversations categorized as personal use spikes from around 35% on weekdays to just under 50% on weekends during the sample period (Figure 1.1)." (p.4, Figure 1.1). Comparison: daily share, weekend days versus weekdays, within chat and Cowork over the sample period.
8. **The weekend shift is compositional in a specific direction.** "Outside the workweek, users' conversations shift from business correspondence, marketing copy, and slide decks to emotional support, medical questions, and investment advice." (p.4). Comparison: weekday versus weekend request-cluster mix. No numbers given for any of the six named clusters.
9. **The weekend shift is largest in high-income countries.** "This shift is biggest for high-income countries." (p.4). Comparison: country income groups. No number, no threshold for "high-income", no figure cited.
10. **Claude Code and the 1P API show the same weekend pattern from a lower base.** "A similar pattern is present in Claude Code and the 1P API traffic (i.e., API traffic routed directly through Anthropic), though both have lower baseline rates of personal use." (p.5; Figure 1.1's caption covers "Claude.ai, Claude Desktop, Claude Code, and 1P API"). Comparison: surfaces against each other, in level and in weekend swing. No numbers.
11. **Claude Code clusters that fall most on weekends: backend architecture, API debugging, data storage.** "On weekends, the Claude Code usage clusters that fall the most include backend architecture, API debugging, and data storage." (p.5). Comparison: weekend versus weekday share by detailed request cluster, Claude Code only. No numbers.
12. **Claude Code clusters that rise most on weekends: AI agent design, quant trading, gaming.** "Those that increase the most include AI agent design, quant trading, and gaming." (p.5). Same comparison as claim 11.
13. **Business-starting conversations peak on Saturday and Sunday, while job-application activity falls with other work.** "Across countries, conversations related to starting a business are highest on Saturday and Sunday. However, job application activities drop on the weekend along with other work-related tasks." (p.5, with the operational definitions in ch.1 fn 5, p.8). Comparison: day-of-week share of a named cluster list versus the rest of the week; "across countries" asserted without a country breakdown.
14. **News requests peak at 7 a.m. local time; business correspondence traces the workday with a slight 10–11 a.m. peak.** "People ask for news at 7 a.m. local time. Business correspondence (e.g., email drafting) traces the arc of the workday, with a slight peak at 10–11 a.m." (p.6, Figure 1.2). Comparison: normalised hourly share of a cluster against that cluster's own 24-hour average, chat and Cowork only, local time inferred from IP.
15. **Recipe requests are 2.3 times more frequent at 6 p.m. than at the daily average.** "One of the biggest spikes is recipe requests, which are 2.3 times more frequent at 6 p.m. compared to the average." (p.6, Figure 1.2). Comparison: 6 p.m. local hour against the cluster's own all-hours average. The single largest published hourly ratio in the report.
16. **Media recommendations concentrate in the evening; sleep advice concentrates just before dawn — "around 3 a.m." in the chapter text.** "The hourly data captures within-day patterns—people most often ask for sleep advice around 3 a.m. and for recipes around 6 p.m." (p.4); "Media recommendations are most concentrated in the evening, while people seek sleep advice in the few hours just before dawn." (p.6, Figure 1.2). **The PDF's introduction (p.2) and the entire web version say 5 a.m. instead of 3 a.m.** The report does not reconcile them.
17. **On nights and weekends, work-related conversations shift toward higher-wage occupations; the bottom two wage quartiles shrink.** "On nights and weekends, when people do turn to Claude for work, the tasks skew toward higher-wage occupations (Figure 1.3). … In contrast, tasks related to jobs in the bottom two quartiles—like telemarketing and clerical work—fall to a smaller share of total conversations." (p.7, Figure 1.3). Comparison, per the caption: "the percent change in the share of work-related tasks coming from the specified wage quartile on nights and weekends versus weekday working hours. Wage quartiles are calculated using BLS data, weighted by number of transcripts." No percent changes are given in the body text.
18. **Robustness: the night/weekend wage tilt is not driven by computer and mathematical tasks.** "This pattern isn't driven exclusively by computer and mathematical tasks: when we removed those occupations from the analysis in a robustness check, higher-quartile tasks still increased on nights and weekends." (p.7). Comparison: same night/weekend versus weekday-hours contrast, with SOC 15 occupations dropped. No number; result stated qualitatively.
19. **Tax-related clusters were eight times as common on April 14 as on the average day in May, stayed about as high on April 15, and fell sharply on April 16.** "On April 14, tax-related clusters were eight times as common as on the average day in May and remained about as high on April 15. On April 16, they dropped sharply." (p.7, Figure 1.4). Comparison: daily share of tax-related clusters against the May daily average; the figure splits "the US and the rest of the world" (Figure 1.4 caption, p.8). The 8× ratio is quoted for the pooled or US series — the body text does not say which.

### Chapter 2 — Artifacts: the new output classifier (pp.9–18)

20. **The artifact classifier finds a concrete output in 93% of Claude conversations.** "Our classifier identified 93% of Claude conversations as producing an artifact (Figure 2.1)." (p.9, Figure 2.1). Comparison: share of chat and Cowork conversations assigned any artifact label versus the "None" residual, which is defined as a catch-all (ch.2 fn 2, p.18).
21. **The three most common artifacts are explanations (17%), documents and reports (15%) and guidance (11%).** "The most common artifacts are explanations (17% of conversations), documents and reports (15%), and guidance (11%)." (p.9, Figure 2.1). Comparison: share of all chat and Cowork conversations. Figure 2.1 shows "the twelve most common output types in Claude conversations" (caption, p.9).
22. **Conversational outputs and written deliverables are each about a third of conversations; code and technical work about a sixth.** "Conversational outputs (like explanations or guidance) and written deliverables (like documents or presentations) each account for about a third of conversations; code and technical work (like apps or scripts) for about a sixth." (p.9). Comparison: three author-defined groupings of the 30+ artifact labels against the whole. The groupings themselves are not defined label-by-label in the report.
23. **The 1P API artifact mix is not shown in the report.** "The 1P API mix is in the Appendix." (Figure 2.1 caption, p.9). Recorded as a deferral, not a claim; see `wiki/reports/economic-index-2026-06-appendix.md`.
24. **Creative writing, guidance and recipes are over 80% personal.** "More than 80% of conversations producing creative writing, guidance, and recipes were classified as personal." (p.10, Figure 2.2). Comparison: use-case split (work / personal / coursework) within an artifact category.
25. **Within creative writing, the 13% that is work-related looks different from the personal remainder.** "Personal creative writing, for instance, is dominated by fanfiction, worldbuilding, and poetry; the 13% that is work-related is mostly in the form of short-form video scripts, screenwriting, and speeches." (p.10). Comparison: request-cluster composition within one artifact category, split by use case.
26. **The most work-tilted artifacts: database queries 82%, blogs or articles 81%, marketing content 80%.** "Categories most likely to be work-related include creating marketing content (80%), creating blogs or articles (81%), and writing database queries (82%)." (p.10, Figure 2.2). Comparison: work share within artifact category.
27. **Plans/strategies and translation split roughly evenly between work and personal.** "Many outputs are equally likely to be used for personal and work reasons, including creating plans or strategies (44% work-related, 49% personal) or translation (42% work, 44% personal)." (p.10, Figure 2.2). Comparison: work versus personal share within artifact category (residual is coursework).
28. **Within the balanced categories the content still differs by use case.** "the most common types of personal planning artifacts include travel itineraries and workout schedules, while work-related plans most often pertain to entrepreneurial or content strategies." (p.10). No numbers.
29. **Coursework-characteristic artifacts: academic papers and theses, educational materials, math-related queries.** "Finally, artifacts that are characteristic of coursework include creating academic papers and theses, educational materials and math-related queries, though a non-negligible share of each falls into both work and personal categories." (p.10, Figure 2.2). No numbers for the coursework shares.
30. **Flipping the conditioning: work conversations produce documents and reports 20%, explanations 9%, email drafts 7%, analyses and summaries 6%.** "Work conversations most often produce documents and reports (20%), followed by explanations (9%), email drafts (7%), and analyses and summaries (6%)." (p.12, following Figure 2.2). Comparison: artifact distribution conditional on use case (the transpose of claims 24–29).
31. **Coursework conversations: documents and reports 21%, explanations 20%, educational materials 11%, academic papers 6%.** "Coursework conversations look broadly similar, with documents and reports leading there too (21%), closely followed by explanations (20%), educational materials (11%), and academic papers (6%)." (p.12). Comparison: same as claim 30, coursework column.
32. **Personal conversations rarely produce a document (6%); they produce explanations (25%) and recommendations (22%).** "In contrast—and unsurprisingly—only 6% of personal conversations produce a document. Instead, the most common results are explanations (25%) and recommendations (22%)." (p.12). Comparison: same as claim 30, personal column; contrast with the 20%/21% document share for work and coursework.
33. **Median conversation tokens rise with the median wage of the mapped occupation.** "The left panel of Figure 2.3 shows a positive relationship between the median conversation-level number of tokens and the median wage in mapped occupation." (p.12, Figure 2.3 left). Comparison: per the caption, "Relationship between median wage for a given occupation and the typical (geometric mean) number of tokens in conversations classified to one of the tasks belonging to that occupation, normalized by overall mean. Both variables are represented on a logarithmic scale." Restricted to work-related chat and Cowork conversations. **No slope, elasticity, R² or standard error is published.**
34. **Marketing managers versus editors: roughly 2× the wage ($80 vs $37 per hour), roughly 2.5× the tokens.** "marketing managers earn roughly twice as much as editors ($80 vs. $37 per hour) and conversations mapping to their tasks consume approximately 2.5 times as many tokens." (p.12, Figure 2.3 left). Comparison: a two-occupation illustration of the gradient; wages from BLS OEWS May 2025 (ch.2 fn 1, p.18).
35. **Counter-example: pharmacists earn ~3× statistical assistants ($68 vs $24 per hour) but their mapped conversations use about one twentieth the tokens.** "Pharmacists, for example, earn nearly three times what statistical assistants do ($68 vs. $24 per hour), yet conversations mapped to pharmacist tasks use only about one twentieth as many tokens." (p.12). Published by the authors as an outlier against their own gradient.
36. **App-building conversations use more than 3× the median conversation's tokens; a typical explanation about one fifth.** "conversations about building apps use more than three times the tokens of the median conversation. On the other end of the spectrum, a typical explanation uses about a fifth of the tokens of the median conversation." (p.13, Figure 2.3 right). Comparison: per-artifact token distribution normalised by the overall median (caption: "Black line illustrates the median, box represents p25 and p75, whiskers represent p10 and p90").
37. **About 44% of the wage gradient in token consumption is explained by output mix.** "About 44% of the wage gradient in token consumption is explained by output mix—higher wage occupations are more likely to produce compute-intensive artifacts." (p.13). Comparison: the wage–token gradient with and without controlling for artifact composition. Method of the decomposition is not described in the report.
38. **Higher-wage conversations: Claude produces 1.34× as much output per turn, users take 1.53× as many turns, extended thinking is enabled in 34% versus 31% of conversations, and total tokens are 2.07× the bottom tercile.** "In conversations mapped to higher-wage occupations, Claude produces more (1.34 times as much output per turn), while users engage more (1.53 times as many turns) and enable extended thinking more frequently (34% of conversations versus 31%; Table 2.4)." (p.13); "the first row shows that a typical conversation mapped to a top-tercile occupation consumes 2.07 times as many tokens as a typical conversation mapped to a bottom-tercile occupation." (Table 2.4 caption, p.14). Comparison: top versus bottom wage tercile of mapped occupations, weighted by conversations matched, compute measures normalised by the bottom tercile's geometric means. Table 2.4's remaining cells are an image and not extractable.
39. **The authors read the co-movement as augmenting rather than displacing.** "Crucially, these move together: more production from Claude does not mean less from the user. If the human remains involved in the highest-value tasks, the pattern looks more labor-augmenting than labor-displacing." (pp.13–14). Conditional interpretation, not a measurement; also logged under open questions and conjectures.
40. **Lowest-autonomy outputs are math or calculations, translations and Q&As; highest are apps and websites, games and presentations.** "the lowest-autonomy outputs are math or calculations, translations, and Q&As. High-autonomy tasks are those that require selection among many possible choices, e.g., creating apps and websites, games, or presentations." (p.14, Figure 2.5). Comparison: mean AI autonomy (1–5) across artifact types. No per-artifact values in body text.
41. **AI autonomy is higher on Claude Code than on chat or Cowork for 26 of the 31 output types shown.** "Across almost all types of outputs (26 of 31 outputs shown) the level of AI autonomy is higher on Claude Code than chat or Cowork." (p.14, Figure 2.5). Comparison: same artifact, two surfaces, same April 10 – June 10 period.
42. **Scripts and code snippets carry 0.53 points more autonomy on Claude Code.** "conversations producing scripts and code snippets involve 0.53 points more autonomy (on average, on the 1-5 scale) when created with Claude Code than conversations producing the same output on chat or Cowork." (p.14). Comparison: within-artifact, across-surface difference in mean autonomy.
43. **The average across-surface autonomy gap is 0.37 points.** "Across all conversations the average difference in autonomy is 0.37 points, and it has two main sources." (p.14). Comparison: all Claude Code sessions versus all chat and Cowork conversations in the window.
44. **Two thirds of the gap is the same tasks executed with more delegation; one third is output mix.** "Approximately two thirds of the difference is explained by the same tasks being executed with more delegation on Claude Code. … The remaining third reflects the different mix of output types across the two surfaces." (p.15). Comparison: within-artifact versus between-artifact decomposition of the 0.37-point gap.
45. **Blog posts: 13 rounds of back-and-forth on chat and Cowork versus a single human prompt on Claude Code (medians).** "The median chat and Cowork conversation producing a blog post or an article involves 13 rounds of back-and-forth, while the median blog-producing Claude Code session contains a single human prompt." (p.15). Comparison: median turn count for one artifact type on two surfaces, where "the requests and tasks behind them are similar on the two surfaces" (p.15).
46. **The one large exception is data and spreadsheets, where chat and Cowork are more autonomous than Claude Code (3.09 vs 2.74), about 70% of it compositional.** "The largest exception is data and spreadsheets, where Claude.ai conversations involve more autonomy than Claude Code (3.09 vs 2.74). This is mostly compositional: about 70% of the gap reflects a different mix of tasks." (p.18, ch.2 fn 5). Comparison: within-artifact across-surface, then decomposed. (The PDF footnote says "Claude.ai"; the web version says "chat and Cowork" — see `## Verification`.)
47. **Cowork is named as part of the chat-and-Cowork lift on that artifact.** "Cowork, where data and spreadsheet work is both over-represented and especially autonomous, accounts for part of the Claude.ai lift." (p.18, ch.2 fn 5). The only place in the report where Cowork is distinguished from chat.
48. **Model mix: 54% of Claude Code sessions are served by Opus versus 10% of chat and Cowork conversations.** "Claude Code sessions run on the most capable models far more often (54% are served by Opus, against 10% of chat and Cowork conversations)." (p.16). Comparison: model composition across surfaces.
49. **Holding the model fixed, the surface gap survives: within Sonnet, Claude Code shows 0.26 points more autonomy.** "among conversations using Sonnet, Claude Code sessions still show 0.26 points more autonomy, suggesting that the product used is likely more important than the underlying model." (p.16). Comparison: within-model, across-surface difference; 0.26 against the unconditional 0.37 of claim 43.
50. **Across artifacts, mean autonomy and median token use rise together, r = 0.68 on chat and Cowork.** "across artifacts, mean autonomy and median token use rise together (r = 0.68 on chat and Cowork; Appendix Figure A.2)." (p.16). Comparison: artifact-level correlation between two constructs. The scatter is in the appendix.
51. **Academic-paper prompts require more than 16 years of education; 15% of those conversations are at PhD level or above (20+ years).** "An average query resulting in an academic paper would require more than 16 years of education, roughly equivalent to bachelor's level, and 15% of these conversations are at PhD level or above (20 or more years of education)." (p.16, Figure 2.6). Comparison: prompt reading level across artifact types; the 15% is a share of academic-paper conversations.
52. **Recipe and guidance prompts require fewer than 10 years of education.** "On the other end of the spectrum are conversations resulting in recipes or guidance, where fewer than 10 years of education are required to understand the prompt." (p.16, Figure 2.6). Comparison: bottom of the same artifact ranking.
53. **Prompt and response reading levels correlate 0.87 across conversations.** "In general, artifact types with higher-reading-level outputs also have higher-reading-level prompts (a correlation of 0.87 across conversations)." (p.16). Note the mismatch between the sentence's unit (artifact types) and the stated unit of the correlation (conversations).
54. **Claude's response sits about one year of education above the prompt in almost every category; widest for image and graphics (+2.6), games (+1.9), apps and websites (+1.7).** "in almost every category, Claude's output is at a higher comprehension level than the prompt, by roughly one year of education on average. The gap is widest where users describe something to be built, such as image and graphics (+2.6 years), games (+1.9), and apps and websites (+1.7)." (p.16, Figure 2.6). Comparison: paired within-conversation difference, response minus prompt, averaged by artifact.
55. **The gap is near zero for audience-facing writing: blogs −0.1, academic papers +0.0, email +0.3.** "the gap is near zero for audience-facing writing (blogs −0.1, academic papers +0.0, email +0.3)" (p.16). Comparison: same paired difference for three artifact types. The report's only negative estimate on this measure.

### Chapter 3 — Perceptions: the first Economic Index Survey results (pp.19–30)

56. **The linked sample is about 9,700 respondents, each characterised from up to 20 randomly sampled sessions in a mid-May to early-June window, with under-five-session respondents excluded.** "We link survey responses to usage data from mid-May to early June using privacy-preserving methods. To characterize each respondent's usage patterns, we randomly sample up to 20 sessions per person within this time window (across Claude.ai, Cowork, and Claude Code, so that the mix of sessions reflects each person's typical usage across surfaces). We exclude respondents with fewer than five sessions to reduce sampling noise. Our final linked sample consists of about 9,700 survey respondents." (p.19). This is the first published Anthropic survey–usage linkage at the individual level.
57. **Expectations about the pace of future AI progress are near-uniform across respondents, even though perceptions of current capability are not.** "While people's perception of AI capabilities depends on their experience, where they live, and how exposed their job is to AI, their expectations about the pace of future progress are strikingly uniform, consistent with a 'rising tide,' in which AI capabilities improve broadly." (p.19; the web version links "rising tide" to https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.01363). Comparison: cross-respondent variance in level versus in expected change.
58. **Early-career workers report the highest AI-doable share of their work and the most concern about job loss.** "Early-career workers report that AI can do the highest share of their work and express the most concern about job loss." (p.19). Delivered in detail as claim 67.
59. **The heaviest delegators are the most optimistic about their own labour-market outcomes and think their skills are gaining value.** "the people who delegate to Claude the most are the most optimistic about their future labor market outcomes, and feel their skills are growing in value." (p.19). Delivered as claims 74 and 78.
60. **Computer and Mathematical occupations are ~30% of survey respondents against 4% of US employment, and that is comparable to their share of Claude usage.** "Computer and Mathematical occupations are the most heavily over-represented, making up roughly 30% of survey respondents—comparable to their share of Claude usage, but far above their 4% share of US employment." (p.20, Figure 3.1). Comparison: survey share versus OEWS US employment share versus Claude session share, across the 22 major SOC groups (military excluded, ch.3 fn 3).
61. **Management is 23% of respondents against a 7% employment share and only 4% of sessions.** "Management, at 23% of respondents, is also heavily over-represented relative to its 7% employment share, even though it accounts for only 4% of sessions." (p.20, Figure 3.1). The respondent-share-versus-session-share gap is the report's evidence for claim 62. Employment status of these respondents (ch.3 fn 2, p.31): "48.1% said they're employed at a company, 24.4% said they were a business owner with employees, and 21.7% said self-employed or contractor."
62. **Managers appear to use Claude for tasks other than management, and respondents name judgment and management as capabilities AI lacks.** "This gap is consistent with managers using Claude for tasks other than management itself: in the survey, judgment and management are named by many respondents (especially those with more experience) as capabilities AI lacks." (p.20). Inference from the respondent-share/session-share gap plus open-ended responses.
63. **Physical occupation groups are under-represented in the survey and in Claude sessions alike.** "Physical occupation categories like Transportation & Material Moving, Food Preparation & Serving Related, and Construction & Extraction are all under-represented in the survey, as they are in Claude sessions as well." (p.20, Figure 3.1). No numbers.
64. **Close to 6 in 10 respondents expect AI to do a larger share of their work tasks next year than today; over a third expect most or nearly all.** "Close to 6 in 10 respondents chose a higher band for next year than for today. Over a third expect AI to be able to do most or nearly all of their work tasks next year (Figure 3.2)." (p.22, Figure 3.2). Comparison: within-respondent, reported versus anticipated exposure across five bands from "almost none" to "nearly all". The Discussion restates it as "over 35% predicted that AI would be able to do most of their work" (p.32).
65. **Reported exposure is positively correlated with both observed and theoretical exposure across occupations.** "On the first question, the answer is yes: reported exposure (grey dots) is positively correlated with both observed and theoretical exposure." (p.22, Figure 3.3). Comparison: occupation-level scatter of survey-reported exposure against the labour-market paper's observed exposure and against Eloundou et al.'s theoretical exposure. No correlation coefficients or slopes published.
66. **Expected 12-month progress is the same across occupations: the reported and anticipated best-fit lines are essentially parallel.** "the best-fit lines for reported and anticipated exposure 12 months from now (orange dots) are essentially parallel, meaning that people in roles with high observed or theoretical exposure expect roughly the same increase in the share of their work tasks AI can do over the next year as those in roles with less observed and theoretical exposure. In other words, a software engineer and a construction manager anticipate roughly the same increment of progress within their profession." (pp.22–23, Figure 3.3). Comparison: slope of anticipated versus slope of reported exposure on the same x-axis. Interpreted "qualitatively rather than as precise estimates" because of binning (ch.3 fn 4, p.31).
67. **Reported exposure is about 10 percentage points lower in high-income countries.** "perceptions of AI's capabilities are negatively correlated with country GDP: The average share of tasks people report AI can do for them now is about 10 percentage points lower among high-income countries." (p.24, Figure 3.4 left). Comparison: reported exposure against GDP per working-age adult, constructed as in the Index (ch.3 fn 6, p.31). The report's only geography-based numerical result.
68. **Reported and anticipated exposure fall with experience: respondents with 15+ years put the AI-doable share about 10 percentage points below first-year workers.** "The middle panel shows that reported and anticipated exposure are also negatively correlated with years of work experience. People with at least 15 years of experience put that share of tasks AI can do roughly 10 percentage points lower than those in their first year of work." (p.24, Figure 3.4 middle). Comparison: reported/anticipated exposure against self-reported years of experience "in their current or closely related roles" (ch.3 fn 7, p.31).
69. **What respondents say AI will never do: judgment, contextual awareness, situational reasoning, and relational work.** "the most common responses emphasized that AI lacks the judgment, contextual awareness, and situational reasoning that their work requires. Respondents, and disproportionately those with at least 15 years of experience, also pointed to the relational and interpersonal dimensions of their jobs—building trust and managing people—as things AI cannot replicate." (p.24). Open-ended responses, classifier-tagged; no shares published.
70. **Expected *future* capability is essentially uncorrelated with GDP per capita and with experience, and uniformly above current capability.** "we find that perceptions about future improvements in AI capabilities are essentially uncorrelated with GDP per capita and years of experience. The expected share of tasks that AI will be able to do in 12 months is uniformly higher than perceptions about AI's capabilities today." (p.24, Figure 3.4). The "rising tide" result of claim 57, stated as a null on two covariates.
71. **Reported and anticipated exposure rise with a respondent's automation share, and the same holds for work share and Claude Code share.** "The right panel of Figure 3.4 shows that reported and anticipated exposure rise with automation share. … The same patterns hold when we replace automation share with the share of sessions devoted to work tasks, or the share conducted in Claude Code." (p.25, Figure 3.4 right). Comparison: observed usage mix (from the linked sessions) against stated exposure. Robustness (ch.3 fn 9, p.31): "Conditioning on these measures therefore attenuates the relationship between automation share and task shares (today, in 12 months, and the change), but all three relationships remain positive and statistically significant."
72. **More than a third of respondents think it likely or very likely that job responsibilities will change significantly in 12 months, for themselves and for each of three reference people.** "More than a third of respondents said it was likely or very likely that responsibilities would significantly change (for themselves, a peer, a junior colleague, and a senior colleague)." (p.26, Figure 3.5 left). Comparison: same question across four targets.
73. **10% rate losing their own job as likely or very likely — slightly below the realised US involuntary-separation incidence, but possibly elevated for this population.** "10% rated losing their own jobs as likely or very likely. This is slightly below the annualized hazard rate of losing a job in the US; however, since our respondents skew toward knowledge workers in stable employment (a group that plausibly faces below-average separation risk at baseline), this may still indicate elevated perceived risk." (p.26, Figure 3.5 right). Benchmark (ch.3 fn 10, p.31): "The US layoffs and discharges rate (BLS JOLTS, total nonfarm, seasonally adjusted) averaged ~1.1% of employment per month over the 12 months through April 2026, amounting to a ~13.4% annualized sum, so 10% is slightly below the realized annual incidence of involuntary separation events."
74. **38% of those who rate their own job loss likely attribute the forecast to AI — an upper bound.** "38% of the respondents who rated their job loss as likely or very likely attributed their forecasts to AI." (p.26). Caveat (ch.3 fn 11, p.31): "This question was asked about the job change forecast and job loss forecast together. The 38% is therefore an upper bound."
75. **Respondents worry more about others than themselves, most of all about junior colleagues: over a third put a junior colleague's job-loss probability above 60%.** "respondents were on average more worried about job loss for others than for themselves. Respondents were especially worried about job loss for their junior colleagues, with over one third stating that the probability of a junior colleague losing their job in the next year was over 60%." (p.26, Figure 3.5 right). Comparison: self versus peer versus junior versus senior.
76. **Job-loss concern is higher for respondents in lower-income countries, for every target.** "Respondents were also more concerned about job loss (for everyone) in lower-income countries." (p.26). No number.
77. **On all six job-quality dimensions, respondents with a higher automation share are more optimistic; the largest effects are on pay and on the ability to find a job.** "Across all six dimensions, people with a higher share of automated sessions feel more optimistic about the effect of AI on their job outcomes next year compared to those who use Claude more augmentatively. We saw the largest effects on expectations about positive impacts on future pay and ability to find a job." (p.27, Figure 3.6). Comparison, per the caption: "coefficient estimates from linear regressions of an indicator for whether a person expected AI to have a positive effect on each of the six dimensions of job quality onto normalized AI automation share." Coefficients are in the figure only. Contrast noted in ch.3 fn 13 (p.31): "This contrasts with country GDP and experience, where lower GDP and experience correlate with higher task shares (as shown in Figure 3.4) and higher stated job loss probabilities."
78. **Controlling for Claude.ai tenure does not move those estimates.** "these estimates don't meaningfully change when we control for user tenure on Claude.ai—which we can think of as a proxy for enthusiasm, because it captures early versus later adopters." (p.27). The report's only attempt to address selection on enthusiasm.
79. **Large majorities report productivity gains: speed 86%, scope 82%, quality 69%; 27% report cost savings on purchased services.** "large majorities of people report productivity gains in speed, scope, and quality of their work (86%, 82%, and 69%, respectively), while 27% report gains through cost savings on services they would otherwise have to purchase." (p.28). Comparison: stated as "Consistent with our previous findings" (the 81k economics paper).
80. **68% report learning more with AI; 57% report that AI has made their skills more valuable.** "the majority of people also report learning more with AI (68%) and feeling like AI has made their skills more valuable (57%)." (p.28, Figure 3.7).
81. **Perceived skill value rises with automation share while perceived learning stays flat.** "the share of people reporting that AI is increasing the market value of their skills rises with automation share, while the share reporting they learn more is roughly flat." (p.28, Figure 3.7). Comparison: two survey outcomes against the same usage measure; the flat line is the report's null.
82. **Heavy delegators do not report less learning — a null offered against the skill-atrophy concern, with the self-report caveat attached.** "A commonly voiced concern about delegation is that handing entire tasks to AI means offloading thinking, with gains in output coming at the cost of learning and skill atrophy. We do not see this pattern here: heavier delegators report learning at the same rate as everyone else. However, these are self-assessments, and skills can erode even as they become more valuable and as someone reports learning more, so the data do not rule out skill erosion." (p.28). No MDE is given for the null.
83. **Women are 12% of the linked sample and use Claude measurably differently, net of occupation: Claude Code share 0.24 SD (6.3 pp) lower, automation share 0.33 SD (7.3 pp) lower, more active chat time.** "Women, who make up only 12% of our linked respondent sample, use Claude differently from men. Even after accounting for occupational differences, they are marginally less likely to use Claude for work, their share of sessions in Claude Code is 0.24 standard deviations lower (6.3 percentage points), and their automation share is 0.33 standard deviations lower (7.3 percentage points). Instead, women tend to use Claude more iteratively, and they log more active time on chat than men, a signal of more collaborative engagement." (p.29, Figure 3.8). Comparison, per the caption: "the difference between women's and men's average on that usage measure, controlling for occupation (SOC minor groups), expressed in standard deviations of the outcome. Whiskers show 95% confidence intervals. The sample is restricted to respondents identifying as women or men." Robustness (ch.3 fn 14, p.31): holds controlling for Claude Code session share and occupation fixed effects. The only claim in the report with published confidence intervals.
84. **Top hopes for an AI economy: augmentation (over half), automation of drudgery (just over half), shared prosperity (about a third).** "The most common theme expressed was one of AI augmentation of work. Over half of survey respondents expressed some version of wanting to collaborate with AI on work that feels meaningful, of wanting their career to still matter, and/or hoping that new industries arise and create new job opportunities. Simultaneously, just over half of respondents hoped for AI automation—specifically of the tedious parts of their jobs—so they could have more free time and more space for meaning outside of work. The third most common theme, expressed by about one third of survey respondents, was one of shared prosperity: the hope that the economic gains from AI will be widely shared." (p.30, Figure 3.9). Themes are classifier-tagged from one open-ended question; multiple tags per response, so shares do not sum to 1. Theme descriptions are deferred to the appendix.

### Use case, tokens, autonomy, reading level

21. **Use case (work / personal / coursework)** (p.10): "Our January Economic Index introduced a primitive that classifies each conversation as work, personal, or coursework. Here, we apply that split to the artifacts produced in Claude conversations (Figure 2.2)."

## Data and methods

In the wiki author's own words, with page references. Nothing here is a quotation.

**Sampling design — the headline methodological change.** Previous Index waves drew a fixed seven-day sample of conversations. This wave runs continuous telemetry that samples a slice of conversations every day, which is what makes day-of-week, calendar-date and hour-of-day analysis possible (p.4). Every analysis in the report runs on privacy-preserving classifiers: transcripts are read only by another instance of Claude, and cells with too few observations are suppressed before anything is reported (p.8, ch.1 fn 1). Local time for the hourly analysis is not self-reported; it is inferred from the conversation's IP address, described in the footnote as "inferring the state" (p.8, ch.1 fn 6) — i.e. a geo-IP step whose accuracy is not quantified anywhere in the report.

**Platforms and how they are partitioned.** Four surfaces appear. "Chat and Cowork" means conversations in the chat and Cowork tabs on Claude.ai and the Claude desktop app, on consumer Free/Pro/Max accounts; the report calls these together "Claude conversations" (pp.2–3, p.8 ch.1 fn 2, p.9). Claude Code is separate. The 1P API is developer traffic routed directly through Anthropic, excluding Bedrock and Vertex, and excluding Claude Code (p.8, ch.1 fn 3). Chapter 1's daily series (Figure 1.1) spans all four. Chapter 1's hourly figure (1.2) is restricted to chat and Cowork. Chapter 2 is chat and Cowork, with Claude Code brought in only for the autonomy surface comparison (p.18, ch.2 fn 1). Chapter 3's linked sessions span Claude.ai, Cowork and Claude Code (p.19). Cowork is never reported on its own except in one footnote remark about data and spreadsheet work (p.18, ch.2 fn 5).

**Windows.** Chapter 1's window is not stated as a date range; it is identified only by the facts that it covers the US tax filing deadline and that Figure 1.4's benchmark is "the average day in May" (p.7). Chapter 2 is April 10 – June 10, 2026, pooled (p.18, ch.2 fn 1). Chapter 3's survey launched in April 2026 and its usage linkage window is mid-May to early June (p.19). The public release carries April and May 2026 as separate calendar months (release documentation). So the report's windows and the release's windows are not the same objects; a replication that assumes the released April/May aggregates reproduce chapter 2 would be assuming a window the report does not claim.

**Classifiers.** Five classifier-derived constructs carry the report.
- *Artifact* — new in this wave. Each chat/Cowork conversation is labelled with the single most prominent concrete output, sorted into more than 30 categories, with a "None" catch-all for conversations that produced no prominent output, including brief, abandoned or errored exchanges and unanswered clarifying questions (p.9, p.18 ch.2 fn 2). The label list is in the appendix; the released machine labels number 32 including `none` and `other`. No accuracy, human-agreement or inter-rater statistic for this classifier appears in the report body.
- *Use case* — work / personal / coursework, the primitive introduced in the January 2026 report and re-used here without restatement of its prompt (p.10).
- *Collaboration mode* — the five-mode classifier (directive, feedback loop, task iteration, learning, validation), with automation defined as directive plus feedback loop (p.25, p.31 ch.3 fn 8). Unchanged from earlier waves.
- *AI autonomy* — a 1–5 rating of how much decision-making is delegated to Claude, from "none" to "extreme" (p.14). Distinct from automation share; the report uses both and does not conflate them.
- *Reading level* — two estimates per conversation, one for the prompt and one for the response, each expressed as years of education needed to understand the text; the prompt is documented in the March 2026 report's appendix, not here (p.16, p.18 ch.2 fn 6).
Request clusters are a sixth classifier-derived object, defined in the March 2025 Sonnet 3.7 report and this wave's appendix rather than here (p.8, ch.1 fn 4).

**Occupation and wage mapping.** Chapters 1 and 2 infer occupation from the conversation's classified task, then attach that occupation's wage — occupation is a property of the task, not of the user, and the report says as much when it notes it cannot identify respondents' jobs (p.7). Wages come from the BLS OEWS May 2025 release (p.18, ch.2 fn 1). The night/weekend analysis bins mapped occupations into wage quartiles weighted by transcript count (Figure 1.3 caption, p.7); the token analysis bins them into terciles weighted by matched conversations (Table 2.4 caption, p.14). Chapter 3, by contrast, uses respondents' *self-reported* occupation coded to the 22 major SOC groups, compared against OEWS employment shares (Figure 3.1 caption, p.21), with SOC minor groups as controls in the gender regression (Figure 3.8 caption, p.29).

**Token measurement.** A conversation's computational cost is its token count — text processed and generated, including Claude's internal reasoning (p.12). Conversation-level token counts are aggregated with geometric means because the distribution is extremely right-skewed; the authors report that medians and cost-weighted tokens give very similar results, with physician occupations among the exceptions (p.18, ch.2 fn 3). Token counts are not adjusted for which model served the conversation (Figure 2.3 caption, p.13). Both axes of the wage–token scatter are logged and both variables are normalised, the tokens by the overall mean in the left panel and by the overall median in the right panel (Figure 2.3 caption, p.13).

**Decompositions.** Three appear, none with a stated estimator. The 44% share of the wage–token gradient attributed to output mix (p.13); the two-thirds/one-third split of the surface autonomy gap into within-task delegation and between-task composition (p.15); and the ~70% compositional share of the data-and-spreadsheets exception (p.18, ch.2 fn 5). The report gives no formula, no weights and no standard errors for any of the three.

**Robustness checks that are reported.** Dropping computer and mathematical occupations from the night/weekend wage analysis, with the sign preserved (p.7). Comparing autonomy within model (Sonnet) to rule out model mix, 0.26 against 0.37 points (p.16). Re-running the exposure slope comparison with a binary "AI can do at least 60% of my tasks" indicator, which is immune to midpoint coding (p.31, ch.3 fn 4). Substituting work share and Claude Code share for automation share, which attenuates but preserves sign and significance (p.31, ch.3 fn 9). Controlling for Claude.ai tenure in the sentiment regressions (p.27). Controlling for Claude Code session share and occupation fixed effects in the gender regressions (p.31, ch.3 fn 14). Medians and cost-weighted tokens as alternatives to geometric means (p.18, ch.2 fn 3).

**Survey design and linkage.** The Anthropic Economic Index Survey launched in April 2026; this report carries its first results (p.2, p.19). Respondents are a random sample of Claude users, so the frame is Claude users, not the population (p.20). Question types visible in the report: five-band categorical items for reported and anticipated exposure ("almost none" to "nearly all"), coded at bin midpoints (p.22, Figure 3.3 caption p.23); likelihood items on job-responsibility change and involuntary job loss asked separately for self, peer, junior colleague and senior colleague (p.26, Figure 3.5 caption); directional expectation items on six job-quality dimensions (p.27); self-reported occupation, employment status, gender and years of experience (pp.20, 29, 31); self-reported productivity gains in speed, scope, quality and cost savings (p.28); self-reported learning and skill value (p.28); and at least three open-ended items — what AI will never be able to do and why (p.24), what is driving the job-loss forecast (p.26), and the closing "dream big" question (p.30). Open-ended answers are classifier-tagged into themes (pp.24, 26, 30). Linkage to usage runs through the privacy-preserving system (Clio / Anthropic Insights): for each respondent, up to 20 randomly sampled sessions in the mid-May to early-June window across Claude.ai, Cowork and Claude Code, deliberately sampled so the surface mix reflects the person's own usage; respondents with fewer than five sessions are dropped; about 9,700 remain (p.19). Note that this makes every usage covariate in chapter 3 an estimate from at most 20 sessions per person, with the sampling error not quantified in the report.

**Regressions.** Two explicit specifications. Figure 3.6 regresses, for each of the six job-quality dimensions, an indicator for expecting a positive effect on normalised AI automation share; the figure reports the coefficients (caption, p.27). Figure 3.8 regresses each usage measure on a woman indicator with occupation (SOC minor group) controls, reporting differences in outcome standard deviations with 95% confidence intervals (caption, p.29). Figures 3.3 and 3.4 show best-fit lines whose specification is not stated. Confidence intervals appear only in Figure 3.8. No minimum detectable effects, power calculations or sample sizes per cell are reported anywhere.

**External data used.** BLS OEWS May 2025 for wages and for US employment shares; BLS data for the wage quartiles; BLS JOLTS layoffs-and-discharges for the job-loss benchmark; World Bank WDI 2024 and UN World Population Prospects 2024 for working-age population; IMF World Economic Outlook 2025 estimates for GDP; the 22 major SOC groups, military excluded; Eloundou et al. (arXiv 2303.10130) for theoretical exposure; the labour-market impacts paper for observed exposure; the IMF's 2024 staff discussion note on AI and inequality as corroboration for the GDP result; a Federal Reserve well-being publication as an analogue for the self-versus-others gap; and the Anthropic Public Record, "a nationally representative survey of more than 50,000 Americans", cited as showing similar patterns outside the Claude user base (p.31, ch.3 fn 1).

**Released data, and what is not released.** The release is two CSVs of aggregated metrics — chat and Cowork at global, country and subregion grain, and 1P API at global grain only — for April and May 2026, with the schema, categories (`overall`, `onet`, `request`, `soc_occupation`), metric list, thresholds and geography sample floor documented in `release_2026_06_26/data_documentation.md`. **No code ships with this release** (consistent with the steward's release enumeration: only the 2025-02-10, 2025-03-27 and 2025-09-15 waves ship notebooks or a code library). The artifact metrics are released as `artifact_{label}_pct` per label, and the collaboration, use-case, autonomy, education-years, time-saving, multitasking and human-only-ability metrics are released alongside. Nothing in the release is at conversation level, nothing is hourly or daily, and nothing carries the survey: **the hourly cadences of chapter 1, the token and reading-level measures of chapter 2 and the entire linked survey of chapter 3 are not in the released data.** Any post building on those chapters builds on a published figure or a conjecture, not on a reproducible number. Confirm the exact file inventory against the steward's `data/releases/release_2026_06_26.md` before relying on this paragraph.

## Limitations (verbatim)

Every limitation the report states, quoted exactly, with page reference. Ordered by page.

1. **Privacy suppression of small cells** (p.8, ch.1 fn 1): "Then we filter out any cells with insufficient observations to ensure privacy-preserving analysis."
2. **Occupation is inferred, not observed** (p.7): "While we can't conclusively identify the jobs of the people making these requests, this could reflect the fact that people in higher-paying occupations—like marketing managers or computer programmers—are more likely to work outside traditional hours."
3. **The night/weekend pattern needed a robustness check because of one occupation group** (p.7): "This pattern isn't driven exclusively by computer and mathematical tasks: when we removed those occupations from the analysis in a robustness check, higher-quartile tasks still increased on nights and weekends."
4. **The "None" artifact label mixes several failure modes** (p.18, ch.2 fn 2): ""None" is a catch-all for the conversations that didn't yield a prominent concrete output. This may include brief or abandoned exchanges, cases resulting in an error or cases where Claude asked a clarifying question and the user didn't continue."
5. **The wage–token relationship is noisy and has outliers** (p.12): "Admittedly, the relationship is noisy and there are notable outliers."
6. **A published counter-example to the report's own gradient** (p.12): "Pharmacists, for example, earn nearly three times what statistical assistants do ($68 vs. $24 per hour), yet conversations mapped to pharmacist tasks use only about one twentieth as many tokens."
7. **Token skew, and exceptions to the robustness of the aggregation choice** (p.18, ch.2 fn 3): "We use geometric means for the conversation level token counts since that variable is extremely right-skewed–a small number of conversations use several orders of magnitude more tokens than a "typical" conversation. The relationship is very similar if we use medians or if we weight the tokens by their respective cost to account for the mix of models used. There are some notable exceptions, including physician occupations."
8. **Tokens are not model-adjusted** (Figure 2.3 caption, p.13): "Token counts are not adjusted for which model served the conversation."
9. **The augmentation reading is conditional** (pp.13–14): "If the human remains involved in the highest-value tasks, the pattern looks more labor-augmenting than labor-displacing."
10. **The surface autonomy gap has a large exception, and it is mostly compositional** (p.18, ch.2 fn 5): "The largest exception is data and spreadsheets, where Claude.ai conversations involve more autonomy than Claude Code (3.09 vs 2.74). This is mostly compositional: about 70% of the gap reflects a different mix of tasks."
11. **Part of the reading-level gap may be register, not comprehension** (p.16): "Some of the gap may simply be register; prompts are often terse and informal, while Claude tends to reply in polished prose."
12. **The survey frame is Claude users, with selection at two further stages** (p.20): "The Economic Index Survey is not representative of the general population. We reach a random sample of Claude users, there may be selection in who completes the survey, and we filter out infrequent users from our analysis."
13. **Respondents with fewer than five sessions are dropped** (p.19): "We exclude respondents with fewer than five sessions to reduce sampling noise."
14. **Slopes in the exposure figure are attenuated by binning, and are read qualitatively** (p.31, ch.3 fn 4): "Because responses are binned—so the lowest possible coded response exceeds zero, and the highest possible response falls short of one—the slopes in this figure are biased towards zero. As a result, we interpret the comparison of slopes qualitatively rather than as precise estimates. However, the patterns (positive slopes and close to parallel lines) are robust to instead estimating the relationship using an indicator for reporting that AI can do at least 60% of one's work tasks, which is unaffected by midpoint coding."
15. **Midpoint coding can manufacture the reported-versus-observed gap** (p.31, ch.3 fn 5): "The binned response scale likely also plays a role: because midpoint coding pulls reported task shares away from the extremes, observed exposure will tend to look as though it understates AI's capabilities in the least-exposed occupations and overstates them in the most-exposed ones, even absent any substantive difference."
16. **Reported exposure exceeds observed exposure partly because of who answers** (p.23): "One explanation for this is that not everybody does every task in an occupation, and our survey disproportionately reaches those who use AI more."
17. **Theoretical exposure is an upper bound, not a usage measure** (p.23): "since theoretical exposure is an upper bound on what is possible instead of a measure of current use, theoretical exposure systematically overstates reported exposure."
18. **Small-sample occupations are grouped for privacy** (Figure 3.3 caption, p.23): "Each point is an occupation, with occupations containing small samples grouped with exposure neighbors to ensure privacy."
19. **The exposure–automation relationship has two directions of causality** (p.25): "This could be because delegation is informative about capabilities—people who hand over entire tasks observe directly what AI can complete on its own—or because people who already believe AI can do their work are the most willing to hand it over."
20. **Conditioning attenuates the automation result** (p.31, ch.3 fn 9): "Conditioning on these measures therefore attenuates the relationship between automation share and task shares (today, in 12 months, and the change), but all three relationships remain positive and statistically significant."
21. **The work-share confound is partly mechanical** (p.31, ch.3 fn 9): "Work usage also matters directly—the survey asks about work tasks, so people who use Claude for work may mechanically expect it to do a larger share of their work tasks."
22. **The 10% job-loss figure is hard to benchmark because the sample is unusual** (p.26): "This is slightly below the annualized hazard rate of losing a job in the US; however, since our respondents skew toward knowledge workers in stable employment (a group that plausibly faces below-average separation risk at baseline), this may still indicate elevated perceived risk."
23. **The 38% AI attribution is an upper bound** (p.31, ch.3 fn 11): "This question was asked about the job change forecast and job loss forecast together. The 38% is therefore an upper bound on the share of people who attribute their own job loss forecast to AI."
24. **The self-versus-others gap may be a known reporting bias** (p.31, ch.3 fn 12): "This mirrors a familiar pattern of people rating their own circumstances more favorably than other people's."
25. **Selection cannot be ruled out in the automation–sentiment relationship** (p.27): "It's possible that this relationship is explained by selection, that the people most enthusiastic about AI are also the most willing to hand over entire tasks to it. We can't rule this out entirely, but these estimates don't meaningfully change when we control for user tenure on Claude.ai—which we can think of as a proxy for enthusiasm, because it captures early versus later adopters."
26. **The learning null is self-reported and does not rule out skill erosion** (p.28): "However, these are self-assessments, and skills can erode even as they become more valuable and as someone reports learning more, so the data do not rule out skill erosion."
27. **The gender result could be surface substitution** (p.31, ch.3 fn 14): "While this could be due to substitution between chat/Cowork and Claude Code, the pattern holds even when controlling for Claude Code session share. These patterns also survive controlling for occupation fixed effects."
28. **Women are 12% of the linked sample** (p.29): "Women, who make up only 12% of our linked respondent sample, use Claude differently from men."
29. **Measurement itself is a moving target** (p.32): "Accurately classifying the work that Claude does will remain a moving target."
30. **Usage logs are not the outcome that matters** (p.32): "Ultimately, Claude's impact on the economy will be visible in economic aggregates like employment and productivity as much as its usage logs."
31. **Usage data has limited informational content on its own** (p.32): "Finally, usage data only carries so much information."
32. **Chat transcripts no longer capture usage** (p.2): "A chat transcript no longer fully captures how people are using AI, and our methods for studying Claude's economic impacts have had to rapidly adapt."

**Note (wiki author):** the report contains **no** limitation on the artifact classifier's accuracy, **no** statement of statistical power or minimum detectable effect for any null (notably the flat learning line, claim 81, and the parallel exposure slopes, claim 66), and **no** limitation on the geo-IP local-time inference underlying every hourly result.

## What it did not test

**This section is the wiki author's inference, not Anthropic's text.** It lists adjacent questions the report had the data to answer and did not, and constructs it used without validating. Every item below was checked against the report's own stated windows and surfaces; items that the appendix might answer are marked, and the appendix entry (`wiki/reports/economic-index-2026-06-appendix.md`) should be read before any of them is treated as open.

### Cuts the data plainly supported and the report does not show

1. **April versus May.** The release publishes two calendar months side by side and the report never compares them. No month-to-month change in any metric — artifact mix, automation share, autonomy, use case, geography — is published, even though the stated purpose of the monthly grain is to make change measurable. This is the largest unexploited cut in the wave.
2. **Any per-capita geography result.** `usage_per_capita_index` is released for countries and US states, and the report publishes no AUI number, no country ranking, no state map. The previous waves' central geographic finding has no continuation here.
3. **Subregion (US state) anything.** The subregion grain exists in the release and the hourly analysis already infers state from IP (p.8, ch.1 fn 6) — so state-level time-of-day cadences were mechanically available and are not shown.
4. **Hourly cadences on Claude Code and the 1P API.** Figure 1.1 puts all four surfaces on a daily series; Figure 1.2 restricts the hourly series to chat and Cowork. Whether agentic sessions have a different diurnal shape — the obvious question given a surface that runs unattended — is not asked.
5. **Non-US tax deadlines.** The tax-day spike is measured against one deadline in one country, while Figure 1.4 already splits US from rest of world. The UK (31 January), Indian (31 July) and Australian (31 October) deadlines fall outside the window, but the April window covers Canada's 30 April deadline, and no non-US deadline is examined.
6. **Whether the weekend shift is composition or behaviour.** Chapter 1's personal-use swing could be different people using Claude on weekends or the same people using it differently. Chapter 3 demonstrates that person-level linkage exists; chapter 1 does not use it. No user-level panel, no within-user decomposition.
7. **Artifact × collaboration mode.** Autonomy by artifact is shown (Figure 2.5); automation share by artifact is not, although both classifiers ran on the same conversations and the released data carries both per node. The direct question — which outputs are delegated as whole tasks — is left to a correlation across artifacts (r = 0.68, claim 50).
8. **Artifact × geography.** The artifact classifier ran on global chat and Cowork traffic and the release carries artifact metrics at country grain, but the report shows no cross-country artifact mix. "This shift is biggest for high-income countries" (claim 9) is asserted for use case only.
9. **Artifact mix on the 1P API in the body.** Deferred to the appendix (claim 23), so the report never compares developer-API outputs with consumer outputs, despite the introduction naming that breakout as one of its three headline changes.
10. **Cowork on its own.** Cowork is pooled with chat throughout. The one footnote that separates it (claim 47) says Cowork is both over-represented and especially autonomous on data and spreadsheet work — i.e. the pooling is known to matter and is not unwound. Nothing in the report says what share of "Claude conversations" Cowork is.
11. **Task-inferred occupation against self-reported occupation.** Chapter 3 has ~9,700 people's self-reported SOC codes *and* their linked sessions. That is exactly the sample needed to validate the occupational inference that chapters 1 and 2, the observed-exposure measure and three prior reports all depend on. The report compares respondent shares to employment shares and to session shares (claims 60–61) but never checks whether a respondent's task-inferred occupation matches the occupation they report.
12. **Person-level observed exposure.** Reported exposure is benchmarked against occupation-level observed exposure (claim 65). With linked sessions, observed exposure could have been computed for each respondent's own task mix, removing the "not everybody does every task" confound the report itself names (limitation 16).
13. **Extended thinking as a channel.** Extended thinking is enabled in 34% versus 31% of conversations across wage terciles (claim 38) and is included in the token count (definition 22), but the token gradient is never decomposed into a thinking component and a visible-output component.
14. **Session length and task horizon.** The surface autonomy gap is attributed to delegation and output mix (claim 44). Session duration, wall-clock length or number of tool calls — the natural third channel, and the one the January 2026 report's task-horizon primitive was built for — is not examined. Active minutes appear only once, in the gender analysis (claim 83).
15. **Any seasonality or year-over-year benchmark.** All cadence results come from one contiguous spring window. The tax-day spike has no prior-year comparison, so the 8× ratio cannot be separated from any April-specific level shift, and the weekday/weekend gap cannot be checked against a season without a tax deadline in it.
16. **Wage gradient in tokens, net of artifact.** 44% of the gradient is attributed to output mix (claim 37); the residual 56% is never characterised. Whether the within-artifact gradient is turns, output length, thinking or model is not decomposed.
17. **Dollar cost.** Tokens are the cost measure and the authors note the cost-weighted variant exists (definition 23), but no cost figure, no cost-per-artifact and no cost-against-wage comparison is published — so "more valuable outputs cost more" (claim 39) is never expressed in comparable units on both sides.
18. **Reading level against any external readability benchmark.** Two reading-level estimates per conversation, no comparison with a standard readability index, no human-coded subsample, no check that "years of education" is monotone in anything external.
19. **The survey's own non-response.** The report names selection in who completes the survey (limitation 12) but publishes no response rate, no invited-sample size, and no comparison of respondents with non-respondents on observable usage — a comparison the linkage infrastructure makes possible.
20. **Gender by country or by occupation group.** The gender result is net of SOC minor groups (claim 83) but is not shown separately for any country, income group or occupation group, and the 12% female share is never discussed as a power constraint.
21. **Whether the automation–optimism relationship is monotone or driven by a tail.** Figure 3.6 reports linear coefficients on normalised automation share; no non-linearity, no quantile comparison, no test of whether the relationship is driven by the most automated decile.
22. **Cross-validation of reported exposure against the 81k interviews.** The December 2025 interviews asked adjacent questions of a much larger, overlapping user population (p.19). No attempt is made to reconcile the two instruments' estimates of perceived AI capability.

### Constructs used without validation in this report

23. **The artifact taxonomy.** More than 30 categories, no accuracy statistic, no human-agreement rate, no confusion structure, no statement of how the categories were chosen, and no sensitivity of any published share to the taxonomy. The three-way grouping into conversational outputs, written deliverables and code (claim 22) is introduced in a single sentence and never specified label-by-label. *Check the appendix before treating the validation as absent.*
24. **The "None" residual as 7% of conversations.** It bundles abandoned exchanges, errors and unanswered clarifying questions (definition 17). Whether the 93% artifact rate is sensitive to that bundling is untested, and no breakdown of "None" is given.
25. **Reading level as "years of education".** A cardinal scale is asserted and then differenced (claim 54, "+2.6 years"), which requires interval-scale validity the report never establishes.
26. **Mapped-occupation wage as the value of the work.** The whole "cost tracks value" chapter rests on occupation median wage as a proxy for the value of a conversation's output. The report's own pharmacist counter-example (claim 35) shows the proxy failing, and no alternative value measure — willingness to pay, time saved, task success — is brought in, though the January 2026 and November 2025 papers built two of those measures.
27. **Geo-IP local time.** Every hourly result depends on inferring location from IP (definition 10). No accuracy figure, no VPN or mobile-network caveat, no sensitivity of the 6 p.m. recipe peak or the 7 a.m. news peak to mis-assigned time zones.
28. **Automation share estimated from ≤20 sessions per person.** Chapter 3's central regressor is a person-level share computed from a small random sample of that person's sessions (definition 7). The report gives no measurement-error correction and no statement of how much attenuation that induces — which matters because several of its conclusions are about the *slope* on automation share.
29. **Active minutes on chat as "a signal of more collaborative engagement".** An interpretive leap from a telemetry field to a behavioural construct (claim 83), with no validation.
30. **"Rising tide" as the reading of a null.** Uniform expected progress is offered as consistent with broad capability improvement (conjecture 18) without ruling out the alternative that binned questions plus ceiling effects produce parallel lines mechanically — a possibility the report's own footnote 4 raises for the level but not for the interpretation.
31. **Self-reported productivity gains.** 86 / 82 / 69 / 27 percent (claim 79) are unvalidated self-assessments, presented without the estimator-based counterpart from the November 2025 productivity paper and without a triangulation attempt.
