## 1. Anthropic's framing

### 1a. The Institute agenda's economic-diffusion section **[fetched]**
Page dated 7 May 2026, https://www.anthropic.com/research/anthropic-institute-agenda. Four pillars: **economic diffusion**, threats and resilience, AI systems in the wild, AI-driven R&D. Extracted wording (verify exact phrasing on the page):

- Framing sentence: *"It's crucial to understand how the deployment of increasingly powerful AI systems changes the economy."*
- **AI adoption and diffusion:** *"Who adopts AI?"* — how access concentrates geographically and by firm size; *"What causes AI adoption at the firm level, and what are the consequences?"*
- **Productivity and growth:** *"What impact will AI have on the rate of innovation and productivity growth across the economy?"* and *"What pre- or re-distributive mechanisms could effectively spread the gains from AI development?"*
- **Labour markets:** *"How will AI change jobs and employment in different parts of the economy?"* — with monthly signals from *"our Anthropic Economic Index Survey"* on how workers experience and anticipate change.
- **The professional pipeline problem:** *"If AI absorbs the tasks that historically built expertise, how do people become experts in the first place?"*

The Economics team page **[fetched]** (https://www.anthropic.com/research/team/economics) states the mission: *"The Economics team studies how AI is reshaping the economy, including work, productivity, and economic opportunity"*; *"We build the empirical foundation for understanding AI's economic impact"*; *"Our research provides evidence to address uncertainty and helps society navigate this transition in ways that are broadly beneficial."*

### 1c. Anthropic's Economic Policy Framework (June 2026) **[fetched, PDF text-extracted]**
Linked from https://www.anthropic.com/policy; PDF at https://www-cdn.anthropic.com/files/4zrzovbb/website/9ea607a5dd67c168093829b701f3a0a6d21156d5.pdf. Backed by "$350 million" for policy trials and partnerships (policy page). The document is US-focused and explicitly tiered by unemployment rate:

- **Values:** *"We are not seeking job displacement. We are working to prevent or minimize it... Whatever happens, we are on the side of people."* Opening evidence cites Canaries, Massenkoff–McCrory, Acemoglu (2024) and Korinek–Suh (2024): *"Economists' models of the future range from modest productivity-driven growth to scenarios where economic output doubles but wages collapse."* *"The central challenge isn't how to stimulate growth; it is making sure the gains are widely shared."*
- **Foundations:** better **measurement** (fund statistical agencies, AI-specific surveys, *"reporting requirements for AI labs and firms—including Anthropic"*), a **dedicated government unit** tracking AI (analogy: the Council of Economic Advisers), and modernised **UI delivery infrastructure**.
- **Tier 1 (~5% unemployment):** universal **pre-distributive capital accounts** (expand the existing birth accounts to exposed cohorts, fundable with AI-company equity); wage insurance; occupational-licensing reform; retention tax incentives; **employer-connected sectoral training grants**; AI-powered job-matching.
- **Tier 2 (~10%):** automatic, uniform UI extension triggers; sector-specific transition support; **basic-needs relief** with enhanced benefits for those moving into care, education, infrastructure.
- **Tier 3 (unprecedented unemployment):** convergence of benefits to a common floor; **new tax bases** (capital gains, consumption taxes, *"sector-specific levies on AI use (measured by tokens, compute, or revenue)"*, digital dividends); redistribution via UBI, **AI sovereign wealth funds**, worker equity-sharing; expanded public investment in human-facing work. If disruption exceeds absorptive capacity, *"we would support government regulations and incentives at the firm level that manage the pace of displacement"* (citing Korinek & Stiglitz 2026, "Steering Technological Progress"). *"We are ready and willing to pay our fair share."*

### 1d. Korinek's and McCrory's public positions
- **Korinek** (UVA; co-director of the Economics of Transformative AI initiative, https://econtai.org/ **[fetched]**; Coursera "The Economics of AI" **[fetched]**). Core claims: in Korinek–Suh (2024) wages rise forever if automation is slow, but *"if the complexity of tasks that humans can perform is bounded and full automation is reached, then wages collapse"* — possibly before full automation if automation outpaces capital accumulation. Trammell–Korinek: full automation *"would dramatically raise the growth rate and lower the labor share, breaking the Kaldor Facts."* "Economic Policy Challenges for the Age of AI" (2024) names eight challenges (inequality, education, stability, macro policy, antitrust, IP, environment, global governance). Recent (korinek.com **[fetched]**): "When Does Automating AI Research Produce Explosive Growth?" (with Davidson, Halperin, Houlden, Jan 2026), "Public Finance in the Age of AI" (with Lockwood, Jan 2026), "Steering Technological Progress" (with Stiglitz). The Policy Framework's tax-base and pace-steering sections are essentially Korinek's programme.
- **McCrory**: methodological owner of the Index's economic primitives and of the productivity estimate; consistently presents Anthropic's numbers as upper bounds subject to success rates and complementarity; the Europe quote above. **Massenkoff**: pushes hard on evidence quality (retraining review, the marginal-significance caveats on young-worker effects) and on occupational forecasting ("How predictable is job destruction? Evidence from the Occupational Outlook," Oct 2025, listed on his site, URL not found **[UNVERIFIED]**).

## 2. Foundational papers (the ones the community actually cites)

**MUST READ FIRST** = ★

1. ★ **Acemoglu & Restrepo (2020), "Robots and Jobs: Evidence from US Labor Markets,"** JPE. One more robot per 1,000 workers cuts the employment-population ratio by 0.18–0.34pp and wages by 0.25–0.5%. The template for commuting-zone exposure designs. https://www.nber.org/papers/w23285 **[fetched]**
2. ★ **Acemoglu & Restrepo (2022), "Tasks, Automation, and the Rise in US Wage Inequality,"** Econometrica. 50–70% of four decades of US wage-structure change is task displacement of routine-specialised groups. The canonical task model in GE. https://www.nber.org/papers/w28920 **[fetched]**
3. ★ **Acemoglu (2024), "The Simple Macroeconomics of AI,"** NBER w32487. Task-based bounding exercise: TFP gain ≲0.53–0.66% over a decade; "so-so" automation. The pessimist anchor that Anthropic's own 1.8pp estimate is argued against. https://www.nber.org/papers/w32487 **[fetched]**
4. **Restrepo (2023), "Automation: Theory, Evidence, and Outlook,"** Annual Review of Economics 2024. The best single survey of the task model (productivity vs displacement effects). https://www.nber.org/papers/w31910 **[fetched]**
5. ★ **Autor, Chin, Salomons & Seegmiller (2024), "New Frontiers: The Origins and Content of New Work, 1940–2018,"** QJE. Most current employment is in job titles created after 1940; automation slows new-work creation while augmentation innovations create it. The "new work" counterweight to displacement. https://www.nber.org/papers/w30389 **[fetched]**
6. **Autor (2024), "Applying AI to Rebuild Middle Class Jobs,"** NBER w32140. AI could let more workers do expert tasks and restore middle-skill work — the optimistic "expertise" thesis. https://www.nber.org/papers/w32140 **[fetched]**
7. ★ **Brynjolfsson, Li & Raymond (2025), "Generative AI at Work,"** QJE. 5,179 support agents: +14% resolutions/hour, +34% for novices, ~0 for experts; AI disseminates best practice. The canonical field evidence for novice uplift. https://www.nber.org/papers/w31161 **[fetched]**
8. ★ **Brynjolfsson, Chandar & Chen (2025; rev. Aug 2026), "Canaries in the Coal Mine? Six Facts about the Recent Employment Effects of AI,"** Stanford DEL. ADP payroll through June 2026: 22–25-year-olds in exposed occupations now ~19pp below the counterfactual, via reduced hiring not separations; gap "has widened steadily." https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/publications/canaries-in-the-coal-mine/ **[fetched]**
9. **Noy & Zhang (2023), "Experimental Evidence on the Productivity Effects of Generative AI,"** Science. 444 professionals: time −0.8 SD, quality +0.4 SD; compresses the productivity distribution; substitutes for effort rather than complementing skill. MIT PDF https://economics.mit.edu/sites/default/files/inline-files/Noy_Zhang_1.pdf **[fetched, text-extracted]**; Science page https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.adh2586 (403).
10. **Dell'Acqua et al. (2023), "Navigating the Jagged Technological Frontier,"** HBS WP 24-013. BCG consultants: +12% tasks, 25% faster, 40% higher quality inside the frontier, worse outside it. Origin of "jagged frontier." https://www.hbs.edu/faculty/Pages/item.aspx?num=64700 **[UNVERIFIED — 403 this session]**
11. ★ **Eloundou, Manning, Mishkin & Rock (2024), "GPTs are GPTs,"** Science. ~80% of US workers have ≥10% of tasks exposed, 19% have ≥50%; exposure rises with wages. The exposure scores everyone (including Massenkoff–McCrory) build on. https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.10130 **[fetched]**; data https://github.com/openai/GPTs-are-GPTs **[fetched]**
12. **Felten, Raj & Seamans (2021/2023), AI Occupational Exposure (AIOE),** Strategic Management Journal; LLM update: telemarketers and post-secondary teachers most exposed; positive wage–exposure correlation. https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.01157 **[fetched]**; SMJ https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/smj.3286 (403).
13. **Webb (2020), "The Impact of Artificial Intelligence on the Labor Market."** Patent-text × task-text exposure; AI is directed at high-skilled tasks and would reduce 90:10 inequality. https://www.michaelwebb.co/webb_ai.pdf **[fetched, text-extracted]**
14. **Frey & Osborne (2013/2017), "The Future of Employment"** (47% of US jobs at risk) and **Arntz, Gregory & Zierahn (2016)** OECD critique (task-level analysis cuts it to ~9%). Know the occupation-vs-task methodological lesson. https://www.oxfordmartin.ox.ac.uk/publications/the-future-of-employment/ **[fetched]**; OECD https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/the-risk-of-automation-for-jobs-in-oecd-countries_5jlz9h56dvq7-en.html **[UNVERIFIED — 403]**
15. ★ **Bick, Blandin & Deming (2024/25), "The Rapid Adoption of Generative AI,"** NBER w32966. ~40% of US adults 18–64 use GenAI; 23% of workers used it at work last week, 9% daily; adoption as fast as the PC; 1–5% of work hours assisted; 1.4% time saved. https://www.nber.org/papers/w32966 **[fetched]**
16. ★ **Humlum & Vestergaard (2025; rev. Mar 2026), "Still Waters, Rapid Currents / Large Language Models, Small Labor Market Effects,"** NBER w33777. Danish admin data: "precise null effects on earnings and recorded hours" (<2% within two years) despite wide adoption; task restructuring precedes wage effects. ## 4. Key things to know

**Core vocabulary**
1. **Augmentation vs automation:** Anthropic's Clio labels — collaborative (task iteration, learning, validation) vs directive/feedback-loop use; the automation share crossed 50% on Claude.ai in Aug 2025 and is far higher (64–77%) in API traffic.
2. **Theoretical vs observed exposure:** theoretical = what LLMs *could* do (Eloundou); observed = Massenkoff–McCrory's usage-weighted measure. Gap is huge (33% vs 94% for computer/math).
3. **Task-based model:** production as a continuum of tasks allocated to labour or capital; automation has a displacement effect and a productivity effect; new tasks ("reinstatement") offset (Acemoglu–Restrepo; Restrepo 2023).
4. **Skill-biased vs routine-biased technical change:** SBTC raises returns to education; RBTC hollows out the middle (Autor). GenAI may be "inverse SBTC" — hitting high-wage cognitive tasks (Webb, Eloundou).
5. **Baumol's cost disease / "essential yet hard to improve":** growth is bottlenecked by the tasks AI cannot do (Aghion–Jones–Jones) — the central argument against explosive growth.
6. **Productivity J-curve:** intangible complementary investment depresses measured productivity early and inflates it later (Brynjolfsson–Rock–Syverson).
7. **General-purpose technology (GPT):** pervasive, improving, spawning complementary innovation; "GPTs are GPTs" pun.
8. **Diffusion lag / adoption staircase:** adoption proceeds in steps as firms reorganise; Anthropic's data show state-level convergence slower than first thought (5–9 years) while country gaps are not converging.
9. **Deskilling vs upskilling:** whether removing AI-capable tasks leaves an occupation with less or more complex residual work (Jan 2026 report: net deskilling on average; travel agents vs real-estate managers).
10. **Professional pipeline:** if entry-level tasks are where expertise is built, automating them breaks the apprenticeship ladder (agenda; Canaries; Claude Code expertise paper).
11. **Economic primitives:** Anthropic's five task dimensions — complexity, human/AI skill alignment, use case, autonomy, success.
12. **AI Usage Index (AUI):** region's share of usage ÷ share of working-age population.
13. **Complementarity and elasticity of substitution:** if tasks are complements (low σ), gains are bounded by the slowest task; Anthropic's productivity estimate falls from 1.8pp to 0.6–0.9pp under complementarity.
14. **New work / reinstatement:** creation of new job titles that absorb displaced labour (Autor et al. 2024; "one-in-five workers hold jobs that didn't exist before 1970" per Resolution Foundation).
15. **Jagged frontier:** capability is uneven across superficially similar tasks, so exposure is discontinuous.
16. **Pre-distribution vs redistribution:** Anthropic's capital-accounts proposal (pre-) vs UBI/sovereign funds (re-); the agenda asks about both.

**Ten key tensions**
1. **Is AI displacing workers yet?** *Canaries* (ADP: 22–25-year-olds in exposed jobs ~19pp below trend, widening) vs Humlum–Vestergaard's precise nulls, Massenkoff–McCrory's insignificant aggregate effects, NY Fed (1 Sep 2026: firms "transform work, not cut jobs"; retraining dominant) and BICS (~6% of adopters cut headcount). *Strong answer:* the effects are on flows (hiring of entrants) not stocks; aggregate nulls and cohort effects are consistent; design tests that can distinguish AI from the 2022–24 tech hiring cycle and interest rates.
2. **Who gains — novices or experts?** Field/lab RCTs (Brynjolfsson–Li–Raymond, Noy–Zhang) show novice uplift and compression; Anthropic's Claude Code data show *persistent* returns to expertise in agentic work (15% vs 28–33% verified success) and March 2026 tenure effects. *Strong answer:* the answer depends on task frontier position and autonomy — chat-style assistance compresses, agentic delegation rewards judgment; propose testing this interaction.
3. **Productivity: 0.5% vs explosive.** Acemoglu ≲0.66% TFP/decade; Anthropic 1.8pp/yr upper bound (halved when success-adjusted); Trammell–Korinek / Davidson et al. explosive growth under full automation. *Strong answer:* the disagreement is about task coverage growth and σ, not arithmetic; state which parameter you would estimate.
4. **Measurement: usage data vs surveys vs payroll.** Usage logs (Anthropic, OpenAI) are high-frequency but selected; surveys (Bick et al., NY Fed, BTOS, BICS) are representative but coarse; payroll/admin (ADP, Danish registers, HMRC) are outcomes without mechanisms. *Strong answer:* triangulate; the June 2026 linked survey is the first bridge, and the Policy Framework asks for lab reporting requirements.
5. **Is exposure a good predictor?** Frey–Osborne's 47% never materialised; Arntz et al. cut it to 9%; Hampole et al. and Massenkoff–McCrory find exposure predicts lower demand growth only modestly. *Strong answer:* exposure is an intent-to-treat measure; usage-weighted (observed) exposure and firm adoption are the treatment.
6. **Fast diffusion or staircase?** Bick et al.: adoption faster than the PC; but firm adoption 35% in the UK, ~10% "extensive"; Anthropic's convergence horizon lengthened. *Strong answer:* individual adoption is fast, organisational adoption is slow (J-curve); the interesting variable is depth (tokens per worker, autonomy), not incidence.
7. **Policy: retraining evidence is weak — so what works?** Roodman–Massenkoff: +2–3pp employment for $13k; sector programs several times better but fragile. Alternatives in the Framework: wage insurance (positive US evidence, weaker Canada/Germany), UI trigger reform, capital accounts, worker equity. *Strong answer:* fund demonstrations with rigorous evaluation now; the $200m Research Fund and UK AI Economics Institute are the vehicles; propose an RCT-able design.
8. **Geographic and international inequality of adoption.** GDP-per-capita elasticity 0.7; India 0.27x; no country convergence; US states converging. *Strong answer:* diffusion inequality may matter more than displacement for global welfare; ask what predicts adoption conditional on income (language, sector mix, education).
9. **Can lab data be trusted?** Single-firm, selected users, Claude-classified labels, no ground-truth outcomes; but only labs see tasks. *Strong answer:* validate against external benchmarks (education correlation r>0.92, BLS projections), build in external replication, and treat Anthropic numbers as one instrument among several — this is exactly the intellectual honesty the mentors display.
10. **The pipeline problem and skill formation.** If AI absorbs entry-level tasks, does expertise still form? Evidence: junior hiring down; June 2026 survey finds 68% learn more with AI and no atrophy among heavy delegators; Stanford AI Index warns of "long-term learning penalties." *Strong answer:* distinguish learning-by-doing from learning-with-AI; propose measuring skill trajectories of cohorts entering post-2023.

## 7. Empirical project ideas (4 months, public data)

1. **UK observed exposure index.** Data: Anthropic Economic Index task-level usage (HF) × O*NET → SOC 2020 crosswalk; ONS ASHE and APS/LFS (Nomis) employment, pay and hours by SOC × region × age, 2019–2026. Method: replicate Massenkoff–McCrory's observed-exposure construction for the UK; diff-in-diff / event study of employment and real pay growth by exposure quartile since Nov 2022; contrast with theoretical (Eloundou/Felten) exposure. TAI question: "How will AI change jobs and employment in different parts of the economy?" and whether observed beats theoretical exposure outside the US.
2. **UK entry-level canaries.** Data: LFS/APS microdata (UK Data Service, SN 5-digit SOC) or Nomis age × occupation tables; Indeed UK postings (Hiring Lab publishes series); HESA graduate outcomes. Method: replicate Canaries for 22–25 vs 30+ workers in high- vs low-exposure occupations; separate hiring from separations using LFS flows; control for the graduate-hiring cycle and youth unemployment (16.4% in Jul 2026). TAI question: the professional pipeline problem; Indeed's "Slow Summer Hiring Another Blow for Young Workers" gives priors.
3. **Firm adoption depth and headcount in the UK.** Data: ONS BICS microdata (Secure Research Service) or published wave tables 2023–2026 by size/industry; ONS management-practices article (Mar 2025). Method: relate adoption depth (number of AI technologies, "extensive use") to reported workforce changes, vacancy postings and productivity; compare with US BTOS. TAI question: "What causes AI adoption at the firm level, and what are the consequences?"
4. **Economic Index geography for the UK.** Data: HF geography file (country level; sub-national UK if released — the Nov 2025 note promised "Europe-specific" regional metrics); ONS regional GVA, sector mix, degree share, broadband. Method: cross-sectional regressions and Shapley decomposition of AUI on income vs sector mix vs education; compare UK regions to US states' convergence. TAI question: who adopts AI and why access concentrates.
5. **Does exposure predict forecasts? A UK Occupational Outlook test.** Data: DfE/Working Futures occupational projections, BLS 2024–34 projections, Eloundou and observed exposure. Method: extend Massenkoff's "how predictable is job destruction" logic: regress projected employment growth on exposure and test whether past projections were revised in the direction exposure predicts. TAI question: early warning of labour-market change.
6. **Retraining evidence for the UK.** Data: DWP/DfE evaluations (Skills Bootcamps, Restart, sector-based work academies, Kickstart), UK Data Service, existing RCTs; Card–Kluve–Weber meta-analysis coding. Method: systematic review and meta-regression mirroring Roodman–Massenkoff, isolating employer-connected/sectoral programmes; cost-per-job comparisons. TAI question: "what pre- or re-distributive mechanisms could effectively spread the gains" — and directly serves the Research Fund's transition priority.
7. **Novice vs expert uplift across task autonomy.** Data: Anthropic Claude Code and chat primitives (autonomy, success, expertise proxies) by occupation; GDPval task list. Method: test whether the expert premium rises with autonomy level and task complexity, then compare with the published RCT effect sizes (Brynjolfsson–Li–Raymond, Noy–Zhang, Copilot). TAI question: deskilling/upskilling and who captures gains.
8. **Productivity nowcast from usage.** Data: Economic Index tokens per conversation and time-saved estimates by O*NET task × ONS/BLS hours by occupation; ONS quarterly productivity by industry. Method: build a bottom-up "potential vs realised" productivity index and test whether industries with faster usage growth show measurable output-per-hour gains (J-curve test). TAI question: "What impact will AI have on... productivity growth across the economy?"
9. **Wage insurance / capital accounts fiscal simulation.** Data: ASHE earnings distribution, HMRC PAYE, LFS displacement rates, Hyman et al. wage-insurance parameters. Method: microsimulate the Policy Framework's Tier 1–2 instruments for the UK under three unemployment scenarios (5/10/15%); estimate cost, coverage and fiscal offsets. TAI question: pre-/re-distributive mechanisms; gives a UK analogue to Anthropic's US-only framework.
10. **Survey-usage validation.** Data: Economic Index June 2026 survey aggregates vs Bick–Blandin–Deming style representative survey (replicable online via Prolific for ~£2–3k) in the UK. Method: compare stated adoption/expectations across selected (Claude) vs representative samples; estimate selection bias. TAI question: can lab data be trusted; the Economic Index Survey's external validity.
